Alaa has posted three write-ups from her labs, and each reached about ten readers. A classmate invites her to a chat group where members like each other's posts in the first hour. Should she join?

## A post spreads when it solves something

<MermaidDiagram title="Two posts about the same week" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Post A: I finished week 6"] --> B["Nothing to use or check"]
  B --> C["No reason to send it on"]
  D["Post B: a bug, the fix, the commit link"] --> E["A reader can use and check it"]
  E --> F["A reason to keep it or send it on"]
`} legend="The same week of work gives two very different posts." />

In a 2012 study of almost 7,000 newspaper articles, useful and surprising ones were shared more, on average. A reader who can use something has a reason to send it on. For Alaa, that means one real bug, the fix and a link to the commit.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the study says, and what it does not">

Berger and Milkman studied almost 7,000 articles from one newspaper over three months, and they also ran experiments. Positive articles were shared more than negative ones. Articles that caused strong feelings, such as awe, were shared more. Articles that were practically useful, interesting or surprising were also linked to more sharing. These links held after the researchers took into account where the newspaper placed each article.

The limits matter. The data came from one newspaper's most-emailed list, in about 2008 and 2009, with mostly US readers. An article that people email is not the same as a post that people share on LinkedIn or DEV. The study finds an average link, not a recipe for one post.

The study also found that strong feelings such as anger and anxiety were linked to sharing. That is not a goal for you. A post that makes people angry is not useful, and it costs the trust you are building. Aim for the useful part.

For a fellow, a useful post gives a reader something to use and something to check. Good examples are a bug and its fix, a small tool someone really uses, a checklist you tested, or a mistake and what you changed. A post that only says "I finished week 6" gives a reader nothing to use.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort the useful from the bait

<InteractiveCanvas slug="useful-or-bait" />

A post can be useful and real. It can get fake activity, which means likes, <GlossaryTerm term="stars" definition="Buttons on GitHub that show interest in a repository. Their number appears on its page." /> or comments that are bought, traded or automated. Or it can carry an invented claim. Sort each card. A wrong bucket tells you why in one line.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what three platforms say">

LinkedIn's community policies tell members not to do things that artificially increase engagement with their content. They also tell members not to agree in advance to like or re-share each other's content. LinkedIn's User Agreement, effective 3 November 2025, bans bots and other unauthorized automated methods, and fake identities.

A group of people who agree to like and share each other's posts is an <GlossaryTerm term="engagement pod" definition="A group of people who agree to like, comment on or share each other's posts so that the posts look popular." />. In November 2025, Social Media Today reported that a LinkedIn executive said the aim was to make engagement pods ineffective. We have not read the original post, so treat it as a report. We found no official LinkedIn statement on how its ranking treats engagement bait, so this course does not claim one. The public rules above are enough to act on.

YouTube's fake engagement policy bans services that inflate views, likes or subscribers, and swaps such as "subscribe for subscribe". Repeated or severe breaks can bring strikes or the removal of a channel, and paying someone else to do it does not move the responsibility. GitHub's acceptable use rules list fake accounts and automated inauthentic activity, and they name rank abuse, such as automated starring or following.

Alaa does not join the group. Instead, she asks two classmates to read her next post and tell her one line that was unclear. That is different from an engagement pod. The classmates agree to read and answer honestly, and nothing is agreed in advance about liking.

</GoDeeper>

## A test of luck with unknown songs

<MermaidDiagram title="Same songs, two kinds of music site" chart={`flowchart TD

In 2006, researchers built music websites with songs nobody knew. In total, 14,341 people took part and downloaded songs they liked. Some copies of the site hid what others had downloaded. Other copies showed the counts.

## Seeing the counts made winners unpredictable

<MermaidDiagram title="What the song market means for a post" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Counts shown to people"] --> B["A few songs take most downloads"]
  A --> C["Results differ by copy"]
  B --> D["A small count does not show a weak post"]
  C --> D
  C --> E["A big count does not show a strong one"]
  D --> F["You control: true, useful, regular"]
  E --> F
`} legend="Two conclusions from the experiment, and the part you can control." />

When people could see the counts, a few songs took most of the downloads, and success was harder to predict from copy to copy. Quality still mattered: the best songs rarely did badly and the worst rarely did well. Between those ends, almost any result was possible. Your post can be good and still be missed.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to take from the song market">

The experiment by Salganik, Dodds and Watts was published in Science in 2006. It used an artificial music market, not LinkedIn, so it cannot predict what will happen to one of your posts. What it shows is a pattern: when people follow what others chose, success becomes more unequal and harder to predict.

For you, this has three practical meanings. First, do not read a small count as proof that a post is bad. Alaa's ten readers say little about the quality of her write-up. Second, do not read a big count as proof of quality either, in your own posts or in other people's. Third, control what you can control: whether the post is true, whether a reader can use it, and whether you keep posting every week.

This is also why bought activity is a poor trade. It tries to remove the luck by paying for it. It cannot, and it replaces a small honest count with a large one that means nothing.

</GoDeeper>

## Fake stars help briefly, then count against you

<MermaidDiagram title="Bought stars and earned stars" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Bought stars"] --> B["A short boost"]
  B --> C["No users or issues follow"]
  C --> D["Readers doubt it: a liability"]
  E["A classmate uses it"] --> F["An issue or a comment"]
  F --> G["A small count you can explain"]
`} legend="The two paths start from the same button and end in different places." />

A 2025 study of GitHub stars found about six million suspected fake stars. Most promoted repositories that spread phishing or malware and did not last long. Fake stars helped for under about two months, then became a liability. GitHub's rules call automated starring rank abuse.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how the study works, and what it leaves out">

The study, by He and colleagues, looked at metadata from GitHub and marked stars as suspected fake when the patterns looked inauthentic. So the figure is a count of suspected stars, not proven ones. Fake-star activity rose sharply in 2024. The authors report that most of the suspected activity promoted short-lived phishing or malware repositories. They also report that fake stars brought a short benefit, under about two months, before becoming a liability.

Star counts are easy to see, which is why people buy them. But a reader who opens a repository with hundreds of stars and no issues, forks or users may start to wonder. A repository with 6 stars from real classmates, and a README that says who uses it, is easier to trust.

Honest signals are small and checkable. A classmate opens an issue. A friend uses your timer in her study group. A reader sends a pull request. You can show and explain each one. You cannot show or explain a number that you bought.

</GoDeeper>

## Write for one reader with one problem

<MermaidDiagram title="A question before you post" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Draft a post"] --> B{"Can one reader use it?"}
  B -->|"no"| C["Add the fix, the command or the number"]
  B -->|"yes"| D{"Can they check it?"}
  D -->|"no"| E["Link the commit or the file"]
  D -->|"yes"| F["Post it, then stop watching the count"]
`} legend="Two questions, in order. Neither needs a big audience." />

Alaa's three posts each reached about ten readers. That is a small audience, not a failed one. Before you post, ask: can one reader use this, and can they check it? Course 16, Your Career Brand, covers LinkedIn and blog mechanics.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a small audience, used well">

Ten readers are enough to learn from. After each post, Alaa asks two of them one question: which line was unclear? She writes the answers in her notes and fixes the post. She also keeps a list of the questions readers ask. Each question is a possible next post, and it comes from a real reader.

Measure the right thing. A like is cheap. A message that says "I used your fix" or a question about line 12 of your code is worth much more. You can count those by hand, and nobody can buy them.

This course does not cover how to set up a LinkedIn profile, how a resume should look, or how to host a blog. Course 16, Your Career Brand, teaches that. This chapter covers only what the content should be, and what you should never do to make it look bigger.

</GoDeeper>
