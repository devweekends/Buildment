## Test claims about AI detectors

<InteractiveCanvas slug="detector-myths" />

Pick a verdict for each claim, then flip the card. Notice which claims the evidence supports and which it does not. A wrong guess is welcome: it shows where your feeling about a score is stronger than the data.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the studies, and their limits">

OpenAI released a classifier for AI-written text in January 2023 and withdrew it on 20 July 2023, because of its low accuracy. At launch it labelled only 26% of AI-written text correctly, and it wrongly labelled human text as AI 9% of the time. OpenAI said it was unreliable on texts under 1,000 characters and advised it only for English.

A wrong flag like that is a <GlossaryTerm term="false positive" definition="A result that says something is present when it is not, such as a detector calling a person's own writing AI-made." />. Liang and colleagues tested seven detectors on 91 essays that non-native English speakers wrote for an English exam. On average, the detectors called 61.22% of those real essays AI-made, and at least one detector flagged 97.8% of them. Essays by US eighth graders were judged almost perfectly. This was a small pilot study, and most of the detectors were built on an older model. Even so, the lesson is plain: a real second-language writer can be falsely flagged.

This course does not teach how to lower a detector score. A score that you aim at tells a reader nothing about who wrote the post. Evidence of authorship does.

Platform rules draw a line between help and generated text. Medium says that grammar or outline help does not need to be disclosed. It says that text generated by AI must be, and that undisclosed AI-generated writing gets Network Only distribution. It also says AI can help authors express themselves in a second language. DEV lets authors choose Hand Written, AI-Assisted or Fully Autonomous, and it warns that undisclosed autonomy may lead to suspension. This course still asks for an AI-use line every time (chapter 10.3), even where a platform does not require one.

</GoDeeper>

## Signs are not proof: edit your own draft

<MermaidDiagram title="A test for every line" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Read one line of your draft"] --> B{"Could only you have written it?"}
  B -->|"yes"| C["Keep it"]
  B -->|"no"| D["Ask: what really happened?"]
  D --> E["Add your fact, number or mistake"]
  E --> B
`} legend="A different word does not pass the test. A fact from your notes does." />

Wikipedia's editors list signs of AI writing, such as inflated importance, vague sources and invented citations. They say these are signs, not proof. Use the list on your own draft. Zaid changed "Joins play a crucial role in modern data work" to "My join returned 12 rows. I expected 30. Two customers had no orders."

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a self-editing list">

The list is an advice page that Wikipedia's editors keep for their own work. It is not a rule for your posts, and it says that detectors have non-trivial error rates. We use it for one purpose: to find lines in your own draft that stayed generic. The table shows the signs, and what to do about each.

| Sign in a draft | What to do |
|---|---|
| Inflated significance ("plays a crucial role") | Say what happened in your lab, with a number |
| Promotional tone | Describe what the code does and where it fails |
| Vague attribution ("experts say") | Name the page you read, or remove the claim |
| Overused "AI vocabulary" | Use the words you would use to a classmate |
| "Not just X, but Y" patterns | State the one thing you mean |
| Forced groups of three | List the real number of things, even if it is two or four |
| Title case and heavy bold | Use plain headings and bold only where you must |
| Chatbot phrases left in the text | Delete lines such as "Certainly! Here is your post" |
| Broken or invented citations | Open every source and check it exists |

Two of these are real faults, not matters of style. A chatbot phrase left in a post shows that nobody read the post before it went out. An invented citation is a false claim with your name on it, the same problem as the fake book in the reading list of chapter 7.3. Fix those two every time.

Notice what the other rows have in common. Each fix adds something only you have: a number from your lab, a page you really read, a plain word you really use. Swapping one fancy word for another does not fix any of them. It leaves the line as empty as before.

</GoDeeper>

## When someone says it looks AI-made

<MermaidDiagram title="Two ways to answer the doubt" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["A classmate says: AI-made"] --> B{"How do you reply?"}
  B -->|"argue the score"| C["The doubt stays and nothing can be checked"]
  B -->|"show files, ask which line"| D["The reader can follow the trail"]
  D --> E["Fix the generic line with a fact"]
  E --> F["Keep the AI-use line true"]
`} legend="Only the second reply gives the reader something to check." />

Zaid thanks the classmate and shows the files. Then he asks which line looked generic. It was the last paragraph. He rewrites it with a real number and leaves his AI-use line as it was. He does not hide that AI helped, and he does not argue about the score.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when the person has power over you">

Sometimes the doubt comes from a teacher, a client or an employer. The same steps work, with one addition: ask what rule applies. Some places have fixed rules about AI that no evidence of yours can change (chapter 10.3). Ask the question early and calmly, in writing, so both of you know the rule.

Offer a short walk-through as well. Ask the person to pick any sentence of your post, and explain where it came from: which note, which lab, which commit. A person who wrote a post can do this in a minute. A person who only pasted one cannot, and that is the difference you are showing.

Do not promise yourself that evidence always works. It supports your word and does not prove it. A reader can still doubt you, and that is a fair thing for a reader to do when AI posts are common. Your goal is not to win the argument. It is to leave a trail that any fair reader can follow, so that the doubt can end.

</GoDeeper>

## Save the trail as you write

<MermaidDiagram title="A folder that shows how a post grew" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["posts/week-07/"] --> N["notes.md, dated"]
  R --> D1["draft-1.md, your words"]
  R --> Q["ai-questions.md, with your answers"]
  R --> D2["draft-2.md"]
  R --> F["final.md with the AI-use line"]
`} legend="One folder per post. Commit each file when you write it." />

Zaid commits each file the day he writes it, with a message that says what it is. He keeps the AI's questions and his answers, not the whole chat. Before he saves anything, he applies the privacy check from chapter 2.2. These files help a fair reader. They are evidence, not proof.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a short routine">

The routine costs about ten minutes a post. After your notes, save them in the folder. After your first draft, save it as it is, before you ask an AI for anything. After the AI's questions, save them and your answers. After the last edit, save the final post and the AI-use line. Commit after each step, in the way chapter 10.6 describes.

If you write on a phone, paste the text into the repository on the same day, using the GitHub site in your browser. Draft files that you copy in weeks later, all on one day, show less than files saved as you went.

Keep out of the folder anything you would not publish: passwords, keys, client names, other people's messages. The AI chat can hold such things without you noticing, so copy only the lines you need. If a draft quotes a person, ask first, as chapter 10.3 describes.

One more limit. Dates on commits can be set by the person who commits, as chapter 10.6 explains. So the trail does not prove when you wrote something. It shows a way of working that a fair reader can follow. That is why you also keep a real story and a checked fact in every post.

</GoDeeper>
