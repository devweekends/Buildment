```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Samir, a bootcamp student, posts write-ups of his labs on LinkedIn. Four facts: 1) His last post explains a real bug and links the commit, and 11 people read it. 2) A group chat of eight fellows proposes that everyone likes each other's posts within an hour. 3) LinkedIn's community policy tells members not to agree in advance to like or re-share each other's content. 4) A tool offers to comment on 50 posts a day in his name, and LinkedIn's User Agreement bans automated methods. What should he do next?"
options:
  - "Join the like agreement, since the likes would come from real people"
  - "Use the comment tool, since the comments would carry his own name"
  - "Wait for 100 readers before he posts again, since 11 is too few"
  - "Write up the next real bug fix, and ask two readers for honest feedback"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Fact 3 names agreeing in advance to like each other's posts. Real people can still give likes that were arranged, and those likes tell a reader nothing about the post."
  - "Fact 4 says the tool is automated, and the User Agreement bans automated methods. A comment under his name that he did not write is not his."
  - "Fact 1 says 11 real people read a useful post. Reach is partly luck, so waiting for a number only stops him from sharing true work."
  - "Right. A real bug and a linked commit are useful and checkable (fact 1), and two readers who answer honestly are not an agreement to like. Neither rule in facts 3 and 4 is broken."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Samir asks a chat assistant how to save his week of work on an order page. It answers: 'Work all week, then make one commit on Sunday called Complete order page. One commit is cleaner to read than many small ones, and squash merging proves that one commit is the standard.' What is the mistake in this answer?"
options:
  - "One big commit hides the steps, and squash merge is a team setting"
  - "It should say to commit twice a week, because once a week is too few"
  - "It should call the commit by its date, such as 2026-10-25, instead"
  - "It is right, but he should add a long body to the single commit"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. Commit when one small thing works, so a reviewer can follow each step. Some projects squash a pull request into one commit, but that is their setting, and the pull request page still carries the story."
  - "A count of commits per week misses the rule. The unit is one small thing that works, so a busy day may hold several commits and a quiet one none."
  - "A date does not say what changed or why. The first line of a message should tell a reader what the commit does."
  - "A body helps, but a single commit still hides the order of the work, any bug found on the way and how it was fixed."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Alaa's mentor asks, 'How do I know this post is yours?' Four facts about Alaa's pack: 1) notes.md, dated Monday, describes the bug in her own words. 2) draft-1.md, dated Tuesday, and draft-2.md, dated Thursday, differ in many sentences. 3) A free detector rated the final post 12% AI. 4) A chat assistant asked her three questions and fixed her grammar, and her AI-use line says so. Which should she show first?"
options:
  - "The detector score, because 12% shows that the post is mostly human"
  - "The AI-use line alone, because it already says what the assistant did"
  - "The dated notes and drafts, because a mentor can open and read them"
  - "The final post alone, because a good post shows who its author is"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "Fact 3 gives a guess. Detectors have flagged real writers and have passed AI text, so a low score does not prove authorship either."
  - "Fact 4 is true, but the line is her statement. It says what the assistant did and does not show where her words came from."
  - "Right. Facts 1 and 2 are files with dates that a mentor can open and compare, and they show the post growing from her own notes."
  - "A good post is not evidence of who wrote it. Facts 1 and 2 give her real evidence, and leaving it out wastes it."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "A reviewer has five minutes with two repositories for order pages. 1) Samir: one commit named 'final', 520 lines changed, made on Sunday night. 2) Wafa: seven commits over six days, each under 90 lines, with messages such as 'Fix: stop an order being marked ready twice'. 3) Both pages work when the reviewer runs them. 4) The reviewer wants to know how each fellow handles a bug. Which conclusion do the facts support?"
options:
  - "Samir's is better, because one commit means he finished faster"
  - "Wafa's shows how she fixed a bug; Samir's hides it"
  - "The two are equal, because both pages work when run"
  - "Wafa's is worse, because seven commits are too many to read"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "Fact 1 gives no timing at all. A Sunday-night commit does not show that the work was quick, only that it was saved at once."
  - "Right. Fact 2 shows a message that names a bug and its fix, and fact 4 is about bugs. A single commit named 'final' shows no step."
  - "Fact 4 asks about bug handling, not about whether the page works. Both pages working (fact 3) cannot tell the reviewer that."
  - "Fact 2 says each of her commits is under 90 lines, so each is quick to read. A reviewer can stop after the one about the bug."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "A mentor compares two profiles. 1) Alaa: 14 green days over six weeks; each message says what changed and why; five commits add tests. 2) Samir: 60 green days in a row; every commit is named 'update' or 'changes' and changes one to three lines. 3) GitHub places a commit on the date it says it was written, and Git lets the author set that date. 4) The mentor wants evidence of honest work and learning. Which profile gives stronger evidence, and why?"
options:
  - "Samir's, because 60 days in a row show more effort than 14 days"
  - "Samir's, because daily commits show a habit whatever the messages say"
  - "Neither, because dates can be set, so the commits prove nothing"
  - "Alaa's, because messages and tests are evidence that a graph is not"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Fact 3 says the dates a graph shows can be set, so a long run of days is weak proof of effort."
  - "Fact 2 says each commit changes one to three lines under a name that says nothing. A habit of tiny, unnamed commits shows little about the work."
  - "Fact 3 makes the graph weak proof, not the commits themselves. Fact 1 shows messages and tests that a mentor can read and check."
  - "Right. Facts 1 and 4 point to readable steps and tests, which a mentor can check, while fact 3 shows why the graph alone is weak."
```
