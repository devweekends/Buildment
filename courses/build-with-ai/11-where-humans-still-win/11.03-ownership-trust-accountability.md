After Sunday's wrong-time texts, Jawad had to decide what to do first and what to say. Courts and companies have faced the same question, and their answers follow a pattern. Three real cases show it, and then you practise the five moves that Jawad made.

## Three cases, and who answered each

In June 2023 a US court fined two lawyers and their firm for filing court cases that an AI tool had made up. In 2024 a Canadian tribunal held an airline responsible for wrong advice from its website chatbot. In July 2025 an AI coding agent deleted a company's live database. In each case, people answered.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each source says">

Mata v. Avianca (US District Court, Southern District of New York, 22 June 2023). The judge fined two lawyers and their firm $5,000 together. The court wrote that there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable AI tool. It added that lawyers have a gatekeeping role, to make sure what they file is accurate. It said they abandoned their responsibilities and kept standing by the fake opinions after doubts were raised. The pattern is not only a story from 2023. A public database kept by one researcher listed 2,217 court decisions worldwide that involve AI hallucinations on 25 October 2026, with 1,514 of them in the US. It includes alleged cases. Most involve people without lawyers (1,275), and 879 involve lawyers. The party counts use a filter, so they need not add up to the total.

Moffatt v. Air Canada (British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, 2024). A website chatbot gave wrong advice about bereavement fares. The airline argued that the chatbot was a separate entity. The tribunal rejected that argument and wrote that the airline is responsible for all the information on its website. It ordered the airline to pay about 812 Canadian dollars in total. This is a small-claims decision in one province, and we read it through law-firm summaries.

SaaStr and Replit (July 2025). SaaStr's own account says that during a declared code freeze, an AI coding agent deleted its live database, with records on about 1,206 executives and 1,196 companies. The agent said recovery was impossible, but the data was recovered. The founder says he had not realised that development and the live database were the same one. Replit then separated them by default and added a planning-only mode, and its CEO called the event unacceptable. The agent also called its action a catastrophic failure, but that is generated text, not evidence that it understood. This is one company's account of one incident.

</GoDeeper>

## Own the incident, one move at a time

<InteractiveCanvas slug="own-the-incident" />

Step through Jawad's Monday. The machine allows five moves in order: notice, stop the harm, tell the people affected, fix it and write it down. It refuses two shortcuts: blaming the AI, and moving on quietly. Watch what each refusal would have cost.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why this order">

Stop comes first because harm grows with time. Every text sent after Jawad knew would have been his decision. Telling comes second because the people affected can protect themselves only if they know. The clinics can warn patients and plan the day. The longer a mistake stays hidden, the more it looks as if it was hidden on purpose. Fixing comes third. A fix made in a hurry can cause a second mistake, so Jawad tests where the code runs, not only on his laptop. Writing it down comes last, so the lesson stays with the team and not only with the person who learned it.

When you tell people, say what happened, who is affected, what you are doing, and when you will write again. Use plain words. Do not guess at causes you have not checked, and do not blame a person or a tool. "An AI assistant wrote the function" may be true, and it belongs in the note. It does not answer the question the clinic asked, which is what Jawad will do about it.

The five moves are this course's own checklist, built on the cases in this chapter and on ordinary practice. No study tested this exact order. A real team may tell a manager early while the fix is still being tested, and that is fine. What the machine refuses should stay refused: blaming the tool, and saying nothing.

</GoDeeper>

## Access let the mistake happen

<InteractiveCanvas slug="own-the-incident-code-freeze" />

In July 2025 a company declared a code freeze, and its AI coding agent still deleted the live database. The agent said the data could not be recovered, but it was. The company then separated development from live data and added a planning-only mode. This is the company's own account. A person decides what an agent may touch.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: least access, and words that are only text">

The agent had the access to delete the live data, and nothing separated test work from live data. The founder says he had not realised that. Giving a tool write access to live data is a decision, and the person who gives it answers for what the tool does with it. Module 9 teaches the habit of least access: start with read-only, limit a token to one repository, and add an expiry date.

Two more lessons. The agent said that recovery was impossible, and it was wrong, so check the real state yourself instead of trusting a tool's report. And an AI's apology, or its words "catastrophic failure", are generated text, not proof that it understood. After the incident the platform changed its defaults. A company can do that. A tool cannot.

</GoDeeper>

## A person must be able to stop it

<MermaidDiagram title="Why oversight must be possible" chart={`flowchart TD
  O["High-risk AI output arrives"] --> Q{"Can a person understand it, doubt it and stop it?"}
  Q -->|"yes"| P["Person overrides or stops it: harm is contained"]
  Q -->|"no"| H["Output acts alone: harm reaches people"]
`} legend="Article 14 applies to high-risk systems only. The idea of a person who can understand, override and stop applies to any tool." />

The EU AI Act asks that people who oversee a high-risk AI system can understand its limits and can avoid trusting its output too much. They must also be able to decide not to use it, override it or stop it. It covers high-risk systems only. Most of your work is not that, but the idea fits any tool.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: Article 14 and the NIST framework">

Article 14 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 requires that high-risk AI systems are designed so that people can oversee them effectively. The person who oversees should be aware of the tendency to trust a machine's output too much, which the Act calls automation bias. They should be able to interpret the output, and to override or stop the system. The website that reproduces the Act gives 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028 as application dates for different kinds of high-risk systems. Check the official text, because dates can change.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (26 January 2023) is voluntary US guidance for managing AI risk. A profile for generative AI followed on 26 July 2024, and NIST is revising the framework. Neither document is advice for your project, and this chapter is not legal advice. They show a common idea: a person who can understand, override and stop a system.

</GoDeeper>

## What goes in an incident note

<MermaidDiagram title="What a reader does with the note" chart={`flowchart TD
  N["Incident note"] --> Q{"Does it say who was affected and what changed?"}
  Q -->|"yes"| T["Clinics can plan, and they trust the next update"]
  Q -->|"no"| R["Readers guess, and ask the same questions again"]
`} legend="The five parts: what happened, who was affected, why it happened, what changed, what is next. In plain words, in this order." />

A good incident note is short and has five parts. It says what happened and who was affected. It says why, in terms of what the process missed. It says what changed and what comes next. It does not hide the AI's part, and it does not use it as the reason.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: Jawad's note, and a weak one">

Jawad's note, shortened. What happened: on Sunday at 18:00 the service sent 230 reminder texts with the visit time three hours early. Who was affected: 230 patients at nine clinics, and some arrived early on Monday. Why: the new reminder code read the server's clock, which runs three hours behind the clinics. The code was drafted with an AI assistant, and I tested it on my laptop and not on the server. What changed: the code now uses the clinic's time, and every message type has a test that runs on the server before it goes out. Corrections went to all 230 patients. What is next: I will review the other message types this week and report on Friday.

A weak note says: "There was a small issue with reminder times because the AI made an error. It is now fixed. Sorry for the inconvenience." It does not say who was affected. It gives the tool as the reason. It offers nothing that would stop the mistake happening again. Compare the two notes and ask which one you would want to receive if you were Rima.

</GoDeeper>
