Manar is a freelance web developer with one café as a client (a fictional case). One afternoon she has eight calls to make, and an AI assistant is open on her screen. For which calls should she use it, and which should she keep?

## Three questions sort every call

<MermaidDiagram title="Three questions for every call" chart={`flowchart TD

  C -->|"yes"| D["AI drafts, you decide"]

Ask the first question, then the second. If a person's word, consent or a rule is needed, the call stays with people. If you can check the result and undo a mistake cheaply, the AI drafts and you decide. Otherwise you lead and the AI helps. These questions are the course's own, not a research result.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: judgment, context and taste">

You met three words in chapter 11.1 and in chapter 1.4. Context is the set of facts around a task that the assistant was not given: the café's phones, the budget, the deadline. Judgment is what you do with those facts: you weigh them for this case and decide. Taste is how you choose between drafts that all work, by asking which one fits these users and your own standard. The three questions use all three. The first is about people. The second is about whether you can check. The third is about context and taste.

Why does a person's word come first? Some calls only work when a person makes them. A client who hears bad news from a person can ask a question and get an answer. An applicant who loses a job wants a person to have decided. A rule at work may say that a person must approve. In none of these cases is the problem that the AI lacks facts. The call itself needs a person.

Why is checking second? The cost of a mistake depends on how likely it is, how large it is, and who sees it first. A mistake you find on a copy before anyone else sees it is cheap, even in a long query. A mistake that reaches customers is not. These questions are tests, not laws. A company rule or a client's contract can move a call from one bucket to another, and then you follow the rule.

</GoDeeper>

## A long query can still be a draft

<InteractiveCanvas slug="who-leads-this-call-query" />

Manar needs a 40-line query for the café's monthly sales report. She runs it on a copy of the data and compares the totals with a hand count of three shops. They match. A wrong query would show itself before anyone else saw it, so the AI drafts and she decides.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when code is not checkable">

A query is checkable because the totals can be counted another way. Code that handles security is harder to check by running it. A security company's 2025 report tested more than 100 AI models on its own tasks. It found that 45% of the code samples failed its security tests, and the result did not improve with model size. This is vendor research on 2025 models. The report does not describe its tasks in full, so treat the number as a warning and not as a measurement of your tool.

The habit is to ask, for each piece of AI code, whether you have a way to check it that does not depend on trusting it. A second count of the totals is such a way. If you have no way, you lead and you find someone who can check. Chapter 11.4 comes back to this.

</GoDeeper>

## Context decides which draft fits

<InteractiveCanvas slug="who-leads-this-call-layouts" />

The assistant drafts three menu layouts for the café. All three work, and it prefers B, which has the biggest photos. Manar knows that the café's customers use old phones with little data, so she picks A, the lightest. Facts only she has, and her taste, decide. The assistant still saved her an hour of drafting.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: giving the assistant your facts">

Manar could now tell the assistant about the phones and the data limit, and ask it to redraw layout B lighter. That is a good use of the tool, and she still picks. The point is not that the assistant was wrong. It was never told what makes this café's customers different. Module 6 shows how the same question gets a different right answer when the context changes.

Taste also grows with practice. Each time you choose a layout, ship it and see how people use it, you learn more about what fits. A draft cannot do that looking for you, because it did not see what happened after your earlier choices.

</GoDeeper>

## A late date is told by a person

<InteractiveCanvas slug="who-leads-this-call-late-date" />

The project will be two weeks late. Manar asks the assistant for a draft and uses it as notes. Then she phones the client herself. The client asks why, and she answers. The assistant is not on the call, and a message the client cannot question is not the same as a conversation.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what makes a call people only">

In December 2022, the Pew Research Center asked 11,004 US adults about AI in hiring. 71% opposed AI making final hiring decisions, and 66% said they would not want to apply to an employer that uses AI to help decide. People also thought AI would do worse than humans at recognising potential and at judging how well applicants would work with coworkers. This is opinion, collected before chatbots were widely used, and it does not show how well AI does these tasks. It does show why an applicant, a client or a patient may want a person to decide.

In NACE's 2026 data on US employers, the skills most often sought on a resume were working in a team (85%) and problem-solving (82%). These are the skills that people-only calls need. Chapter 11.3 adds a legal example: the EU AI Act asks that people can override and stop high-risk systems.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort Manar's afternoon

<InteractiveCanvas slug="who-leads-this-call" />

Tap the bucket each call belongs in, or drag the card onto it. A wrong bucket names the question the card fails. Use the questions in order: a person's word first, then check and undo, then your facts and taste.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cards people argue about">

Some readers put "putting the change on the live page" under AI drafts, because an AI can write the steps. The test is whether a mistake can be undone cheaply before customers see it. A change on the live page reaches customers at once, so Manar leads and runs it herself. The AI can draft the checklist and the steps for undoing the change.

Choosing between applicants is another card that tempts. Even if an AI could rank the two well, its ranking would decide in practice, and the applicants deserve a person's decision. The plan for her own project is a third case. She may ask the AI to check her dates or suggest areas, but the hours and the goals are hers.

</GoDeeper>

## What developers keep for themselves

<MermaidDiagram title="Share who do not plan to hand each task to AI" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Deployment and monitoring: 75.8%"] --> B["Project planning: 69.2%"]
  B --> C["Committing and reviewing code: 58.7%"]
  C --> D["Testing: 44.1%"]
  D --> E["Writing code: 28.9%"]
`} legend="Stack Overflow's 2025 survey of developers: the share who said they do not plan to hand each task to AI. Plans, not ability, from people who chose to answer." />

In a 2025 developer survey, most people said they do not plan to hand deployment or project planning to AI, and fewer said so about writing code. It measures plans, not ability. When they do not trust an AI answer, 75.3% would still ask a person.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading this survey">

Stack Overflow's survey is run by a company that hosts a developer community, and the people who answered chose to take part. The percentages are what developers say they plan, not what AI can or cannot do. In 2026 the same survey series found that only about 20% of developers use AI for deploying, operating or troubleshooting production. It also found that the first place developers go with a work question is their coworkers (72%). Those 2026 figures come from a blog summary of the results, so treat them as a pointer.

What the surveys give you is a habit worth copying. When you doubt an answer, ask a person who knows. Chapter 11.3 shows what happened when nobody did.

</GoDeeper>

```quiz
  - "Telling a parent that their child's class place was removed"
  - "Making ten ideas for the school newsletter's title"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "A reminder note is a draft that Sami reads and edits before it goes out. A mistake is cheap and he sees it first, so the AI can draft and he decides."
  - "Sami knows which teachers share the room and when. His facts decide it, and the AI can list options, so he leads. Nobody's word or consent is needed to choose a timetable."
  - "Right. A parent who loses a child's place will want to hear it from a person and to ask questions. The AI can help with notes, but the call is people only."
  - "Ten title ideas cost nothing to read and nothing to reject. The AI drafts and Sami decides."
```

```quiz
question: "Ehab uses an AI assistant for two scripts on the same day. 1) Script one renames 500 files in his study group's shared folder, and the folder has no backup. 2) Script two counts the words in his own notes, and he can compare the result with one note counted by hand.
```
