## Course checklist

- [ ] I read AI claims for what they are, name what AI does not know, make the calls myself, and never paste what a stranger should not read
- [ ] I choose tools by the job, keep my work in files I own, and have a fallback when a tool changes
- [ ] I learn a topic from five angles, test myself from memory, and run short daily labs with one part broken on purpose
- [ ] I slow down to understand: I research before I ask, compare two assistants, check the primary page, and give context because it changes the right answer
- [ ] I plan in months with a large learning share, check what AI suggests, and keep only what I can explain
- [ ] I grow and share the human way: I adopt tools with a loop, use the least access, show my thinking, write in my voice, and never fake activity
- [ ] I know who leads each call, how to own a mistake, and when not to use AI
- [ ] I answered the questions in 11.2 to 11.4, finished the review in 11.5, and submitted my capstone, my defence and my answer below

## Your AI-as-enabler toolkit

The table holds one line for each habit in the course, in the order you met them. Each line is short on purpose. When a new task arrives, at work, in study or in your project, you will not have time to reread eleven modules. You will have time to read these lines.

| Habit | One line to remember |
|---|---|
| Read the claim | Prediction or measurement? Vendor or independent? What sample, which country, and what counts as success? |
| Evidence, with limits | Say what the data shows and where it stops; say "in US data" when it is US data; no study predicts one person's future |
| What AI does not know | Your context, your private data, local and recent facts, and what nobody wrote down |
| You make the call | AI drafts, explains and compares; you decide, check and own the result |
| Hints, not answers | Ask AI to make you think; answers it hands you can weaken your practice |
| Habits teams value | Give context, check, own the result, work well with others |
| Before you paste | Would I be fine if a stranger read this? If not, remove it or do not paste it |
| Jobs, not tools | Choose the kind of tool for the job; keep two examples and a fallback |
| Own your files | Notes, saved prompts and logs live in files you own, not in a chat history |
| Recall, not rereading | Close it, recall it, study only the gaps, and recall again days later |
| Five angles | Explain it, picture it, work an example, break it, then build or teach it |
| Listen, then read | Audio to meet a topic; the sources to learn it and to check the audio |
| Daily lab | Study, build, break one part on purpose, write: about 30 to 45 minutes |
| Check before you run | Commands against the official docs; packages on the official package site |
| Research before you ask | Read the primary page and write down your context first |
| Two assistants, then the page | Disagreement shows where to check; agreement is not proof; the primary page decides |
| Ask what was left out | What did you leave out? What would change your answer? What is the strongest case against it? |
| Context changes the answer | The common answer is a start; give workload, size, team, budget and licence |
| Plan in months | A large learning share and one new area at a time |
| Check it exists | Open every video and article an AI suggests; find a listing for every book |
| Explain-back | Keep no part you cannot explain without notes; understood beats big |
| Licences | No licence means read and learn, not copy; credit what you reuse |
| Adopt in days | Docs first, a tiny lab, explain it back, then use it |
| Show thinking | Public work is evidence others can check, not noise |
| Explain every contribution | Edit and explain every AI-assisted change you send to a team or a program |
| Least access | Read-only first, one repository, an expiry date; cancel a leaked secret first |
| Your voice, your consent | Record your own voice; clone only your own, with consent and a label |
| Test the critique | Treat each AI critique point as a claim: fix it, test it first, or reject it with a reason |
| Write it yourself | Draft from your log, let AI edit and ask, then check every fact |
| AI-use line | Which tool, what it did, and what you checked |
| Useful, not bait | Make content people find useful; never fake likes, stars or activity |
| Show authorship | Drafts, notes, the real story and the checked fact; never try to beat a detector |
| Real commits | Small steps whose messages say why |
| Who leads the call | A person's word: people only. Check and undo cheaply: AI drafts. Your facts or taste: you lead |
| Own the mistake | Notice, stop the harm, tell the people affected, fix it, write it down |
| When not to use AI | Costly and unchecked, private, or your own lesson: change the task, anonymise, or do it yourself |

Copy the table somewhere you will see it: the first page of your notes, a pinned note on your phone, or the README of your lab repository. Read it before you open an AI tool for a new kind of task. Much of the value of this course is in these lines being near you at the moment you need them.

You will not need every line every time. A new topic for your project needs "five angles", "recall, not rereading" and "check it exists". A new decision needs "research before you ask", "two assistants, then the page" and "context changes the answer". A client task needs "what AI does not know", "before you paste" and "you make the call". A Friday write-up needs "write it yourself", "show authorship" and "AI-use line". Part of the skill is choosing the two or three lines that fit the task in front of you. You practised that choice in the mixed review in 11.5.

### How to look back at gate 1.10

The first question matters most. In module 1, you checked one claim about AI and jobs. You also split a task into what AI can help with, what it does not know, and the calls that stay yours. You did it before you knew most of the habits in this course. Reading it again is a clear way to see how your thinking has changed.

Here is an example from a fictional fellow, Heba, whom you met in chapter 10.3. In gate 1.10 she used the provided case, the donation tracker for an NGO. Her answer then was short. AI could write the code, design the database and write the README. AI did not know the NGO's budget. The calls that stayed hers were the page colours and the app's name.

Reading it now, she sees three things. First, she handed AI the database design without any facts about the NGO, such as what kinds of donations it gets and in which currencies. She would now give it those facts first, and check its design against them. Second, she missed the biggest call of all: what data to keep about donors, and who may see it. That is a privacy decision, and it stays with the people involved, not with a tool. In the language of chapter 11.2, it is a call where a person's word is needed. Third, she gave AI the parts she most needed to learn. She would now ask AI to explain and quiz her on database design, and keep the design itself as her own work, explained line by line.

Heba also noticed something she did well. She already listed the NGO's budget as missing context, months before she knew the words for it. Write your own look-back the same way. Say what you would now keep for yourself, what you would give to AI with more context, and one thing you already did well. Four or five honest sentences are enough, and your mentor reads them as part of this gate. If you would rather not write about your own work, answer with the donation tracker case or any case from the course.

## Your next 30 days

The course ends here, but your project does not. Your answer below has two parts: the look-back you just wrote, and the next 30 days of your plan. The second part turns your plan into real work, so the habits keep running after the course is over. A plan for 30 days is short enough to be honest about. It is long enough to show a pattern: new areas, daily labs, recall tests and write-ups. It now also holds one researched decision, one demo and one published piece.

Take Firas, a fictional student, as an example of a strong answer. His project is a small app where students on his university databases course share practice questions. Over the next 30 days, the database is his new area in week 1, and week 2 builds it into the app. Week 4 starts the API, his next new area. In most weeks he will run four labs of about 30 minutes, and in week 2 he will stop the database on purpose and record what the app shows. Each Thursday he will do a recall test on the week's area, with his notes closed. Each Friday he will write a short write-up from his log, with an AI-use line. Week 3 falls in his exam period, so it is a review week with two labs instead of four, and no new area.

His 30 days also hold three pieces from the later modules. On day 10 he will research one decision: whether to keep the practice questions in a database or in plain files. He will ask two assistants, read the database's documentation page and write down its date, and ask what the answers left out. On day 24 he will record a 60-second demo on his phone, with a caption that says the questions are made up. On day 28 he will publish his week 4 write-up on one platform, built from his real commits, with the AI-use line.

Notice how his answer is built. Every week has a clear area or task. The labs have a number and a break-on-purpose step. The checks are named, with days. The decision, the demo and the piece each have a day. And he planned for a week that will go badly, instead of pretending every week will be the same.

If you prefer not to use your own project, use Ibrahim's case from the capstone. Take the improved plan you wrote for him, or use this one. He has about 6 hours a week. Month 1 is the API and the database, and month 2 is the queue logic and the waiting screen. Before anything else, his database password must be dealt with. Write his next 30 days, and include the decision about the queue number, the demo you described for him, and the post you wrote.
