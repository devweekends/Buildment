A year after this course began, a mentor looks at the work of two fellows, Laith and Mira. Each gave about the same number of hours.

## Forty repositories, one question

<InteractiveCanvas slug="grow-laith-forty-repos" />

Laith has forty repositories. The mentor has ten minutes. She opens one and asks why it is built that way. The README is one line and says nothing about choices. Forty finished tutorials are real work, but they give her nothing to check.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: Laith's year">

Laith is quick and he finishes what he starts. After the course he decided to learn something new every week. Each week he followed a tutorial from start to end, pushed the result to GitHub, and moved on. After a year he had forty repositories. Every one of them ran. Every one used a tool he had not used before. Every README was one line: the name of the tutorial.

Laith felt busy and sure that he was growing fast. He never wrote down why a design was chosen, what broke, or what he would change. The mentor asked why repository 17 keeps its data in a file and not in a database. He said, "The tutorial did it that way." That was true. It was also the whole problem. The mentor had ten minutes and one question, and the repository could not help her answer it.

Laith did learn tools, and nothing in this story says that forty repositories are bad. The point is smaller and harder: more of the same gives a reader no more to check. After a few months Laith could not remember the details of most of them either, because he never explained any of them back to himself.

</GoDeeper>

## Mira's twelve weeks

<MermaidDiagram title="Mira's twelve weeks" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Weeks 1 to 2: read the rules, run it. Leaves: a test log"] --> B["Weeks 3 to 4: one small change. Leaves: a review thread"]
  B --> C["Weeks 5 to 10: one real change. Leaves: three rounds of replies"]
  C --> D["Weeks 11 to 12: review a newcomer. Leaves: a write-up"]
`} legend="Mira's plan and her project are made up. Every step leaves something a reader can check." />

Mira joined a small open-source project and stayed twelve weeks. She read its rules, ran it, and made a small fix. Then she made one real change and answered three rounds of review. A reader can follow her choices, her mistakes and her replies.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: Mira's twelve weeks">

Mira finished the same course. She had a shorter list of ideas than Laith: one small open-source tool for school timetables, which a teacher at her university used. The project is made up. Its rules page asks newcomers to start with a small change and to say what they tested.

In week 1 she read the rules and ran the project's tests on her laptop. In week 2 she fixed a typo in the docs, which showed her how the project takes changes. In weeks 3 and 4 she picked a real problem from the project's list of issues: a date shown in the wrong order. Her first change went through two rounds of review. A reviewer asked why she had changed a function and not the page that shows the date. She explained, then saw that the reviewer was right and moved the fix. That cost her a week and taught her more than a tutorial would have.

In weeks 5 to 10 she wrote a bigger change: a week view for the timetable. She asked an AI assistant to draft a test. She read the test line by line and changed two lines. In the description of her change she wrote what the assistant drafted, what she changed and what she ran. In week 11 she read a newcomer's change and left two kind comments. In week 12 she wrote a short post about what broke and what she learned.

She gave about 8 hours a week. Over 12 weeks, that is 96 hours. Twelve weeks is also close to the length of the team programs you will read about in chapter 8.4. This story does not promise Mira a job, and it does not say that team projects are better than solo work. It says that her twelve weeks left choices, replies and fixes that a stranger can read.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: key words">

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Adopt** | To decide that you will use a tool for real work, not only try it once |
| **Public work** | Work you share where others can open it and check it, such as a repository, a write-up or a small tool |
| **Time-boxed** | Has a fixed start and a fixed end, set by someone else |
| **Code review** | Another person reads your change and comments on it before it joins the project |
| **Blocker** | Something that stops your work until someone else acts |
