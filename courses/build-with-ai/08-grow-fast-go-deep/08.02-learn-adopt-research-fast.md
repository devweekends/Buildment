Yazan builds class reminders for a language school (a fictional job). A post says that a scheduling tool called Mawid (made up) needs only ten lines. His deadline is Sunday.

## One post, ten lines, two bugs

<MermaidDiagram title="The fast path that failed" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Post: ten lines"] --> B["AI writes the setup"]
  B --> C["Works on his laptop"]
  C --> D["Used in the school's app"]
  D --> E["Server restarts at night"]
  E --> F["Jobs lost, then added twice"]
`} legend="Every step looks reasonable. The failure comes from a step that was left out: reading the docs." />

Yazan pasted an AI-written setup into the school's app. It worked on his laptop, so he shipped it. After a server restart, the scheduled jobs were gone. He added them again by hand, and some then ran twice. The speed was real. The understanding was missing, and reading the docs would have taken 30 minutes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the fast path cost">

In this example, the post saved Yazan one evening on Tuesday. The two bugs cost him two evenings on Friday and Saturday. That is a made-up count, but the pattern is common. A shortcut moves the cost to a later time, when you have less time to spare.

The setup he pasted was not wrong. It matched the post. What he lacked was a fact that only the tool's own docs held: by default Mawid keeps its jobs in memory, so a restart forgets them. Without that fact he could not even name the first bug, so he fixed it by hand and created the second one. You cannot diagnose a tool you have never read about.

Chapter 7.2 gave you a rule for this kind of moment: feeling faster is not the same as being faster, so measure your hours. Yazan's count of lost evenings is a measurement. His feeling on Tuesday was not.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask the docs, not the post

<InteractiveCanvas slug="adopt-a-tool-loop-sources" />

Yazan asks three sources one question: do scheduled jobs survive a restart? The post gives no limit. The AI answer is fluent and says yes. Only the docs give a version, a date and the real limit. A fluent answer is not a checked answer. Read the docs first, and write the date down.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to write down from the docs">

Open the tool's own pages, the primary pages of module 5, and not a copy or a summary. Write these down in your notes with the date you read them:

- **The version** you will install, and the version the page describes. They can differ.
- **The date** of the latest release note, and whether anything in it changes what you plan to do.
- **The licence**, from the licence page or file. Chapter 7.5 told you what no licence and the common licences allow.
- **The limits** the docs state: what it does not do, what is off by default, what it needs.
- **The way out**: how you would remove it from your project.

These habits come from the Redis case in module 6. Read the vendor's licence page and its dated announcement, not a summary. Compare release notes between versions. Note the date (the course's reading of its sources: B29, B31, B32, B33). A tool changes its rules, and a note without a date cannot be checked later.

An AI assistant can help here. Paste the page in and ask for a summary and for what the page does not say. Then read the page yourself. OpenAI's terms of use (effective 2026-01-01) tell users not to rely on output as a sole source of truth. They say to evaluate it, including by human review [B66]. Suppose the assistant names a package or a setting that you cannot find in the docs. Treat it as unconfirmed, as chapter 4.3 taught. Find the package on its official site, and check that it is real, used and maintained.

</GoDeeper>

## Four steps, two refusals

<InteractiveCanvas slug="adopt-a-tool-loop" />

Step through Yazan's four days, one step at a time. The loop has five states and refuses two moves: using a tool because a post said so, and using it because the lab ran. Each step took 20 to 40 minutes, about two hours in total, and each one leaves something written down.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the four days in minutes">

| Day | Step | Minutes | What he leaves with |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Docs first | 30 | Version 3.2, a dated release note, the licence, one limit |
| 2 | Tiny lab | 30 | A lab entry; a restart loses a 20:00 job |
| 3 | Explain back | 20 | The tool in his own words, with one gap fixed |
| 4 | Use small | 40 | One feature, a restart test, version and date in the README |

The minutes add up to 30 + 30 + 20 + 40 = 120, which is two hours over four days. These are the example's numbers. A bigger tool takes longer, and then you make the steps smaller, not skip them.

The tiny lab is the daily lab of chapter 4.2 with the study part already done by Day 1. Build 15 minutes: run the docs' example. Break 10 minutes: set a job for 20:00, stop the program on purpose and start it again. Write 5 minutes: the five-part entry from chapter 4.5, including what AI helped with. His break step is what found the limit by itself, instead of leaving it for a night in production.

Use small means one feature, with a test that would catch the thing you just learned. He also writes a review date, two weeks later, to ask whether the tool still earns its place.

</GoDeeper>

## Explain it back with four questions

<MermaidDiagram title="The explain-back check" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Say it with no notes"] --> B{"What does it do?"}
  B -->|"can say"| C{"What does it need?"}
  C -->|"can say"| D{"What breaks, and why?"}
  D -->|"can say"| E{"Why this one?"}
  E -->|"can say"| F["Use it small"]
  B -->|"stuck"| G["Back to the docs or the lab"]
  C -->|"stuck"| G
  D -->|"stuck"| G
  E -->|"stuck"| G
`} legend="Any question you cannot answer sends you back one step. Then you try again from the top." />

Explaining back is a short test, not a speech. Say four things with no notes: what the tool does, what it needs, what breaks, and why you chose it. Where you get stuck, go back to the docs or the lab and try again. It is the explain-back rule from chapter 7.4.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why this step is worth 20 minutes">

Explain-back is where you find out whether you understand the tool or only recognise it. Use the five angles from chapter 3.3, especially the failure case ("what breaks?"). Use the quiz habit from chapter 3.4 too. Ask an assistant to quiz you one question at a time and to wait for your answer, not to explain.

Two surveys give a reason to build your own checking habit. A 2025 survey asked 319 knowledge workers for 936 examples. Higher confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking. Higher confidence in themselves went with more [B64]. It is self-reported and shows an association, not a cause. In Stack Overflow's 2026 developer survey, 48% of respondents said they trust AI when they can easily validate the answers [B65]. It is a self-selected online sample. A tiny lab is one way to make validation easy, and explaining back is how you check your own understanding, not the tool's.

The most common mistake is to explain with the page open. Close it. If you cannot start without it, that is the answer.

</GoDeeper>

## Research a topic the same way

<MermaidDiagram title="A new topic in three steps" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Official page, with its date"] --> B["One example, by hand"] --> C["Explain it back"]
`} legend="The same loop, shorter: a topic has no project to join, so the last step is your own words." />

A new topic works like a new tool. Read the official page and write its date. Run one small example yourself. Then explain it back. Ask an assistant to find gaps in your explanation, not to replace the reading. Module 5 teaches how to ask two assistants and compare their answers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where AI helps in each step">

| Step | AI can help you | You must still |
|---|---|---|
| Docs first | Summarise a page you paste in; list what it does not say | Read the page; write the version and the date |
| Tiny lab | Draft the plan and suggest what to break (chapter 4.3) | Run it; check each command and package against the docs |
| Explain back | Quiz you one question at a time (chapter 3.4) | Answer with no notes |
| Use small | Suggest a first feature; draft a test | Read and edit every line you keep (chapter 7.4); update your log |

One decision never goes to the assistant: whether to adopt the tool. It does not know your deadline, your team or your users (chapter 1.5).

Measure the loop once, so you do not rely on the feeling of speed. In one careful study, experienced developers felt faster with AI but were slower. Later tools may be better. The lesson is to measure, not to trust the feeling [v1 S97a, S97b]. Write down how long each step took in your first week. By the fourth tool, you will know your own numbers.

</GoDeeper>
