Faisal joins a team of four at a small web agency (fictional). On Thursday of week 1 he sends a pull request of 900 lines that an AI assistant mostly wrote. The reviewer, Ruwa, asks why the code retries three times. He cannot say.

## Two pull requests, two evenings

<MermaidDiagram title="The same person, two weeks" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Week 1: 900 lines, mostly AI, unexplained"] --> B["Ruwa reviews alone: 2 evenings"]
  B --> C["Trust falls"]
  D["Week 2: 60 lines, a note on AI and checks"] --> E["Ruwa reviews: 20 minutes"]
  E --> F["Trust grows"]
`} legend="The times are this example's numbers. The pattern is the lesson: what you can explain, a reviewer can check." />

In week 1 Ruwa reviews alone for two evenings, because Faisal cannot explain what the assistant wrote. In week 2 he sends 60 lines with a note: what AI drafted, what he changed, what he ran. She approves in 20 minutes. He is the same person in both weeks. What changed is what she can check.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the same change, two ways">

The numbers are made up, but the pattern is real enough to plan around. A reviewer reads every line of a change. A change of 900 lines that its author cannot explain asks the reviewer to understand the code for both of them. Outreachy gives the same reason for its AI rule: AI code often looks correct at first glance and is slow for mentors to review [B67]. The reviewer in a team pays the same cost.

Faisal's week 2 note was short and had four parts:

- **What this changes:** the retry in the booking form, from three tries to two.
- **AI drafted:** the test. **I changed:** the retry count, because the form should fail in 6 seconds, not 9. **I ran:** the new test and the old ones.
- **Not checked:** a very slow network.
- **Question:** is 6 seconds the right limit for the agency's clients?

Note the last two lines. Naming what you did not check, and asking one question, tells the reviewer where to look first. It is the AI-use line of chapter 10.3 used inside a team.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort the team habits

<InteractiveCanvas slug="trust-or-erode" />

Sort eight team habits. Small, explained, early and written ones build trust. Big, hidden, late and unwritten ones erode it. Two cards have AI in them, and AI is not what changes their answer. What changes it is whether a teammate can rely on you.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three practices, with their sources and limits">

The sorting rests on three practices from large companies. Each has a limit, and none is proof that one way is best everywhere.

**Code review.** Google's published engineering practices say a reviewer should favour approving a change once it definitely improves the overall health of the code, even if it is not perfect. They also say that comments which help a developer learn something are always fine [B75]. This is one company's practice.

**Team safety.** In Google's team research, summarised by its re:Work site, <GlossaryTerm term="psychological safety" definition="A team's shared belief that people can speak up, ask a question or admit a mistake without being punished or laughed at." /> ranked first of five team dynamics, ahead of dependability, structure and clarity, and meaning and impact. The study covered 180 teams, 115 engineering project teams and 65 sales pods. Factors such as seniority, tenure, extroversion and sitting in one place were not significantly connected to how effective a team was [B76]. The limits: one company, and the summary does not give the study dates.

**Written updates.** GitLab's handbook uses <GlossaryTerm term="asynchronous" definition="Done without everyone being present at the same time, for example by written messages and notes instead of a live meeting." /> communication as a starting point. It works through public issues and merge requests, and it writes down the conclusions of conversations held offline [B77]. This is a company's stated practice, not evidence that it is better.

One more reading, for people who look for work. In US data, NACE's 2026 spring survey of 185 employers of college graduates found that working in a team was the skill most often sought on a resume (85%) [A33]. These are employers' self-reported preferences, not a promise.

</GoDeeper>

## Review is how a team teaches

<MermaidDiagram title="One round of review" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["You send a small change"] --> B{"Does it clearly improve the code?"}
  B -->|"yes"| C["Reviewer approves, may add an optional note"]
  B -->|"not yet"| D["Reviewer asks, you explain or fix"]
  D --> A
  C --> E["You read the note and learn"]
`} legend="Google's published standard: approve a change that clearly improves the code, even if it is not perfect." />

A review is not a test that you pass or fail. It is how a team teaches. Google's published standard says to approve a change once it clearly improves the code, even if it is not perfect. Your part is to make that easy: send small changes, and answer every question with a reason.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reviewing other people's work, with AI in the loop">

Mira did this in week 11 of chapter 8.1: she read a newcomer's change and left two kind comments. When you review, ask a question before you give a verdict ("why does this retry three times?"). Say what is good as well as what to change. Keep a comment about the code, not about the person.

If you ask an assistant to review a change for you, treat its comments as claims to test (module 9 returns to this in "Let AI critique your work"). You are responsible for every comment you post, whoever drafted it. Do not paste an assistant's review as if it were your reading.

The course "Collaboration, Feedback & Conflict Resolution" teaches giving and receiving feedback in depth. This chapter only shows where it sits in a team that ships code.

</GoDeeper>

## A written update, read in a minute

<MermaidDiagram title="A Friday update" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Friday update"] --> B["Done: what changed, with a link"]
  A --> C["Next: one thing, with a day"]
  A --> D["Blocked: what, and who can help"]
  A --> E["Decided: any call, written down"]
`} legend="Four short lines. The blocker line is the one teams most need and people most often leave out." />

A short written update replaces a meeting for teammates in other time zones. Say what is done, what is next, what blocks you and who could help, and what was decided. Keep it to four lines. A blocker is not a confession. It is information the team can act on today.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: Faisal's update, line by line">

Here is Faisal's update at the end of week 2, made up for this example.

- **Done:** booking form now fails after 6 seconds (link to the pull request).
- **Next:** the confirmation email, by Tuesday.
- **Blocked:** I cannot run the email service on my laptop. I tried the setup page and two fixes. Ruwa, could you check my settings file for 10 minutes?
- **Decided:** in Thursday's call, we keep the 6-second limit for now. Ruwa will ask the client.

Three habits show in it. The blocker names what he tried and who could help. The decision from the call is written where people who missed it can read it. And nothing in it is a plan for next month.

The word "blocker" was defined in 8.1. If you write the update with an assistant's help, you still own every sentence in it, and you check each fact against your own work, as chapter 10.2 taught.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask early, with what you tried

<MermaidDiagram title="Ask on day 1, or on day 5" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Stuck on day 1"] --> B["Ask: what I tried, expected, saw"]
  B --> C["Answer in minutes"]
  A --> D["Stay quiet until day 5"]
  D --> E["Ask under pressure"]
  E --> F["Rework and a missed date"]
`} legend="The costs are this example's. Asking is cheaper at the start for everyone, including you." />

Asking early costs a teammate minutes. Asking late costs the team days. A good question says what you tried, what you expected and what you saw. Try the assistant for a short, fixed time first, then ask a person. Saying "I do not know" out loud is part of the habit.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: asking a person, and the team that lets you">

Set a limit before you start, such as 20 minutes with the assistant on one problem. Then write your question for a person: the goal, what you tried, what you expected, what happened. Often writing it shows you the answer. If it does not, you have given your teammate everything they need.

People do this already. In the 2025 Stack Overflow developer survey, 75.3% of respondents said they would ask a person when they do not trust an AI answer [A64]. In the 2026 survey, as summarised on its blog, coworkers were the first source people used for work questions (72%) [A65]. Both surveys are self-selected online samples, so they describe habits, not what is best.

Asking is easier on a team that makes it safe. That is the finding about psychological safety in Google's team research [B76]. You help build that safety yourself, by answering other people's questions kindly and by saying "I made a mistake" early. The course "Ownership, Initiative & Adaptability" covers owning mistakes, and module 11 comes back to it when AI was involved.

</GoDeeper>

### Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Osama writes his Friday update for a team of five. Four facts: 1) He has been blocked for 3 days on a login error. 2) A teammate wrote that part and could answer in minutes. 3) His draft says 'Progress: good. Next: login. No issues.' 4) The release is in 5 days. Which change to the update builds the most trust?"
options:
  - "Add the blocker: what he tried, and ask the teammate who wrote it, by name"
  - "Keep it short and positive, because a long update takes the team time to read"
  - "List every small task he finished this week, so the update looks complete"
  - "Send it on Monday, once he has solved the login error alone and can report it"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. Fact 1 says 'No issues' is untrue, and fact 2 says help is near. A named blocker with what he tried lets the teammate help today, with 5 days left."
  - "Short is good, but 'No issues' hides a 3-day blocker. The team cannot help with a problem it cannot see."
  - "More completed tasks make the update look fuller, but they do not change fact 1. The blocker is still missing."
  - "Waiting for Monday adds more days of silence (fact 4 says only 5 remain), and it leaves the team with the same wrong picture."
```
