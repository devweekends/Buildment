Ruwa (a fictional fellow) can give about 15 hours a week for about three months. She wants to work with a real team, not alone, and she is new to open source. Her question is which team project to read about first.

## Three ways to spend three months

<MermaidDiagram title="Three ways to go deep" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["Ruwa: about 15 hours a week"] --> P["A program: apply, get a mentor"]
  R --> C["A community project: you join yourself"]
  R --> E["An event: one month"]
  P --> P2["Fixed dates, rules, a stipend"]
  C --> C2["Your own pace, no stipend"]
  E --> E2["Hacktoberfest 2026: no pull requests counted"]
`} legend="Each path has its own rules. The rules, not the name, decide whether it fits you." />

A program is time-boxed: fixed dates, a mentor, rules and sometimes a stipend. A community project has no application and no pay, and you set the pace. Hacktoberfest is an event of one month, and in 2026 it no longer counts pull requests. Every change you offer must follow that project's rules, so read them first.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the programs, with the date I read them">

A <GlossaryTerm term="contribution" definition="A change you offer to a project, such as a fix, a test or a page of docs." /> is the word programs use for each change you offer. These rules were read on their own pages on 2026-10-25. They change every year, so read the current page before you plan. In short: Google Summer of Code, LFX and Outreachy are paid programs of about three months. Hacktoberfest is a one-month event, and in 2026 it no longer counts pull requests.

| Program | Length | Pay | Who can join |
|---|---|---|---|
| Google Summer of Code (GSoC) | Projects of about 90, 175 or 350 hours. About 75% run 12 weeks; 8 to 22 weeks are possible [B70] | Two payments: 45% midway, 55% at the end. Set by purchasing power and project size. The 2026 table lists West Bank and Gaza at $900, $1,800 and $3,600 for small, medium and large projects [B71] | 18 or older, and a student or a beginner in open source. The FAQ says it is not for experienced open source developers [B70] |
| LFX Mentorship (Linux Foundation) | Each project sets its length. Most start at 12 weeks, from an FAQ page that still names 2022 programs, so treat it as medium confidence [B73] | For programs starting on or after 2026-07-01: a purchasing-power formula from a $6,000 baseline, minimum $1,000, maximum $6,600. The amount for Palestine was not found [B73] | 18 or older by the start date. Not in another Linux Foundation mentorship. Allowed to work where you take part. You apply as an individual [B72] |
| Outreachy | 13 weeks for the round from 7 December 2026 to 8 March 2027 [B74] | $7,000 for the internship [B74] | 18 or older. Full time, 30 hours a week. For people who face under-representation or systemic bias in tech in their country. Open to applicants around the world. You can be accepted once [B74] |
| Hacktoberfest | One month, October | No stipend. Digital stickers; a physical pack for three virtual stickers, shipped 8 to 12 weeks after the event [B69] | Anyone is welcome. In 2026 pull requests do not count; credit comes from livestream check-ins, DEV challenges and events [B69] |

Country rules sit on top of these. GSoC says contributors must not live in a U.S. embargoed country, and that a country missing from the stipend table cannot be paid [B70, B71]. LFX says you must not live where taking part is prohibited under US or other applicable law, and its page has no country list [B72]. Outreachy's page has no sanctions list and says offers are void where prohibited or restricted by law [B74]. Its December 2026 application closed on 31 August and its contribution period ends on 9 November, so that round is not open to new applicants now. The pages we read gave no 2027 dates [U7].

Why a team? We found no study showing that team projects teach early-career engineers more than solo projects [U14]. The reason in this course is practical: a team gives you review, deadlines and people who depend on you, which solo work does not.

The picture leaves out most of what decides a real application. Outreachy also asks about your situation. A community project you join yourself needs none of this. Use the health signs from chapter 7.5 (a licence, recent commits, several contributors, answered issues), read its contributing page, and start with one small change.

## Read the rules page, write the date

<MermaidDiagram title="Five facts from a rules page" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Open the program's own rules page"] --> B["Hours and length"]
  A --> C["Pay, and how it is paid"]
  A --> D["Who can join"]
  A --> E["The AI rule"]
  A --> F["Next dates"]
  B --> G["Write each answer with today's date"]
  C --> G
  D --> G
  E --> G
  F --> G
`} legend="Five questions, one page, one date. A missing answer is a question to ask, not a gap to guess." />

Rules pages change every year, and last year's page may be wrong today. Read the program's own page, not a summary, and write down five things with the date: hours, pay, who can join, the AI rule and the next dates. If a fact is missing, ask the program. Do not guess.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a fellow in Gaza and the missing facts">

Three facts were missing when this chapter was written. We did not find the next dates for GSoC 2027, for Outreachy's next round or for LFX terms starting in 2027 [U7]. We did not find the LFX stipend for Palestine [U8]. And we could not confirm whether fellows in Gaza can receive payments from GSoC, LFX or Outreachy, or whether Hacktoberfest swag ships there [U9].

So the honest rule is: do not plan around money until the program has answered you in writing. Ask a short, practical question: "Can a participant living in my place receive the stipend, and how is it paid?" Keep the answer with its date, beside the rules you read.

A <GlossaryTerm term="proposal" definition="The plan you send with your application to a program, saying what you will build and how." /> is the part of a GSoC application where the AI rule matters first. GSoC calls each open-source project that takes part an organisation, and each organisation sets its own AI policy. Read the organisation's page, then the program's.

</GoDeeper>

## Explain and edit every AI-assisted change

<MermaidDiagram title="From AI draft to accepted change" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["AI drafts a change"] --> B["You read every line"]
  B --> C["You run it and its tests"]
  C --> D["You edit it to the project's style"]
  D --> E["You write the pull request in your words"]
  E --> F["You answer the review"]
  A -.->|"unedited, cannot explain"| X["Not accepted"]
`} legend="The dotted line is the shortcut that programs now refuse in their rules." />

Two programs now say this in their rules. Outreachy will not accept unedited AI work that you cannot explain or correct. GSoC warns that an AI-written proposal could be rejected by an organisation. So read, run, edit and explain. In the pull request, say what AI drafted and what you checked.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the two AI rules and the course's line">

Outreachy's applicant guide discourages generative AI. It says it will not accept unedited AI-generated contributions that the applicant cannot explain or correct. Its reason: AI code often looks correct at first glance and is slow for mentors to review [B67]. The GSoC FAQ, updated 2026-02-20, warns that using AI to write a proposal could mean automatic rejection by an organisation, and tells applicants to check each organisation's policy [B68]. We did not find an AI rule on the LFX pages we read, so read the policy of the project you apply to. A community project often has one in its contributing file.

The course's own line is shorter and goes further. Explain and edit every AI-assisted contribution, in every place you contribute, whatever the rule says. Never send a pull request you cannot explain. This is the explain-back rule of chapter 7.4 and the AI-use line of chapter 10.3. Chapter 8.5 shows why it matters to the person who must review your work. OpenAI's own terms of use, effective 2026-01-01, also tell users to evaluate output, including by human review, and not to rely on it as the only source of truth [B66].

Note what the rule does not say. Programs do not forbid you to think with an assistant. They refuse work you cannot explain or correct. A mentor who must review code that looks right, but that its author cannot explain, has to find every mistake alone.

</GoDeeper>

## Twelve weeks, week by week

<MermaidDiagram title="A twelve-week plan" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Weeks 1 to 2: read the rules, run the project"] --> B["Weeks 3 to 4: one small change"]
  B --> C["Weeks 5 to 10: one real change, reviewed"]
  C --> D["Weeks 11 to 12: help a newcomer, write it up"]
  D --> E["Keep the links, the log and the write-up"]
`} legend="The same shape as Mira's twelve weeks in chapter 8.1. A community project may run longer, up to six months." />

A plan for about three months looks like Mira's in chapter 8.1. Read and run first. Make a small change, then one real change that goes through review. In the last weeks, help a newcomer and write up what you learned.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to keep as evidence">

Keep what a stranger can open and check, without asking you:

- Links to your pull requests and the review threads, including the rounds where you changed your mind.
- Your lab log for the weeks, with the five-part entries from chapter 4.5.
- A short write-up in your own voice (chapter 10.2), with the AI-use line of chapter 10.3.
- A note of each AI-assisted contribution: what the assistant drafted, what you changed and what you ran.

This is evidence, not a promise. Nothing in our sources shows that a team project leads to a job. It leaves choices and replies that you can point to, and that is the whole claim.

Plan the hours honestly. At 15 hours a week, 12 weeks is 15 x 12 = 180 hours, about the size of a 175-hour project. If an exam week or a power cut takes one week, write that in your plan and move the later weeks, as the weekly plan of chapter 7.3 allows.

</GoDeeper>
