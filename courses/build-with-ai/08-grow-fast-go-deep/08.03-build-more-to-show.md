Hanan and Osama each give 8 hours a week for 8 weeks, which is 64 hours. Then a mentor has ten minutes to look at what each of them made.

## Two ways to spend 64 hours

<MermaidDiagram title="The mentor's ten minutes" chart={`flowchart TD
  M["Mentor opens a project"] --> Q{"Is there a choice, a reason, a user or a failure to check?"}
  Q -->|"yes"| Y["She checks it, asks one question, and learns how the builder thinks"]
  Q -->|"no"| N["She can only count repositories"]
  Y --> H["Hanan: shift board, 2 trade-offs, 5 users"]
  N --> O["Osama: six one-line READMEs"]
`} legend="The mentor asks one question of every project. Which side a project lands on depends on what is inside it, not on how much there is." />

Hanan builds one small shift board for a café, used by five colleagues, and keeps a log of eight entries. Osama follows six tutorials and pushes six clones. In ten minutes the mentor can check Hanan's choices. She has nothing to check in Osama's. The hours are equal, so the difference is in what the work contains.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the same 64 hours">

The hours are equal on purpose. Hanan's 64 hours: 4 hours talking to the café, 36 building, 12 breaking and fixing, and 12 writing the log and the README. That is 4 + 36 + 12 + 12 = 64. Osama's 64 hours: six clones at about 10 hours each is 60, and 4 hours pushing and tidying them. That is 60 + 4 = 64.

Osama did real work and learned real tools. He may even have learned more tools than Hanan. But a tutorial's choices belong to the tutorial's author, and his READMEs say so in one line. Hanan's choices are hers, and each one has a reason that a stranger can read and argue with.

Nobody in this story is offered a job. The comparison is only about what a reader of the work can check.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort the work

<InteractiveCanvas slug="shows-thinking-or-noise" />

Sort eight pieces of public work. Ask one question of each: can a reader check how this person decides? A choice, a reason, a user or a failure explained all pass. Volume does not. A wrong bucket shakes and tells you why, so try again. Then press Sort again, and see which card you still miss.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what public work can and cannot promise">

Public work gives people evidence they can check. It does not promise a job. The evidence behind it is modest, and you should know how modest.

Start with one survey. It was published in 2021 by Linux Foundation Research and edX, and it asked more than 200 technical hiring managers and 750 open source professionals. In it, 44% of hiring managers named experience contributing to open source as the most important technical skill or experience, the highest of any item [B78]. The limits are clear. It is from 2021. A foundation that supports open source published it. The people asked already work in or hire for open source.

A page for the same foundation's tenth annual report says 69% of employers are more likely to hire an open source professional with a certification. It also says prior experience is becoming less of a requirement if candidates can show they can do the job [B79]. Its date is unclear, because the download is labelled 2022.

Outreachy says its contributions give a public portfolio, and that employers can see a history of public collaboration [B80]. That is a program's statement, not a study. An older interview study from 2013 found that employers and job seekers saw GitHub activity as a more reliable signal than a CV. It was quick to check [v1 S105]. The study was qualitative, and it recorded perceptions, not hiring outcomes.

We found no study that compares solo public projects with team contributions [U13]. So the sorting rule in the picture is the course's own reading. If a reader can check a choice, a reason or a failure, the work shows thinking. Say it this way: public work gives evidence others can check.

</GoDeeper>

## A series: one lab a week

<MermaidDiagram title="A weekly series" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["One lab a week (chapter 4.2)"] --> B["Entry in the lab log (chapter 4.5)"]
  B --> C["Short write-up in your voice (chapter 10.2)"]
  C --> D["Next week's question"]
  D --> A
`} legend="A series reuses work you already do. The new part is the question you set for next week." />

A series is low-cost public work, because you already run the labs. One lab a week becomes one log entry, then a short write-up. Each week ends with next week's question. A reader sees what you asked, what broke and how your questions change over the weeks.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: plan a series">

| Part | A plan that works |
|---|---|
| Length | Eight weeks, then a review. Stop or change the topic on purpose |
| Weekly output | One lab entry with the five parts from chapter 4.5: goal, what I did, what broke, what I learned, what AI helped with |
| Monthly output | One short write-up in your own voice, with an honest AI-use line (chapters 10.2 and 10.3) |
| One failure | At least one entry that explains something that broke and what you checked next |
| Before every push | No keys, no client data, no one else's personal data (chapters 2.2 and 4.5) |

Eight entries in eight weeks is a promise to yourself, not to a reader. If a week is lost to exams or a power cut, write one line that says so and carry on. Honest gaps are better than filler.

A series also gives you the next idea for free. The question at the end of one entry is the goal of the next. When the series ends, the log is a list of questions and answers that a mentor can read in order.

</GoDeeper>

## A small tool someone uses

<MermaidDiagram title="A small product loop" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Find one real user"] --> B["Build the smallest useful version"]
  B --> C["Watch them use it"]
  C --> D["Write the decision in the README"]
  D --> E["Change one thing, log why"]
  E --> C
`} legend="The loop runs on a real person's feedback. A product nobody uses has no loop." />

A small product can show more than a big one that nobody uses. Find one real user: a classmate, a club, a café. Build the smallest useful version, watch them use it, and write each decision in the README. Three users and two written trade-offs already give a reader a lot to check. You do not need a big idea, only a real person who uses it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a README that explains decisions">

A README is the first page a stranger reads, so it is where your thinking has to show. A useful one has six parts:

- **What it is for, and who uses it:** one line, with a number if you have one ("five colleagues at one café").
- **How to run it:** the shortest steps that work.
- **Decisions:** two or three, each with what you chose, what you gave up and why. This is the trade-off habit from chapter 7.1 in writing.
- **What broke:** one real failure and what you did about it.
- **What AI helped with:** the AI-use line from chapter 10.3.
- **What you would change next.**

AI can build the first version of a small tool quickly. That is fine, as long as the explain-back rule of chapter 7.4 holds: keep only what you can explain. Suppose an AI built a big app in a weekend, and the README says only "built with AI". It is noise even when it works. A reader has nothing to check.

</GoDeeper>

## Choose one to start

<MermaidDiagram title="Choose where to start" chart={`flowchart TD
  A{"Do you have a real user now?"} -->|"yes"| B["Small product and a README"]
  A -->|"no"| C["Weekly series and a log"]
  B --> D["Add a log entry each week"]
  C --> E["Look for one user by week 4"]
`} legend="Either path ends with a log and a README. The first step depends on whether a user exists today." />

Start with one thing and do it well. With a real user, build the small product and keep its README. Without one, run a weekly series and look for a user by week four. Never fake activity to make your profile look busy.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: honest limits">

Never fake activity: no commits made by a script, no backdated commits, no bought stars. They show nothing a reader can check, and platforms and readers notice. Chapter 10.6 comes back to this with commit history.

Keep other people safe too. Do not publish a client's data, a user's name or a screenshot with someone's private message. Ask a user before you name them. If a key or password ever reaches a public repository, treat it as stolen and cancel it first, as chapter 4.5 taught.

Last, keep the plan small enough to finish. A series that stops after three weeks and says so honestly is better public work than a project that was never updated and never explained.

</GoDeeper>
