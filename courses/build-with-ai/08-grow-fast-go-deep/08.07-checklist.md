## Module 8 checklist

- [ ] I can adopt a new tool with the loop: docs first, a tiny lab, explain it back, then one small use
- [ ] I can tell public work that shows thinking from noise, and I never fake activity
- [ ] I read a team project's own rules page and wrote down its hours, pay, who can join, its AI rule and the date I read it
- [ ] I will explain and edit every AI-assisted contribution, and say what AI drafted
- [ ] I can name the habits that keep a team's trust: small changes, written updates and early questions
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 8.2 to 8.5, finished the quiz in 8.6, and submitted my two plans below

## Learning log

1. **Your last shortcut:** Think of a tool, a library or a technique you used because a post, a video or an assistant said it was easy. What did you not read or test? What would the loop have asked you to do first?
2. **Your public work:** If a mentor opened your GitHub today for ten minutes, what could they check about how you think? What is the first thing you would add?
3. **Your hours:** How many hours a week can you really give a team project? Which project in the picture of chapter 8.4 fits them, and what would you ask before you rely on a stipend?
4. **Your last review:** When did someone review your work, or you review someone else's? What would you do differently now, when AI is part of the work? If nothing comes to mind, use Faisal's week 1.

## Your two plans

This task turns the module into two plans that you could start on Saturday. The first plan is about public work: what you will show, so that a reader can check how you think. The second is about a team project: where you will work with other people for about three months, under rules that you have read yourself. Plans change, and yours will. Three things matter. Your numbers add up. You read the rules on the program's own page yourself. And you can say how you will explain every line that an AI helps you write.

Ruwa from chapter 8.4 shows what a strong answer holds. She can give 15 hours a week, she needs a stipend, and she is new to open source. For public work she chose a small product, because she has real users. Three coaches at her sports club use a sheet for the keys to the hall, and she turned it into a small web page. She wrote what a reader can check in ten minutes. The README holds two decisions: a phone page instead of a paper sheet, which gave up working without a connection; and a rule that only coaches can edit. It also holds one failure, where the page showed yesterday's bookings because the coaches' phones kept an old copy. Her rhythm is one log entry each Friday for eight weeks. Her stop rule is a review in week 8: if the coaches do not use the page, she says so in the README and stops. Before every push she checks for keys and for the coaches' phone numbers, as chapters 2.2 and 4.5 taught.

For the team project, the picture in chapter 8.4 sent her to Google Summer of Code. She opened the program's own pages and wrote down what they said, with the date she read them. Projects are of about 90, 175 or 350 hours, and most run 12 weeks. A project of 175 hours over 12 weeks is 175 divided by 12, which is about 15 hours a week, so it fits. Payment comes in two parts. Contributors must be 18 or older, and a student or a beginner in open source. Then she wrote the AI rule in her own words. Using AI to write a proposal could lead an organisation to reject it. Every organisation has its own AI policy. So she will write her proposal herself, and she will read the organisation's policy before she applies. She also wrote what she could not find: the next dates, and how a payment would reach her. She will ask the program in writing before she plans around the money.

Her AI habit has four steps. For every change that an assistant helps with, she reads every line and runs it with its tests. Then she edits it to the project's style. Last, she writes in the pull request what AI drafted, what she changed and what she ran. She uses the assistant to explain error messages and to quiz her, not to write her proposal.

A weak answer reads very differently. "I will join Google Summer of Code because it pays. I will use AI to write my proposal and my code so I finish fast. For public work I will make more repositories." It is weak for four reasons. It has no hours and no sum, so nobody can tell whether the project fits. It shows no rules page read and no date. It ignores the AI rule, and the plan it does state is the one the programs warn against. And "more repositories" is volume, not evidence: a reader still has nothing to check. A mentor can only help with a plan that has numbers and a rule to discuss.

If you prefer not to use your own project, use this provided case. Osama is a university student. He has 9 hours a week, he is new to open source, and he would like a stipend but can manage without one. He has six lab log entries about caching for a campus café's ordering page, in a private repository with a one-line README. He also has five tutorial repositories. He has no real user yet. He uses an assistant every day.

Osama found a made-up program called Jisr Summer, and its rules page says this:

- **Length:** 12 weeks, from 1 June to 24 August.
- **Projects:** small, about 100 hours, or large, about 200 hours.
- **Stipend:** $600 for a small project and $1,200 for a large one, paid in two equal parts, midway and at the end.
- **Who can join:** 18 or older, new to open source (no more than three merged changes so far), and free for all 12 weeks.
- **AI rule:** "You may use AI tools. Every change you send must be one you can explain and have edited. Do not send AI output you have not read and run. Say in your pull request which part AI drafted. Proposals must be written by you."
- **Next dates:** applications open on 1 February.

Write Osama's two plans from these facts. One plan is for his public work. The other is for the project, with the hours sum, the AI rule, and how he will explain and edit every AI-assisted change. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a voice note link as well.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. Two careful people can plan the same three months differently. If your project is not the one your mentor would choose, that is fine, as long as your numbers add up and you read the rules yourself.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public-work plan | One series or one product, what a reader can check, a weekly or monthly rhythm, a stop rule, no faked activity | A plan, but the stop rule or what a reader can check is missing | Only a list of repositories or tools |
| Team project fit | Names a project and shows the sum of hours a week against its length, and says why it fits | Names a project, but the sum is missing or wrong | No project, or the hours are ignored |
| Rules page | Hours, pay, who can join, the AI rule and next dates, each with the date read or a note that it was not found | Three or four of the five | Fewer than three, or no date |
| AI rule and explain-and-edit | States the AI rule in your own words and a habit with four steps: read, run, edit, say what AI drafted | The rule or the habit, but not both | No AI rule, or "AI is allowed" with no way to explain |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet", at least three parts are "Met", and your reply to the mentor's follow-up question answers it with evidence.

After you submit, your mentor replies with one or two short questions. One might be: "If a mentor asks you to explain a function an assistant wrote and you cannot, what do you do in the next hour?" Another might be: "If your series stops after week 3, what do you write in the log?" A reply with evidence points at something real: a line of your plan, a rule you read, or a number you worked out. Answer in writing or in a short voice note.
