## Your table of when each option wins

This task is the table you started in 6.6, finished and checked. The point of the module is a habit. When an answer arrives quickly and sounds sure, you ask what your situation adds. You read the project's own page, and you say in writing when the answer would change. The table is the proof that you did all three. It is also the first part of the decision record you will use in your capstone.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. Dima runs the metrics for her university lab's three services on one Prometheus server. Her default came from an assistant: "Use VictoriaMetrics. It uses 7 times less memory than Prometheus." She wrote five facts. One server holds all three services. It keeps 30 days of data. Memory use sits near 20% of the server's RAM. She builds and runs it alone, and the lab pays nothing for it.

She asked a second assistant. It agreed about VictoriaMetrics, but it gave no figure. When she asked what it had left out, it said her memory use was not a problem. She read Prometheus's storage page and the VictoriaMetrics README, and wrote the date by each. She labelled "7 times less memory" as a claim from VictoriaMetrics's own benchmarks. Her table had two rows. Prometheus wins when one server holds the series and the retention. VictoriaMetrics is worth testing when memory or disk is the pain. She kept Prometheus. The fact that changed the answer, from the assistant's default, was that memory was not her pain. She would test VictoriaMetrics if memory use passed 70% of RAM, a limit she set herself, or if she added a second server.

Look at the shape of Dima's answer. The default is quoted. The context is five facts with numbers. The vendor figure is labelled, and nobody is accused of lying. The pages have dates. The table says when, not what. The decision comes with its own exit. A mentor can check each of these in a minute.

A weak answer looks different. It has a table of features for each tool with no context, such as "fast, popular, open source". It repeats a speed figure from a vendor page as if it were fact. It names no page, or it names a blog post and calls it the source. It ends with "it depends" or "use the fastest". Such a table is not wrong in every cell. It is empty of evidence, so nobody, including its author, can tell later whether the choice was a good one.

This gate gives you less help than earlier ones, on purpose. You now have the habits, so test your own answer against the shape of Dima's. For each part, ask whether a mentor could find the evidence in what you wrote.

## The provided draft

If you have no project, or you prefer not to use your own work, review this provided draft instead. Nabeel's team of three students is building a small tool for their university's student office. It runs on one machine, it is used only by the office, and the team sells nothing. They need a plain cache. Nabeel asked an assistant, and then wrote this table for the team. It contains mistakes, and finding them is your task.

| Option | Wins when | Loses when | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Redis | It is popular and everyone knows it | It is closed source now, so avoid it | the assistant |
| Valkey | It is about 3 times faster than Redis, so use it everywhere | Never | a project blog post |

His decision line reads: "Valkey, because it is faster. No need to read anything else." For this case, treat the figure "3 times faster" as something a project blog post said about its own tests. No machine or sizes are stated, and no date was written down.

Your task has three parts. First, find at least four problems in Nabeel's draft, name the habit from this module that each one breaks, and say how you would fix it. Second, rewrite the table for his team's real facts. Include a context row, one row each for Redis and Valkey, and what would change the pick. Third, say which two pages Nabeel should open first, what he should write next to each, and why a speed claim should not decide this choice.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer with one of the two tables below, depending on the route you chose. Your own table can be about any default and any outcome. What counts is whether the evidence is visible.

**Route 1: your own table.**

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Context that changed the answer | Five facts with numbers or a plain yes or no, and the one fact that changed the answer named | Three or four facts, or the changing fact is not named | Only goals, or fewer than three facts |
| Project pages | At least two pages from the projects themselves, each with the date read, and every maker's number labelled | Pages named, but a date is missing or a maker's number is not labelled | No project page, only assistant answers |
| Table of when each wins | At least two options, each with when it wins, when it loses and its page | Two options with a missing cell | One option, or no table |
| Decision and exit | A pick that follows from the context and what would change it | A pick without what would change it | A pick with no link to the context, such as "it is the fastest" |

**Pass rule for route 1:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met", two of them the project pages and the table. Your reply to the mentor's follow-up question must also answer it with evidence.

**Route 2: Nabeel's provided draft.**

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problems found | At least four of the five problems found | Three found | Two or fewer found |
| Habit named | The habit named for every problem you found | The habit named for all but one | The habit named for fewer |
| Table rewritten | A context row, Redis and Valkey rows with when they win and lose, and what would change the pick | Rows present, but the context row or the exit is missing | Not rewritten |
| Pages and claim | Two pages named with a date to write by each, and the speed claim handled as a maker's claim to test | Pages named, but the claim is handled loosely | No pages named, or the claim is used to decide |

**Pass rule for route 2:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met". Your reply to the mentor's follow-up question must also answer it with evidence. The licence mistake is a must: an answer that does not correct "closed source" cannot pass.

After you submit, the mentor replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Which fact changed the answer, and where did you read it?" or "How would you test the figure on your own data?" A good reply points to something you can show: a row in your table, a date, a page. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a link as well.
