Tala and Munir work in different places. On the same Monday, each asked an AI assistant the same question. Both people and both teams are fictional.

## Two teams ask the same question

<InteractiveCanvas slug="two-teams-same-answer" />

Tala leads a two-person team that is building an internal tool for a school office. Munir works at a company that sells hosted caching to its customers. Each asked an assistant: which cache should we use? Both heard the same answer, Redis. Only one of them could use it without checking more.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the story in full">

Tala's tool shows the school office which rooms are free. It is small. Two people build it, about 30 staff use it, and it runs on one machine in the school. The tool asks the same questions all day, so Tala wants a cache. A cache keeps answers close by so the program does not recompute them every time. She typed one line into an assistant: which cache should we use? The assistant answered Redis, and gave three reasons. It is popular, it is fast and it is easy to start. Tala then did what you will learn to do in this module. She opened the project's own licence page, wrote down the date, and checked her use against it. She did not sell the cache and did not offer it to anyone else. She decided that Redis was fine and wrote one line in her notes saying why.

Munir works at a company that sells hosted caching. Customers pay the company, and the company runs caches for them. He asked the same assistant the same question, and it gave the same answer, Redis. The answer had the same three reasons. It did not mention a licence at all. Munir had a feeling that something was missing, so he opened the licence page too. He found that Redis had changed its licence in March 2024. One of the licences now used for newer versions says you may not provide the software to others as a managed service. His company does exactly that.

The assistant was not lying to either team. It gave the common answer to a short question. What it did not have was a fact about the asker. Tala's use and Munir's use look the same in one sentence ("which cache?") and are very different once you know what each team does. That missing fact is what this module calls context.

Notice also what Munir's team did not do. They did not decide that Redis is bad. They did not decide that the assistant is useless. They took one missing fact, checked it on a page the project itself publishes, and made the choice again. Chapter 6.4 follows this story to its end, with the dates and the licences.

This is not legal advice. Tala read the page and made a plain reading for an internal tool. A company with money at stake, like Munir's, would also ask a lawyer.

Four to six words come back all through the module.

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| default | The answer most people and tools reach first, which is not always the answer that fits you |
| context | The facts about your situation that change the right answer: what you do, its size, who runs it, what you can pay |
| workload | What a system does all day: what it reads, what it writes, how often and how much |
| licence | The rules the owner sets for using, changing, sharing and selling the software |
| benchmark | A test that runs the same job on different systems and reports numbers, which is only as good as the job it runs |

## What the assistant could not see

<MermaidDiagram title="Same question, two situations" chart={`flowchart LR
  T["Tala: internal tool"] --> A["Assistant"]
  M["Munir: sells hosted caching"] --> A
  A --> R["Answer: Redis"]
  R --> T2["Tala: fine for her use"]
  R --> M2["Munir: read the licence first"]
`} legend="One assistant, one answer. The next step is different because of a fact the assistant did not have." />

The assistant saw one sentence. It did not see that Munir's company sells the cache to others, and Redis changed its licence in 2024. A fact about the asker, not about Redis, made the difference. Facts like this are called context. This module teaches you to give context first, then check the answer.

```openquestion
prompt: "Tala and Munir got the same answer from the same assistant. What one question would you ask each of them before you said yes to it? Write in everyday words."

modelAnswer: "I would ask Tala what the tool is for and who uses it, because a small tool for one office is a different job from a product. I would ask Munir whether his company sells the cache to other people, because that can change the rules about what he is allowed to use. Both questions are about facts the assistant never saw."
```
