Rahaf is building an attendance app for a sports club. She asked an assistant to name a cache three times in a row and got Redis three times. Was the assistant checking her project, or reaching for the usual answer?

## A reply picks from a ranked list

<InteractiveCanvas slug="likely-next-word" />

A model scores many possible next words, then picks one. Likely words rank first, and the common answer is usually the likely one. Ask 20 times and the common word comes out most often, but not every time. These weights are made up to show the idea.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how a reply is picked">

Google's Gemini API documentation describes the pick in plain terms. A model gives every possible next piece of text a score. A piece of text can be a word or a part of a word, and the documentation calls it a <GlossaryTerm term="token" definition="A word or a part of a word: the small piece of text a model reads and writes, one piece at a time." />. A setting called temperature controls how random the pick is. At temperature 0, the highest-scoring piece is always picked. Two other settings, top-k and top-p, narrow the candidates before the final pick [B1].

You may read advice such as "turn the temperature up to get different answers". It is not a lesson for you. The same Google page says its newest models (Gemini 4, and Gemini 3.6 and later) ignore custom values for these settings (checked 2026-10-25). Chat apps hide them [B2]. What you control is the question.

OpenAI's terms of use (effective 2026-01-01) say that outputs may not be unique, and that other users may get similar output [B10]. So if you and a classmate type the same short question, expect similar answers.

Anthropic's prompt guidance says that context, such as why something matters, gives the model more information about your goals. That helps it give a more targeted answer [B11]. This is vendor guidance, not an independent test, and it gives no size for the effect. The sampler above shows the idea; it is not a measurement.

| Claim in this chapter | Where it comes from | How far to trust it |
|---|---|---|
| A reply is picked from a ranked list | Google's API documentation [B1] | Good for that vendor's models |

## Tuning for taste makes answers more alike

<MermaidDiagram title="From tuning to similar answers" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Many people ask a similar question"] --> B["A model tuned with human feedback"]
  B --> C["A narrower set of answers to draw from"]
  C --> D["Many askers may hear similar answers"]
`} legend="The middle step comes from one 2023 study of two models. The last step is a likely result, not a measurement." />

Many assistants are tuned with feedback from people who rate answers. In one 2023 study of two models, that tuning helped on new tasks and made the answers less varied. A tuned assistant may therefore draw from a narrower set of answers, and many people may hear similar ones.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the study did and did not show">

Kirk and colleagues compared two ways of training a model. One is supervised fine-tuning, where the model learns from examples of good answers. The other is <GlossaryTerm term="RLHF" definition="Reinforcement learning from human feedback: people rate the model's answers, and the model is trained to produce answers that get higher ratings." />. They tested summarising and following instructions. The second way did better on inputs the model had not seen, but it "significantly reduces output diversity" on several measures [B5].

The limits matter. The study used two base models and two kinds of task, it is a preprint from 2023, and it is about variety within one model. It supports the sentence "tuned models give narrower answers". It does not support "every assistant gives one answer", and it does not say which answer is wrong. Narrow is not the same as bad. It means the answer you get may be an average answer, not the one that fits you.

</GoDeeper>

## Popular choices are picked often

<MermaidDiagram title="Popular is not always needed" chart={`flowchart TD
  T["A coding task"] --> P["The model picks the popular library"]
  P --> Q{"Did the task need it?"}
  Q -->|"yes"| F["Fine"]
  Q -->|"no"| X["An extra dependency: up to 45% of cases in the study"]
`} legend="From one 2025 study of eight models writing code. It measured code libraries and languages, not databases." />

In a study of eight models writing code, the models often picked a popular library, such as NumPy, that the task did not need. That happened in up to 45% of cases. Python stayed dominant in 58% of cases. Rust was never chosen. The study covers code libraries, not databases. The same pull is a fair warning for both.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the study and how far it reaches">

Twist and colleagues (first posted in March 2025, revised June 2026, in the Findings of ACL 2026) tested eight language models on coding tasks. They found a strong tendency to overuse widely used libraries, for example NumPy in up to 45% of cases where it was not needed. They found a default preference for Python even where another language fits better, with Python still dominant in 58% of cases, and Rust was not used once. The authors conclude that models favour familiarity and popularity over suitability [B6].

What it covers: eight models and a set of coding tasks. What it does not cover: databases, caches or patterns. So the course makes a smaller claim than "an assistant always gives the most popular answer". The claim is smaller. Assistants often lean to the popular option. That is measured for code libraries and languages, and the same lean is a reasonable warning for the choices in this module. Nobody has measured it for each of them.

Why might it happen? A common guess is that the popular option has more text about it in the training data. No source we read tests that cause, so treat it as a guess and keep the measured part: the lean exists.

</GoDeeper>

## The model may not know what changed

<MermaidDiagram title="Changes after a knowledge cutoff" chart={`flowchart TD
  K["Text the model learned from"] --> C["Its knowledge cutoff"]
  C --> A["2024-03-20: Redis changes its licence"]
  A --> B["2024-03-28: Valkey is announced"]
  B --> D["2025-05-01: Redis adds AGPLv3 for Redis 8"]
`} legend="An assistant whose cutoff falls before these boxes may describe the world as it was before them." />

You met the knowledge cutoff in 5.2. The three changes above happened in 2024 and 2025. An assistant whose cutoff falls before them describes Redis as it was before the change, and nobody is at fault. A tool that searches the web can see newer pages, so check the project's own page and write the date.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the Redis story fits this chapter">

Chapter 5.2 explained the cutoff and how makers publish it [B3, B4]. Here it explains why the common answer can be out of date as well as popular. Redis announced on 2024-03-20 that from Redis 7.4 it would use a different licence [B29]. The Linux Foundation announced Valkey on 2024-03-28 [B34]. Redis added a third licence option with Redis 8 on 2025-05-01 [B30]. Chapter 6.4 gives the details.

An assistant that stopped learning before March 2024 would describe a world where Redis is simply open source. So an old cutoff and the lean to popular answers can push the same way, toward the answer that was common before.

A tool that searches the web can read newer pages. It can still summarise them wrongly, so the check does not go away. Some facts change, such as a licence, a price, a version or a program rule. For those, open the project's own page, read it and write down the date.

</GoDeeper>

## It tends to agree with you

<InteractiveCanvas slug="likely-next-word-agrees" />

You met the agreeing habit in 2.2, and 5.3 gave the evidence. It matters here for one more reason. If your question leans toward a popular tool, such as "Redis is best, right?", an assistant may agree, and you hear the common answer twice. Ask "when would Redis be wrong?" as well.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: agreeing and the common answer">

Chapter 5.3 gave the evidence. Sharma and colleagues found sycophancy in five assistants, and people sometimes preferred a convincing agreeing answer to a correct one [B7]. OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update in April 2025 for being overly agreeable, and its follow-up post gave its own account [B8, B9]. Those are the company's words, not an outside audit.

The link to this chapter is simple. The common answer is the likely first word. A leaning question makes the same answer more likely to be confirmed. When you ask "Redis is best, right?", two pulls point at Redis: the popular option, and your own wish to hear yes.

What to do: word the question neutrally, and ask for the case against your idea. The three questions from 5.3, "What did you leave out? What would change your answer? What is the strongest case against it?", are the course's own method. No study has tested that exact wording. It fits what the vendors advise: ask for uncertainty and for supporting quotes [B16]. The method does not make an answer true. It gives you more to check.

</GoDeeper>

## Change the question, not a setting

<MermaidDiagram title="Fix the question, then check the answer" chart={`flowchart LR
  F["Your facts: workload, size, team, budget, licence"] --> Q["A question with the facts in it"]
  Q --> A["The assistant's answer"]
  A --> L["Ask what it left out"]
  L --> P["Check the project's own page, with the date"]
`} legend="The question and the check are the two parts you control." />

A setting will not fix this. Google's documentation says its newest models ignore custom randomness settings, and chat apps hide them. What you control is the question. Put your facts in it, ask what was left out, and check the answer on the project's own page, as in module 5.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: facts to put in a question">

For a technical choice, five kinds of fact change the answer most often. Write them before you ask.

| Fact | Example for Rahaf's attendance app |
|---|---|
| What it does all day | About 400 members check in over the weekend; staff read the list a few times a day |
| How big it is | One club, one rented server, a few thousand rows |
| Who builds and runs it | Rahaf alone, a few evenings a week |
| What it may cost | A small monthly hosting bill she pays herself |
| Rules that apply | She does not sell the app to anyone, so no licence worry |

These numbers belong to the example. With them, the question is no longer "which cache?". It is "which cache for a club app with these facts, and what would change your answer?". The assistant now has something to be specific about, and you have something to check it against.

Why not ask three times and keep the most common answer? Because the most common answer is the one we are questioning. Repeating a question helps when there is one checkable right answer, such as a sum [B12]. It does not tell you which cache fits your club. Asking several assistants is the subject of 5.3; remember there that different assistants often make the same mistake, so agreement is not proof [B15].

</GoDeeper>
