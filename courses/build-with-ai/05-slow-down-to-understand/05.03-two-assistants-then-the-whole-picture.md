Yara has read the pages and written her facts. Before she decides, she wants to see whether a second assistant says the same, and what each answer might leave out.

## Same question, two assistants

<InteractiveCanvas slug="agreement-is-not-proof-two" />

Yara asks assistant A and assistant B the same question, in two fresh chats and with the same facts. On who can join GSoC they differ, so she knows where to look first. On the October reward they agree: both say four pull requests. That agreement feels like proof. It is not, and the next beat shows why.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to ask two assistants fairly">

Send the same text, with your own facts, in two new chats. Do not paste the answer of A into B and ask "is this right?". That question invites a yes, which chapter 2.2 called the agreeing habit. Keep the chats apart, and save each answer with the name of the tool and the date.

Chapter 2.3 gave the rule of two tools per job. At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), good choices are chat assistants such as Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude. Claude is for people aged 18 or over, so a younger fellow uses another pair. Check that each tool opens for you, at your age, on your device, and use the web version in your phone's browser if an app is missing.

If only one tool opens, ask it in two new chats and compare. Anthropic's guide to reducing hallucinations suggests running the same prompt several times and comparing, because answers that differ can show a made-up detail. That is vendor advice, not an independent test. It is weaker than two different tools, because one model tends to repeat itself, so the primary page must do more of the checking.

Apply the privacy check from chapter 2.2 to both chats. Your facts go in; passwords, client data and other people's personal details stay out.

</GoDeeper>

## Why two can make one mistake

<MermaidDiagram title="Two readers of much the same text" chart={`flowchart TD
  T["Much of the same public text"] --> A["Assistant A"]
  T --> B["Assistant B"]
  A --> W["The same wrong answer"]
  B --> W
  W --> P["Only the primary page settles it"]
`} legend="Assistants learn from much of the same text, so two of them are not two independent checks." />

Assistants learn from much of the same public text, so they can make the same mistake. In one 2025 study of over 350 models, two models that were both wrong gave the same wrong answer 60% of the time, on one leaderboard. Two assistants are not two independent experts. Agreement should raise your confidence less than it does.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what studies say about asking several">

Four published studies bear on asking several times or asking several models. Each is independent academic work, and none tested your question.

- **Same model, many tries.** Wang and colleagues (ICLR 2023) sampled several reasoning paths from one model and took the most consistent final answer. On 2022 models, accuracy rose by 17.9 points on a grade-school maths test (GSM8K), 11.0 on SVAMP, 12.2 on AQuA, 6.4 on StrategyQA and 3.9 on ARC-challenge. These tests have one checkable answer. The method does not help when the model is wrong the same way each time.
- **Copies debating.** Du and colleagues (a 2023 preprint) had several copies of a model propose answers and debate. They report better maths and reasoning and fewer false facts. The copies come from one model family, and it costs more calls.
- **A crowd of different models.** Schoenegger and colleagues (a 2024 preprint) combined 12 models on 31 forecasting questions. The crowd was not statistically different from 925 human forecasters. It was one tournament with yes or no questions, and the authors saw the models lean toward yes.
- **Shared errors.** Kim and colleagues (ICML 2025) tested over 350 models. On one leaderboard, when two models were both wrong, they chose the same wrong answer 60% of the time. Larger and more accurate models had more similar errors, and models from the same provider or design added to it.

Read together: asking several is good at finding disagreement, and it is not a panel of independent experts. Disagreement tells you where to look. Agreement tells you less than it seems to.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort what two answers tell you

<InteractiveCanvas slug="agreement-is-not-proof" />

Tap each pair of answers into a bucket. Where the answers differ, look first, because that is the cheapest place to find a mistake. Where they agree on a fact that matters, read the page anyway. Where they agree on wording, a draft can be kept. The tricky cards are the ones where agreement feels safe.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what counts as a fact that matters">

| Kind of claim | What to do | Example from the cards |
|---|---|---|
| A rule, limit, price, date or deadline | Read the primary page | The reward rule, the email limit, the deadline |
| A licence or a permission | Read the licence file | Use in a paid product |
| A command or a setting | Read the docs for your version | The install commands |
| Wording, a title or a tone | Edit it yourself; it can be kept as a draft | The first line, the post title |
| What is best for you | Decide it yourself, using your facts | Which program to apply to |

Stakes decide how hard to check. A wrong first line costs a rewrite. A wrong deadline can cost a whole application. "Kept as a draft" does not mean the draft is yours yet. You still read it, edit it and answer for every sentence, as chapter 1.5 said.

The cards are invented examples. Real replies mix agreement and difference in one answer, so sort claim by claim, not answer by answer.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask what was left out

<InteractiveCanvas slug="agreement-is-not-proof-left-out" />

Yara asks three questions. What did you leave out? What would change your answer? What is the strongest case against it? She asks neutrally, because "is this right?" invites a yes. The assistant names two things the first answers skipped. Anything new it names goes back to the page, so she checks both on the FAQ.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the three questions, and why they are neutral">

In Yara's words: "What did you leave out of your answer about joining a program? What would change your answer? What is the strongest case against applying to GSoC, given my facts?" The questions do not say which answer she hopes for. So the assistant has nothing to agree with.

These three questions are this course's own method. No study tested this exact prompt. The method fits vendor advice from the guide mentioned above. That advice is to let a model say it does not know, to ask for a supporting quote, and to run a prompt several times. The method also fits what studies found about agreeing with the user. Sharma and colleagues (a 2023 preprint) found that five assistants tailored answers to what the user seemed to believe. They also found that people sometimes preferred a convincing agreeing answer over a correct one. In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update that it called overly flattering or agreeable, and later said launching it had been the wrong call.

What the assistant says it left out can be wrong too. Every new claim goes back to the primary page. In Yara's case, the GSoC FAQ does warn that using AI to write a proposal could mean automatic rejection by an organisation. It also asks for proof of residency and tax forms. Neither point was in the first answers or in the answers of A and B. She adds a line to her notes: write the proposal herself, and read each organisation's AI policy first.

</GoDeeper>

## The whole method on one page

<MermaidDiagram title="The method, with its loop" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["Research: the page and your facts"] --> A["Ask two assistants"]
  A --> C["Compare the answers"]
  C -->|"differ, or agree on a fact"| K["Check on the primary page"]
  C -->|"agree on wording"| W["Keep it as a draft"]
  K --> L["Ask what was left out"]
  L -->|"a new claim"| K
  L --> D["Decide; write the record"]
`} legend="A new claim loops back to the page. Wording can skip the page." />

Follow the arrows. Anything new the assistant says goes back to the page, and the loop ends only when no new claim is left. Wording can skip the page and stay a draft. The record in chapter 5.4 keeps these steps on one page.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the record holds">

The decision record has nine parts, in this order. They are the question and your facts. Then the first answer, with the tool and the date. Then assistant A and assistant B. Then where they differed and where they agreed on a fact that matters. Then the primary pages you read, with dates, and what each confirmed or changed. Last come what was left out, and your decision with what would change it.

Notice that the first answer is saved before anything else. Without it you cannot see what the research changed, and that change is the evidence that slowing down was worth it. Chapter 5.4 gives you the steps and a full example.

</GoDeeper>
