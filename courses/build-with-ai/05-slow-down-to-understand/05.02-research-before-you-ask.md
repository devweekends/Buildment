Yara has about 8 hours a week. She wants to join an open-source program this year, which means working on a project whose code anyone may read and reuse. She asks a chat assistant how, and the answer arrives in five seconds.

## A fast answer with two doubtful claims

<InteractiveCanvas slug="first-or-researched-answer-claims" />

The answer makes two claims. Open four pull requests (proposed changes to a project's code) in October and you earn a reward. And Google Summer of Code, called GSoC, is only for students. Both sound sure, and nothing shows which part is current. So Yara opens the pages the claims are about.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the whole first answer">

Yara typed one line, with none of her own facts: "How can I join an open-source program this year?" This is the answer, invented for this example.

> You have two good options. First, join Hacktoberfest in October. Open four pull requests on any public project and you earn a T-shirt. Second, apply to Google Summer of Code. It is only for students, so check that you are enrolled. Contributors work on a project for a summer and are paid.

Hacktoberfest is a one-month event in October, and anyone may take part. The answer is short, clear and friendly. It also holds four facts that can be checked: the reward, the number four, who may join GSoC, and that contributors are paid. Yara cannot tell from the text which of the four are current, because the tone is the same for all of them.

This is a first answer, as chapter 5.1 named it: given before anything was checked and before she shared her own facts. A first answer is useful, because it tells her what to look up. It is not yet a decision. In 2026, Hacktoberfest's own page says that pull requests no longer count toward rewards, so the first claim is out of date.

</GoDeeper>

## Read the page before you ask

<MermaidDiagram title="The order of work" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q["Your question"] --> P["Read the primary page"]
  Q --> F["Write your own facts"]
  P --> A["Ask the assistant"]
  F --> A
  A --> K["Check its claims on the page"]
  K --> D["Decide; note the date"]
`} legend="Read and write first, ask second, check last. The date goes in your notes, because pages change." />

The primary page is the page the owner publishes: rules, docs, a licence file or release notes. Read it first and write down the date you read it. Then write your own facts. Only then ask, and check each claim in the answer against the page.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: which page is primary for which question">

| You want to know | The primary page |
|---|---|
| Who can join a program, and its rules | The program's own rules page or FAQ (its page of common questions) |
| Whether a library is still looked after | Its repository: last release, recent changes, open problems, licence file |
| What a free plan includes | The tool's own plans or pricing page |
| What you may do with some code | The licence file in its repository |
| Which command works | The tool's docs, for your version |
| What changed in a new version | The release notes |

A primary page is the best place to start, but it is not a guarantee. It can be old. One mentorship program's FAQ page that we read for this course still names programs from 2022. So write down two things: the date on the page, if it has one, and the date you read it. Also copy the exact sentence you relied on. When the page changes later, you can see what changed.

It is easy to skip this step, because a confident answer feels finished. In a 2025 survey of 319 knowledge workers by Microsoft Research, higher confidence in a generative AI tool went with less critical thinking. The survey was self-reported, and it shows a link, not a cause. OpenAI's terms of use, effective 2026-01-01, tell users not to rely on its output as the only source of truth. They also tell users to check it for accuracy, including by human review. We read those terms in a translated version, so check the English text before you quote them.

Reading a public page needs no private data. When you write your own facts for an assistant, use the privacy check from chapter 2.2: leave out passwords, client data and other people's personal details.

</GoDeeper>

## An answer can be older than the rule

<InteractiveCanvas slug="first-or-researched-answer-dates" />

An assistant learned from text written before a date, its <GlossaryTerm term="knowledge cutoff" definition="The date after which a model has seen no new text. Newer facts may be missing from its answers." />. A rule that changed later can be missing. Hacktoberfest 2026 stopped counting pull requests, yet an older answer can still promise a reward for four.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: cutoffs, and tools that search the web">

Makers publish a cutoff for each model. Anthropic's model overview, for example, separates a "reliable knowledge cutoff" from the cutoff of the broader training data. The first is the date through which the model's knowledge is most extensive and reliable. A 2024 preprint by Cheng and colleagues, which studied models from 2023 and 2024, found that a model's effective cutoff often differs from the date its maker reports. So the date a maker publishes is a guide, not a promise.

Tools that search the web can read newer pages. They still make mistakes. Chapter 1.4 gave the figures from a study of news answers by the European Broadcasting Union and the BBC, and the same care applies here. A tool that shows links has given you a reading list. It has not checked the facts for you.

The habit is short. Ask the assistant for its cutoff, or look it up on the maker's page. Then, for any rule, price, limit or date, check the project's own site. The picture above uses an invented date for the assistant, so do not read it as a fact about any real tool.

</GoDeeper>

## Write your facts before you ask

<MermaidDiagram title="Yara's five lines of context" chart={`flowchart TD
  Y["Yara's facts"] --> H["Hours: 8 a week"]
  Y --> D["Device: old laptop, phone"]
  Y --> L["Level: new to open source"]
  Y --> G["Goal: one real project"]
  Y --> X["Limit: no paid tools"]
`} legend="Five short lines. Each is a fact the assistant cannot know unless she writes it." />

Next Yara writes her context, the facts around her question. The hardest facts to see are her <GlossaryTerm term="constraint" definition="A fact you cannot change, such as your hours, your money, your device or a deadline." />. Five short lines are enough. Leave out names, passwords and anything private.

```predict
prompt: "Yara adds her facts to the same question: about 8 hours a week, an old laptop, new to open source. She still has not opened any rules page. What is the new answer most likely to be?"
options:
  - id: fits-old
    label: "Fits her better, old rule may stay"
  - id: fits-fixed
    label: "Fits her better, old rule is fixed"
correctOptionId: fits-old
explanation: "Her facts change which programs the answer suggests and how many hours it expects, so it fits her better. They do not tell the assistant what changed in 2026. Only the page does that, so the old reward rule can sit inside a better-fitting answer."
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to write your facts">

Write your question again with the five lines inside it. For example:

> I am a beginner in open source with about 8 hours a week, an old laptop and a phone. I want one real project to work on this year, and I cannot pay for tools. Which programs or events could I join, and what are their current rules? Tell me what I should confirm on each program's own page.

The last sentence asks the assistant to point you to the primary page. That turns the answer into a reading list. Anthropic's guidance on prompting says that giving context, or the reason behind a request, can help a model give a more targeted answer. That is vendor advice, not an independent test, and it does not measure how big the effect is.

Separate wishes from constraints. "I would like to be paid" is a wish. "I have 8 hours a week" cannot change this month, so it is a constraint. Constraints do most of the work in a decision, because they remove options early. Chapter 1.4 listed what an assistant cannot know about your task. Your constraints are the part of that list that you can type.

</GoDeeper>

## Give the assistant your facts

<InteractiveCanvas slug="first-or-researched-answer" />

Tap the pills and watch which answer Yara ends with. Reading the page fixes the facts. Giving her hours and her laptop fixes the fit. Each does a different job. A second assistant, on its own, can repeat the same old rule.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each setting shows">

The four answers in the picture are the four places Yara can end. "Old answer" is a first answer with no page and no facts. "Fits her only" is the surprise. Her facts made the answer fit her, yet the old reward rule can still sit inside it, because nothing in her facts could correct it. "Current only" has the right rules but may suggest a project far too big for 8 hours a week. "Researched" has both.

The second assistant does not change the result by itself. A 2025 study at a machine learning conference tested over 350 models. On one leaderboard, two models that were both wrong gave the same wrong answer 60% of the time. Different assistants learn from much of the same text, so they can share a mistake. Chapter 5.3 shows how to use a second assistant well.

The rules in the picture are this course's own, written for Yara's example. They are not a measurement of how often assistants are wrong.

</GoDeeper>

## Her researched answer, claim by claim

<MermaidDiagram title="Yara checks each claim" chart={`flowchart TD
  C1["Claim: 4 pull requests earn a reward"] -->|"Hacktoberfest page, 2026"| R1["No: pull requests do not count"]
  C2["Claim: GSoC is for students only"] -->|"GSoC FAQ"| R2["Partly: students or open source beginners, 18 or older"]
  C3["Question: does it fit 8 hours a week?"] -->|"project sizes"| R3["Yes: 90 hours over 12 weeks is 7.5 a week"]
  U["Open: can a payment reach her?"]
`} legend="Two claims corrected, one fit checked with arithmetic, one question left open." />

Yara checks each claim on its page. Pull requests do not count toward Hacktoberfest rewards in 2026. GSoC is for students or open source beginners. A 90-hour project over 12 weeks needs 7.5 hours a week, and she has 8. One question stays open.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the program facts Yara read (checked 2026-10-25)">

| Program | What its page says | Id |
|---|---|---|
| Hacktoberfest 2026 | Runs in October. Anyone is welcome. Pull requests no longer count toward rewards. Credit comes from livestream check-ins, DEV challenges and events. Rewards ship 8 to 12 weeks after the event. | B69 |
| Google Summer of Code | You must be 18 or older, and a student or a beginner in open source. It is "not for experienced open source developers". Projects are about 90, 175 or 350 hours. About 75% of projects run 12 weeks, and 8 to 22 weeks are allowed. | B70 |
| GSoC stipends | A stipend is a payment to a contributor. It is paid in two parts, 45% midway and 55% at the end, set by purchasing power and project size. The 2026 table lists "West Bank and Gaza" at $900, $1,800 and $3,600 for small, medium and large projects. A country not in the table cannot be paid. Proof of residency and tax forms are needed. | B70, B71 |
| GSoC proposals | The FAQ warns that using AI to write a proposal could mean automatic rejection by an organisation, so check each organisation's policy. | B68 |

Yara's arithmetic uses the page's own numbers. A 90-hour project over 12 weeks is 90 divided by 12, which is 7.5 hours a week. A 350-hour project over 12 weeks is about 29 hours a week, far over her 8. A medium project's $1,800 would arrive as $810 midway (45%) and $990 at the end (55%), and $810 plus $990 is $1,800.

The pages do not show everything. They do not say whether a payment could reach a person in Gaza, so Yara writes that down as a question to ask the program. She also found no dates yet for next year's program. She leaves a note to look again, with today's date, 2026-10-25. Her notes keep the claim, the page, the sentence and the date for each row, which is what chapter 5.4 turns into a record.

</GoDeeper>

### Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Suhaib builds a booking page for a café and asks a chat assistant: 'Which free host should I use?' It answers: 'Use Brightbox. Its free plan gives 5 GB and your site never sleeps.' Suhaib notes four facts. 1) The page needs about 40 MB. 2) The café gets about 30 visits a day, spread through the day. 3) He gave the assistant facts 1 and 2 in his question. 4) Brightbox's own plans page, read today, says free sites sleep after 10 minutes without a visit. What do these facts support?"
options:
  - "His facts shaped the answer, but only the plans page can correct the sleep claim"
  - "The sleep claim is probably right, because he gave the assistant his facts first"
  - "The 5 GB claim is the problem, because his page needs only about 40 MB"
  - "The sleep rule cannot matter, because 30 visits a day keep the site awake"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. Fact 3 explains why the answer fits the café, but the sleep claim is about the plan itself. Fact 4, from the plans page, is the evidence that settles it."
  - "Giving the assistant his facts helps the answer fit him. It does not make each claim current. Fact 4 says free sites sleep after 10 minutes, so the claim is wrong."
  - "Fact 1 shows storage is not the problem, because 40 MB is far below 5 GB. The claim that fact 4 contradicts is the sleep claim."
  - "Thirty visits spread over 24 hours is about one every 48 minutes. That is longer than 10 minutes, so the site would sleep between visits (facts 2 and 4)."
```
