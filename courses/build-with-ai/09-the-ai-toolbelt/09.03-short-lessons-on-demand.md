Murad is a new freelancer. On Thursday a client will talk about their API, and Murad has 15 minutes tonight and no one to ask.

## A request with four parts

<MermaidDiagram title="What a lesson request holds" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["Your request"] --> L["The lesson arrives"]
  L --> Q{"Does it list sources?"}
  Q -->|"yes"| O["Open each source before you trust it"]
  Q -->|"no"| A["Ask again for the sources"]
  A --> L
`} legend="A lesson with no sources gives you nothing to check, so you send it back." />

A good request names the topic and the minutes. It also asks for three things: a plan, three checks at the end, and the sources to read. The plan keeps the lesson short. The checks make you answer. The sources give you somewhere to go next. A lesson with no sources gives you nothing to check.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the request as text">

Here is the request Murad types. You can reuse the shape for any topic.

```text
Give me a 15-minute lesson on how a web API request and response work.
I am a beginner. Start with a plan with minutes for each part.
End with three questions. Ask them one at a time and wait for my answer.
List the pages I should read to check what you said.
```

Each line has a job. The topic and the minutes stop the answer from growing. "Beginner" sets the level. The one-at-a-time rule makes Murad do the recalling, which chapter 3.4 explained. The last line asks for sources, and a source is useful only if it exists. As in chapter 7.3, open every source before you trust it.

The idea of a "15-minute lesson" is this course's own method. No study has tested whether a 15-minute lesson from these tools is accurate. So treat the lesson as a first pass that you check, never as a source.

</GoDeeper>

## Run the loop with Murad

<InteractiveCanvas slug="short-lesson-loop" />

Step through Murad's evening. The loop has five states: ask, lesson, quiz, check and your notes. Two shortcuts are refused: pasting the summary as notes, and skipping the check. The check is one claim, not the whole lesson. Watch the quiz score at step 4 and what the check finds at step 7.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why one claim is enough">

You cannot check every claim in a 15-minute evening. So choose well. A good claim is one you would repeat to someone else, or one you will build on. A number, a command, a name or a rule is a good choice, because you can look it up and compare the words.

If the check finds nothing wrong, you still gained something. You now know what the page says, and you found out that this lesson was right about the part that mattered. If it finds a mistake, as Murad's did, you learned what to distrust in the next lesson from the same tool.

Asking the assistant "are you sure?" is not a check. Studies find that AI assistants lean towards agreeing with the person they talk to, as the agreeing habit in chapter 2.2 showed. In one study, five assistants showed this across four kinds of tasks. A second answer from the same tool is more of the same. The check must come from outside the chat.

</GoDeeper>

## One claim, one primary page

<MermaidDiagram title="The check, in three boxes" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Lesson: 404 means crashed"] --> B["Docs: 404 means not found"] --> C["Your note: 404 means not found"]
`} legend="The lesson and the page disagree. The note follows the page." />

Pick a claim you would repeat. Open the primary page, the place where the fact is decided, not the assistant. Compare the words. If they differ, trust the page and fix your note. Write the page name and the date, as you did in chapter 3.5.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: which page counts">

Check it on a primary page, as in module 5: the official documentation, a rules page, a licence file or the release notes. A blog post, a video or another AI answer is not a primary page. They may be right, but they can repeat the same mistake.

Write the date next to what you read. Documentation changes, and your note is only as fresh as the day you read the page. If the page and the lesson disagree and you cannot tell which is newer, ask a mentor before you repeat the claim to a client. If two pages disagree, say so in your note and do not pick one by guessing.

</GoDeeper>

## Tools that quiz you

<MermaidDiagram title="Three ways to be quizzed" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q["Does a study tool open for you?"] -->|"yes"| U["Use it, and expect mistakes"]
  Q -->|"no"| F["Tell any assistant: one question at a time"]
  U --> C["Still check one claim"]
  F --> C
`} legend="Both routes end in the same check." />

At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), ChatGPT has a study mode and Gemini has Guided Learning. Both ask you questions, and both say they can be wrong. If neither opens for you, tell any assistant to ask you one question at a time. Open the web version in your phone's browser first.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: access, ages and limits">

ChatGPT study mode works on the web, iOS and Android across plans. It can quiz you one question at a time, give hints and build a study plan from your notes. OpenAI says it can make mistakes and may sometimes give a direct answer. It uses your normal plan limits, and the Study option is not available in Temporary Chats, GPTs or Projects. Gemini Guided Learning starts from the prompt box at the Gemini web app: Add files, More tools, then Guided Learning. Its help page lists no age or country limit for Guided Learning itself, but its OpenStax resources are in English, for the United States, and not for under-18 users.

ChatGPT and Gemini are for ages 13 and over, and ChatGPT asks for a parent's permission under 18. Claude is for ages 18 and over. The web pages of ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude list Palestine, while the Gemini Android download list does not, so use the browser. Check each one on your own device.

For listening, Gemini Notebook, called NotebookLM until July 2026, can turn sources you add into an audio overview. Chapter 3.5 taught the habit of listening first and then checking one claim. Its formats at the time of writing are Deep Dive, The Brief, The Critique and The Debate. The longer length and the talk-back mode are English only. Google says audio overviews, including voices, are AI-generated and may contain inaccuracies or audio glitches, so the same check applies.

</GoDeeper>

## Keep the lesson, not the transcript

<MermaidDiagram title="From a lesson to something you own" chart={`flowchart TD
  L["The lesson"] --> X["Paste it as your note"]
  L --> N["Write five lines in your own words"]
  X --> XR["Only the assistant's words, unchecked"]
  N --> NR["You can explain it with no lesson open"]
`} legend="Only one of the two notes survives a question from a client." />

The lesson belongs to the tool. The note belongs to you. Write five lines in your own words, add the page you checked and the date, and link the note to the project it serves. Come back after a few days and quiz yourself, as chapter 3.4 taught.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when a short lesson is enough">

A short lesson fits a first pass before a call, a meeting or a new tool. It does not replace the habits of module 3. For a skill you need to hold for months, use the five angles, daily practice and spaced review. For something a client will rely on, check more than one claim and read more than one page.

Keep the notes in files you own, as chapter 2.4 asked. If the tool changes or your account closes, your notes remain, and the lesson can be asked for again in a minute.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

<MermaidDiagram title="The loop in one line" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Ask"] --> B["Lesson"]
  B --> C["Quiz"]
  C --> D["Check one claim"]
  D --> E["Your notes"]
`} legend="The five moves of this chapter, in order." />

Keep these five moves. Two questions follow, each on a new case.

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Wisam asks for a 15-minute lesson on Git branches. The lesson says: 'A branch copies all your files into a new folder, so keep branches few.' He scores 3 of 3 on the quiz and plans to explain branches to his team tomorrow. What should he do first?"
options:
  - "Explain the claim tomorrow with the quiz score as his proof"
  - "Paste the lesson summary into his notes unchanged"
  - "Ask the assistant to verify the claim"
  - "Compare the claim with Git's own documentation"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "The quiz came from the same lesson, so it can repeat the lesson's mistake. A full score shows he understood the lesson, not that it is true."
  - "A pasted summary holds only the assistant's words. Nobody has checked the claim, and it is not in Wisam's own words."
  - "The same assistant tends to agree with the person it talks to. The check has to come from a page outside the chat."
  - "Right. The claim is one he will repeat, and the documentation is the primary page. He compares the words and fixes the note if they differ."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Shatha has 15 minutes before a call with the library's IT volunteer. Her assistant's lesson makes eight claims and lists three sources, one of them the library system's own documentation. She can open only one source in the time she has. One claim is a number she will say aloud on the call. What should she check?"
options:
  - "All eight claims, checked one after the other"
  - "The number she will say aloud, on the library page"
  - "The first claim, the one the lesson starts with"
  - "The most surprising claim, asked again in a second chat"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "She can open only one source in her time. Eight claims do not fit, and one well-chosen check builds the habit."
  - "Right. It is the claim she will repeat, a number can be looked up, and the library system's own page is the primary page. It fits her time."
  - "Coming first does not make a claim important. She should pick the one she will repeat, not the one that came first."
  - "A second chat is not a primary page. It can repeat the same mistake in new words."
```
