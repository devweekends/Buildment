## Same code, two visitors

<InteractiveCanvas slug="toolbelt-one-line-readme" />

The first visitor opens Bashar's repository and finds one line: "A timer for groups." She cannot tell what it does, so she leaves. The second visitor opens the same repository after Bashar adds a picture and a 40-second demo. He understands it in about a minute. The code did not change. What Bashar showed did.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: Bashar's story in full">

Bashar studies software engineering at a university. Five students in his course study together four evenings a week, and they kept losing time arguing about when to stop and when to rest. So Bashar built a small timer. One person taps Start, and every phone that opened the group link shows the same 25-minute countdown, then a 5-minute break, then the next round. A chime sounds when each one ends. It took him three weeks of evenings, and his group used it every night for a month.

He put the code on GitHub and shared the link in two group chats, hoping other students would use it. Over the next month a few classmates opened the repository. No one ran the timer. Its README, the file that explains a project, held a single line: "A timer for groups." Later a classmate told him, "I thought it was a clock." Bashar was disappointed, because the code was good.

His mentor asked him to do one thing: open the repository as a stranger would. He saw no picture, no steps and no hint of what the timer did that a clock does not. A stranger has no reason to spend a long time guessing. The project was a good one that gave a visitor nothing to hold on to.

In one evening he added three things. First, a diagram of how the pieces fit, written as text. Second, a recording of one round on a phone, about 40 seconds long. Third, a short "Try it" section of four lines with the link. The next week two classmates tried the timer, and one opened an issue with an idea for a sound. Showing does not guarantee users. In this example it removed one reason why people could not try the project.

Notice what Bashar still had to do himself. He had to check that the diagram matched the real code, and he had to record the real app, not a made-up one. Tools can help with a picture, a recording or a lesson. They cannot know what his project really does. That job stays with the person who built it, which is the idea behind this whole course.

</GoDeeper>
