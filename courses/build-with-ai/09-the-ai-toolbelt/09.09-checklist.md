## Learning log

1. **Your picture:** Which box or arrow in your visual did you remove or confirm, and how did you know what was behind it? If you have not made one, use Shatha's deleted "Text message server" box from chapter 9.2.
2. **Your claim:** Which claim in your lesson or README section did you check, and on which page? What would you have repeated if you had not checked?
3. **Your access:** What does your token or tool see that the job does not need? How will you make it smaller?
4. **Your no:** Which critique point did you reject, and what would change your mind?

## Your demo kit

This task collects what you made in chapter 9.7. It does not need to be impressive. It needs to be real, honest and safe to show. If you made the kit for your own project, hand it in. If you worked on Lujain's case in 9.7, or you prefer not to use your own work, review the provided kit below instead.

A strong answer, told about a fictional fellow, goes like this. Nabil built Shelf, a one-page list of which books are out at his school library. His reader was the librarian. He asked an assistant for a diagram as Mermaid text. It drew a box called "Login service". Shelf has no login, so he deleted the box and the arrow, and he wrote in his note that he looked for the code behind each remaining box. For the README, he wrote "Try it in three steps" himself and checked one claim, that `gh issue list` shows all issues, against the GitHub CLI manual. The manual says it shows open issues, 30 by default, so he wrote that and added the page name and the date.

For the GitHub task, Nabil made a fine-grained token for the `shelf` repository only, with read and write access to issues and a 7-day expiry. He ran `gh issue list`, then `gh issue create` for one next step, and he wrote that the first command only reads. He deleted the token the same day. For the critique, the assistant gave him four points. He fixed the missing run steps, which he could see. He tested the point about dates on 31 January, found a real bug and fixed it. He rejected a rewrite in a framework, with the reason "the page has 60 lines and my goal is a page a volunteer can read". He rejected the fourth, which was only praise.

Nabil's answer works because every part has evidence a mentor can see. There is a deleted box, a checked claim with its page and date, and a token with one repository and an expiry. There is also a rejection with a reason tied to his own numbers. A weaker answer says "I checked the diagram" and shows the same diagram, or says "I used a safe token" and does not say what it could reach. It lists five accepted critique points with the reason "the assistant knows more than I do". A mentor cannot learn anything from those words, and neither can you.

Your own kit can be about any project and can go wrong in any way. What counts is whether the habits are there. For each part, ask whether a mentor could see the evidence in what you wrote. If a part has only a claim and no proof, add the proof or say plainly that you did not do it.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with one of the two tables below, depending on the route you chose. Your own kit can be about any project. What counts is whether the habits are there.

**Route 1: your own demo kit.**

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visual | Every box and arrow matches the project, and you name at least one item you removed or confirmed and how you knew | A visual with no item named, or one box you cannot explain | No visual, or boxes with nothing behind them |
| Lesson or README section | Three steps a stranger can follow, and one claim checked with the page name and the date | Steps, but the claim is unchecked or has no page or date | No steps |
| GitHub workflow | One repository, only the permissions the task needs, an expiry, reads and changes told apart, and untrusted text not given to a tool with write access | Two of those four are present | A token for every repository, a secret shown, or no access limit at all |
| Critique log | Every point has a decision and a reason, with at least one tested and one rejected | Decisions for all points, but some without a reason | Every point accepted, or no reasons |

**Pass rule for route 1:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met", one of them the GitHub workflow. Your reply to the mentor's follow-up question must also answer it with evidence.

**Route 2: Lujain's provided kit.**

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problems found | All four problems found | Three of the four found | Two or fewer found |
| Habit named | The habit named for every problem you found | The habit named for all but one | The habit named for fewer |
| Fix works | Every fix would work, and the token is revoked first | Three of four fixes work, and the token is revoked first | The token is not revoked first |
| Log rewritten | Both entries have a decision and a reason that uses a fact from the case | One entry has both, or the reasons do not use the facts | Neither entry has a reason |

**Pass rule for route 2:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met". Your reply to the mentor's follow-up question must also answer it with evidence. Revoking the token first is a must: an answer that does not revoke it cannot pass.

Once you submit, your mentor answers with one or two short questions. Here are two possible ones: "Which arrow did you remove, and how did you know?" or "What would you change in your token if the job doubled?" A good reply points to something you can show, such as a line in your kit, a page and a date, or a result you saw. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a voice note link as well.
