## Part 1: Check one claim about AI and jobs

The first part uses chapters 1.2 and 1.3 on one claim you have really heard. Choose one sentence about AI and jobs from a video, a post, a classmate or someone in your family, and write it down exactly. Then check it in four steps. First, say what kind of claim it is, a prediction, a company's or vendor's own claim, or an independent measurement, and who made it. Second, say what the evidence in this module shows about it, with the country, the date and the sample. Say whether it supports the claim, partly supports it, or does not. Third, say the limits: what that evidence cannot tell you. Fourth, say what you will do about it this week, which may be nothing, and why.

The point is not to prove the claim true or false. The point is to say how far to trust it. Many claims are partly true. A claim can also hide two parts, a fact about now and a prediction about later, and each part needs its own check. If a claim made you afraid or relieved, that is a fine reason to check it, but a feeling is not evidence.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Nadim's uncle told him, "AI writes most code now, so there are no software jobs left." Nadim splits the sentence in two. "AI writes most code now" is a claim about today with no source. The nearest public claim is a company statement: Google's CEO said in 2024 that more than a quarter of its new code is generated by AI and then reviewed. That is a company claim, not a count of all code. "There are no software jobs" is a claim about jobs, so he checks it against chapter 1.3. In US data, the labour department projects 10% more developer jobs by 2035, which is a projection. Indeed postings were about 21% below their February 2020 level in October 2026, and postings are not jobs. Stanford's payroll study shows hiring of workers aged 22 to 25 in exposed jobs below trend, which its authors call descriptive. Nadim's verdict is partly: a first job is harder to get in US data, and "no jobs" goes too far. His limits: it is all US data, none of it is about his city, and none of it predicts his own future. This week he will ask his uncle where he heard it, and he will add one finished project to his public profile.

A weak answer would read: "This claim is just hype, so I will ignore it." Another weak answer would read: "This claim is true, so I am worried." Both pick a story and stop. Neither names the kind of claim, a source, a date or a limit, so a mentor cannot tell whether the evidence changed anything. It is Rana's path or Khaled's path from chapter 1.1, in one sentence.

If you prefer not to use a claim you have heard, use this provided claim. A commentator in a video says: "AI will replace all junior developers by the end of next year. The data already shows that nobody is hiring them." Check it in the four steps.

## Part 2: Split one task

This part brings the module together on one piece of real work. You take a task and split it three ways. First, what AI can help with: the drafting, searching, explaining and comparing. Second, what the AI does not know about this task, under the four kinds from chapter 1.4. Third, the calls that stay yours, with how you will check each one. The point is not to use as little AI as possible, or as much. The point is to see the line clearly before you start, so the fast parts go to the tool and the thinking parts stay with you.

Choose a task you will really do in the next few weeks, from your study, a project or paid work. A good task is concrete and has a person it is for: "a weekly homework page for my students' parents", not "learn web development". If you prefer not to use your own work, use the provided case below. Both are marked the same way.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Reem, the teacher from chapters 1.4 and 1.5, wants a simple web page where her 32 students' parents can see each week's homework. Under AI help, she lists four things. The AI can draft the layout of the page and compare two free ways to publish it. It can draft the message that tells parents where the page is, and explain how to update it each week in a few minutes.

Under what the AI does not know, Reem writes one fact or more for each kind. Context: most parents will open the page on a phone, and term starts in three weeks. Private data: students' names and marks must never appear on the page, and she will not paste them into a chat. Local and recent: the school changed its rules this year on what teachers may post online, so she must check them with the head teacher. Nobody wrote it down: she asked two parents, who said many families share one phone, so the page must load fast on weak data.

Reem's calls are the strongest part. She will choose how to publish the page after testing both ways on an old phone. She will agree with the head teacher what may appear on it. She will check each week's homework against her own lesson plan before it goes up. She also writes one line about who answers: "If a parent sees the wrong homework, I fix it the same day." Every call has a check next to it, and a person's name.

A weak answer to the same task would read: "AI can help with everything. AI does not know my class. I will check the results." It may be true, but it names no drafts, no missing facts and no checks. It would not change a single thing Reem does, and a mentor cannot tell from it where the line between the tool and the person sits.

If you prefer not to use your own work, use this provided case. Majd, from chapter 1.4, has been asked by a small NGO that runs free coding workshops for teenagers to build a donation tracker. Here is what he knows. About 40 regular donors give each month: some in dollars online, some in shekels in cash at the office. Today the office manager writes every donation in a paper notebook. The NGO wants to record each donation, see the monthly total, and send each donor a thank-you message. The budget is $250 with nothing to pay each month. Two volunteers will use it on one shared laptop at the office. The deadline is in six weeks. Split Majd's task three ways, as Reem did.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read both parts with the table below. Two careful people can read the same claim, or split the same task, differently, and that is fine. What matters is that every item is specific, that every source has a country, a date or a sample, and that every call you keep has a check.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kind of claim | Names the kind (prediction, company or vendor claim, or independent measurement) and who made it, and says why | Names a kind, but not who made it or why | No kind named, or only "true" or "false" |
| Evidence and limits | A verdict (supports, partly, does not) with a source that has a country, a date or a sample, and one stated limit | A verdict with a source but no limit, or a limit with no source | No source, or a number with no date or sample |
| AI help | Three or more specific drafts, searches, explanations or comparisons | One or two specific items | No specific item, or "AI can do everything" |
| What the AI does not know | A specific fact for each of the four kinds | Specific facts for two or three of the four kinds | Specific facts for one kind or none |
| Calls that stay yours | Three or more calls, each with how it will be checked | One or two calls with a check | No call with a check, or the AI makes the main decision |
| Who answers | Both: one sentence on who answers, and no private data given to the AI | One of the two | Neither |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet", at least four parts are "Met", and your reply to the mentor's follow-up question answers it with evidence.

After you submit, the mentor replies with one or two short questions about your claim check or your split. For example, they may ask what would change your verdict on the claim. They may ask which missing fact came from a person rather than a page, or how exactly you will check one call. A good reply points to evidence: the person you asked, the page you read, or the test you will run. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a voice note link as well.
