Rawan leads a small, fictional software agency. A year ago it had five engineers. Now its budget fits three, and the clients get about the same amount of work, because the team uses AI tools. Two seats were easy to decide. The third was between Omar, Sana and Mazen. She says it was the hardest decision of her year, and that nobody's work was bad. The agency is invented, but the pattern is in what real companies said in 2025 and 2026.

## Companies say: fewer people, more work

Several companies said they would do more work with fewer or flat numbers of people. Some also pulled back. Klarna began hiring support staff again, and the CEO of Duolingo said he did not see AI replacing employees. We found no company in these sources that handed all its software work to AI.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each company said, with dates and limits">

The number of people a company employs is its <GlossaryTerm term="headcount" definition="The number of people a company or team employs." />. Every item below is a company's statement about itself, often reported second-hand.

**Shopify**, memo of 7 April 2025: staff must show why they cannot get what they want done using AI before they ask for more headcount. AI use also counts in performance reviews. Shopify's headcount fell from 8,300 to 8,100 in 2024, and its CFO said it could keep headcount relatively flat. Other layoffs in 2022 and 2023 also shaped those numbers.

**Amazon**, CEO memo of 17 June 2025: "We will need fewer people doing some of the jobs that are being done today, and more people doing other types of jobs". It expects AI to reduce its total corporate workforce in the next few years. That is forward-looking.

**Salesforce.** In 2025, its CEO said customer support went from about 9,000 to about 5,000 people ("I need less heads"), with about half of interactions handled by agents. A spokesperson said hundreds of staff were moved to other roles. On its May 2026 earnings call, he said engineering headcount had stayed at about 15,000 for two years because of AI efficiencies. He said hiring is in sales, which people still do.

**Block**, shareholder letter of 26 February 2026: the company cut from over 10,000 people to just under 6,000, citing "intelligence tools" and "smaller and flatter teams". That is Block's explanation. Gartner said in February 2026, about customer service, that most recent reductions were influenced by broader economic conditions.

**Klarna.** In May 2025, its CEO said cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor", which led to lower quality. Klarna began recruiting human support staff while its chatbot still handled about two thirds of enquiries. A year earlier it had said the assistant did the work of 700 agents. **Duolingo**: an April 2025 memo said it would gradually stop using contractors for work AI can handle. After a backlash, its CEO wrote on 22 May 2025 that he did not see AI replacing what its employees do, and that hiring continued at the same speed. **IBM**: its CEO said in May 2025 that AI replaced the work of several hundred HR staff. He said total employment had gone up and that the savings paid for more programmers and salespeople.

Gartner also made two forecasts. In June 2025, it said that by 2027, half of the organisations that expected to cut customer service staff a lot would drop the plan. In February 2026, it said that by 2027, half of the companies that blamed headcount cuts on AI would rehire similar roles under different titles. Forecasts are not facts. The pattern in the sources is fewer hires, and flat or smaller teams, with people kept in the loop and some reversals.

</GoDeeper>

## Beginners gain speed, and find fewer openings

<MermaidDiagram title="Two true things about beginners" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Task speed, in studies"] --> S2["Beginners often gain the most"]
  H["Hiring, in US data"] --> H2["Young workers in exposed jobs: fewer openings"]
  S2 --> C["Speed alone does not decide who a team keeps"]
  H2 --> C
`} legend="Studies: support agents, freelance programmers, developers at three large firms. Hiring: Stanford payroll data, revised August 2026." />

In studies, less experienced workers often gain the most speed from AI. In US data, hiring of the youngest workers in exposed jobs is down. So speed cannot be the whole reason a smaller team keeps someone. Speed is now easy to get. Something else has to separate people.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: who gains, who is hurt, and what is mixed">

Who gains speed. Among 5,179 support agents, novices gained about 34% and experienced agents little. Among 95 freelancers building a web server, less experienced programmers benefited more. In three field experiments with 4,867 developers, juniors gained about 27% to 39% and seniors about 8% to 13%. Among 758 consultants, those below average gained 43% and those above average 17%, but everyone gained on tasks inside what the tool did well.

Who is hurt in hiring. The Stanford study finds the gap in workers aged 22 to 25, and no comparable gap for experienced workers in the same jobs. SignalFire, a venture capital firm, reports a large fall in entry-level hiring at big tech companies and startups, with the causes being its own interpretation.

What is mixed for experts. The 2025 METR trial found experienced maintainers slower with AI, and METR now calls that result out of date, with newer data too weak to size the change. Anthropic's usage data is a vendor's data about its own chat product. It found that long-time users had about 3 to 5 percentage points higher success, judged by the model itself. DORA's 2025 report says AI is an amplifier of what a team already is.

Do not read this as "seniors win" and "juniors lose". Speed gains and hiring harm are different measures, and years of experience are not the same as judgement. What the evidence supports is a different question, which the next beat answers.

</GoDeeper>

## Four habits a smaller team keeps

<MermaidDiagram title="Four habits, one loop" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["1. Give the AI your context"] --> B["2. Check what it made"]
  B --> C["3. Own the result"]
  C --> D["4. Work well with others"]
  D -->|"next task"| A
`} legend="The course's reading of the evidence in the fold below, not a rule for hiring." />

Four habits show up in the evidence. Give the AI the context it lacks (chapter 1.4). Check what it makes. Own the result, because people and companies answer for AI mistakes (chapter 1.5). Work well with others: employers still rank teamwork and problem-solving first. These are the course's reading of the evidence, not a hiring rule.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the evidence behind each habit">

**Give the AI your context.** DORA's 2025 report, a survey of about 5,000 technology professionals sponsored by Google Cloud, describes AI as an amplifier. It raises a team's existing strengths and its existing weaknesses. An assistant with the client's rules and limits gives better drafts than one without them, as chapter 1.4 showed.

**Check what it made.** In Stack Overflow's 2025 survey, 46% of developers distrusted AI accuracy and 33% trusted it. Two thirds named answers that are almost right, but not quite, as their biggest frustration. In its 2026 survey, a self-selected sample of over 30,000, nearly half trusted AI when they could verify its work, and only 6.6% trusted it for important decisions. Veracode, a security vendor, reported that 45% of code samples failed its security tests. Use is high, trust is conditional, and checking is the work.

**Own the result.** In June 2023, a US court fined two lawyers and their firm $5,000 for filing cases that an AI had invented. It wrote that lawyers have a gatekeeping role. Module 11 studies this and other cases in full. Developers also keep some tasks for themselves. In the 2025 survey, 75.8% did not plan to hand deployment and monitoring to AI, 69.2% project planning and 58.7% committing and reviewing code. When they did not trust an AI answer, 75.3% would still ask a person.

**Work well with others.** In a spring 2026 survey of 185 US employers, teamwork (85%) and problem-solving (82%) were the skills most often sought on resumes. Employers rated communication and teamwork highest, at 4.5 of 5. In Stack Overflow's 2026 survey, 72% named coworkers as their first source for work questions. Gallup found that fear of job loss was lower where workers felt respected by their managers. That is an association, not a cause.

None of these sources says who a particular team will keep. The four habits are where the evidence points, and each one is something you can practise.

</GoDeeper>

## Rawan's last seat

<InteractiveCanvas slug="who-the-smaller-team-keeps" />

Change how much each habit matters to Rawan and watch the totals. Omar ships the most. Sana gives the AI context, checks every result and answers to the client. Mazen is steady and helps others. Find the settings where Omar leads.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: Rawan's answer, and how the scores were chosen">

Rawan kept Sana. Her reason was a Tuesday in March. A clinic's booking page showed the wrong opening hours for two days. Omar's change had gone in quickly, and his assistant had been told the layout of the page but not that the clinic closes on Fridays. Nobody tested a Friday. Omar fixed it when asked, and said the assistant had written that rule. Sana's changes always came with the client's rules in the prompt, a test for the odd days and a note on what she had checked. When something did fail, she called the client herself. Years of experience did not decide it: Sana is the newest of the three.

The scores run from 1 to 5, and they are the course's judgement for three fictional people. Omar scores 5 on raw output, 3 on giving context, 2 on checking, 3 on owning the result and 3 on teamwork. Sana scores 2, 4, 5, 5 and 4. Mazen scores 3, 3, 3, 4 and 4. Each score is multiplied by how much that habit matters, and the products are added.

At Rawan's weights, Sana has 48 points, Mazen 38 and Omar 32. Omar leads in only 3 of the 243 possible weight settings. In all three, raw output "Matters a lot" while checking and ownership "Matter a little". In four more settings he ties with Sana. Mazen never leads. Real people do not fit five numbers, and Rawan also looked at the clients, the budget and who else was on the team.

## Why this is easier for you than ever

<MermaidDiagram title="What AI gives a learner, and the habit that keeps you in charge" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["What AI gives a learner now"] --> B["Explanations at your level (3.3)"]
  A --> C["Help when a lab breaks (4.4)"]
  A --> D["Pointers to the page to read (5.2)"]
  A --> E["A second look at your answer (5.3)"]
`} legend="The number in brackets is the chapter with the habit for that kind of help." />

Nobody can promise you a job. But the help available to you is better than it was. An assistant can explain, help you debug, point to the page to read and check your answer. Each has a habit in this course that keeps you in charge.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: access, not a promise">

What changed is access. A learner with a phone and a browser can ask for an explanation at their level at any hour. They can ask why a command failed, which official page covers a question, and for a second look at an answer. Courses have always offered these, but a fellow studying alone with weak data rarely had a person available for each.

Each kind of help has a risk and a habit. Explanations can feel clearer than they are, so close them and recall (chapters 3.2 and 3.3). A fix for a broken lab may be wrong, so you run it and break it yourself (chapter 4.4). A pointer can be out of date or invented, so you read the primary page (chapter 5.2). A second opinion from the same assistant is not a check, so you ask two assistants and treat agreement as not enough (chapter 5.3).

Many workers are still early. In Pew's September 2025 survey, 21% of US workers did some of their work with AI, and 65% used it little or not at all. That is US data from one survey, and it changes. It suggests that a fellow who builds these habits now is not behind most workers. It is not a promise about any job.

AI also changes what is rare. Building a first version is now easy, as chapter 1.8 shows. Giving context, checking, owning and working with others are what is left, and you can practise them from this week.

</GoDeeper>
