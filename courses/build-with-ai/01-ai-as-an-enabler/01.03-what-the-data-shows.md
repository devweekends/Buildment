Dalia finishes her degree in four months. This week she heard three things: "there are no junior jobs left", "AI only helps beginners" and "it is all hype". She wants to know which ones the data supports. Chapter 1.2 gave her a way to read a claim. This chapter uses it. It reports real harm plainly, and it reports what the data does not show.

## Worry is not the same as job loss

<MermaidDiagram title="Two different questions" chart={`flowchart TD
  E["What workers expect"] --> E2["18% say AI or automation will likely end their job within 5 years (very or somewhat likely)"]
  H["What laid-off workers report"] --> H2["1% name AI or automation as the main reason"]
  E2 -.->|"not the same as"| H2
`} legend="Gallup surveys of US workers in 2026. Expectation and outcome are different questions. Workers may not know why they were laid off." />

Many workers are worried, and the worry is real. In a February 2026 Gallup survey of 23,717 US employees, two questions got very different answers. One asked what people expect. The other asked why laid-off people lost their jobs. The first measures a feeling about the future. The second measures a reason for a past event.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: more surveys, and what they cannot tell you">

Worry is common and rising. In an October 2024 Pew survey of 5,273 US workers, 52% were worried about AI's future effect on the workplace and 36% were hopeful. Also, 32% expected fewer job opportunities for them in the long run. Only 16% said then that some of their work was done with AI. By September 2025, that share was 21%, and 65% used AI little or not at all.

Experts and the public disagree. In Pew's 2024 surveys, 64% of US adults but 39% of AI experts expected AI to mean fewer jobs over 20 years.

In August 2026, Gallup asked 507 workers whether technology could make their job obsolete. 27% said yes, a new high, up from about 20% a year earlier and 13% in 2017. The question says technology, not AI, and its margin of error is up to 6 points. In a separate Gallup analysis, workers who used AI often were more likely to expect their job to be eliminated, and supportive managers narrowed that gap. Those are associations, not causes.

Developers worry about accuracy. In Stack Overflow's 2025 survey, 84% used or planned to use AI tools, 46% distrusted their accuracy and 33% trusted it. Two thirds named answers that are almost right, but not quite, as their biggest frustration.

All of these measure opinion and expectation. They tell you how people feel about the future. They do not count lost jobs.

</GoDeeper>

## Test six claims against the data

<InteractiveCanvas slug="fear-versus-data" />

Pick a verdict for each claim before the card flips: the data supports it, partly, or does not. Each card shows what the data says and where it comes from. Most of this data is from the US. A job is AI-exposed when AI can do many of its tasks. No source here predicts one person's future.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading the cards">

**Laid off because of AI.** The laid-off group in the Gallup sample was small, and the answers are self-reported. Workers may not know whether AI influenced a decision. The card says what the survey found, not what happened in every company.

**Developer jobs shrinking.** The US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) counts software developers, QA analysts and testers together. That group had about 1.9 million jobs in 2025, with 10% growth projected from 2025 to 2035. Computer programmers are a separate and much smaller group, about 110,800 jobs, with 7% fewer projected. BLS says companies are expected to use AI to automate repetitive programming tasks. Separately, Indeed's index of US software development postings was 78.9 on 16 October 2026, about 21% below its February 2020 level. Postings are on one site, and they are not jobs.

**Replacing all developers.** This is a prediction, and none of the sources in this course's research shows a company handing all its software work to AI. Chapter 1.7 shows what companies did say.

The other three cards are explained in the beats that follow.

</GoDeeper>

## Both findings can be true

<MermaidDiagram title="Different groups, different results" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["All US workers"] --> A2["No clear AI effect yet (Yale, Oct 2026)"]
  A --> A3["Denmark: no effect above 2% on earnings or hours"]
  B["Ages 22 to 25 in AI-exposed jobs"] --> B2["19% below trend (Stanford)"]
  B2 --> B3["Mainly fewer hires, not more exits"]
`} legend="Stanford calls its result descriptive, not proof of cause. Denmark covers the first two years after ChatGPT." />

A whole economy is very large, and the effect so far sits in one small group. The diagram shows two groups and what each study found. The Stanford authors say their result shows a pattern, not the cause.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the harm, stated plainly, and its limits">

This is the strongest evidence of harm to people like you, so it is stated without softening. In US data, it is harder for young workers to get hired in some jobs where AI can do many of the tasks.

The Stanford Digital Economy Lab study, revised in August 2026, uses payroll data on millions of US workers through June 2026. Employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed jobs is 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers. Experienced workers in the same jobs show no comparable gap. The gap works mainly through fewer hires, not more people leaving. It is concentrated in jobs where AI substitutes for tasks, and employment is flat or rising where AI complements workers. The same paper finds no evidence of widespread job loss across the economy.

Its limits matter. The authors call the results descriptive, not causal. Earlier versions reported 13% and then 16%, and the latest reports 19%, as the data and method changed. It weakens when education is controlled for, some trends diverged before ChatGPT, and the gap is smaller in national survey data. It is US data, and it is a working paper that has not been peer reviewed.

Other sources point the same way or add context. SignalFire is a venture capital firm. It said in June 2026 that entry-level and new graduate hiring was down, compared with 2019. The fall was roughly 65% at 12 large tech companies and roughly 76% at early-stage startups. That is the firm's own data, "new grad" is not defined, and the causes are its interpretation. The New York Fed reported in the second quarter of 2026 that US recent college graduates had unemployment of about 5.6% and underemployment of 42%, for all majors together. In a spring 2026 survey of 185 US employers, NACE found employers projecting 5.6% more hiring of the Class of 2026, and 11% discussing eliminating positions because of AI.

Some evidence points the other way. Yale's Budget Lab said in October 2026 that the US occupational mix is not yet changing in ways that clearly line up with AI. Its authors expect AI to leave a mark eventually. A Danish study, revised in March 2026, linked surveys to government records. Two years after ChatGPT launched, it ruled out effects on earnings and hours larger than 2%. The International Labour Organization estimated in May 2025 that about one in four workers worldwide are in jobs with some exposure to generative AI. It put 3.3% in the highest category. It judged transformation more likely than replacement, and exposure is not job loss. The World Economic Forum's January 2025 survey of more than 1,000 employers forecast 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030. That is a forecast.

This course found no data on the labour market in Gaza or the wider region. Most of the evidence here is US data.

</GoDeeper>

## Gains depend on the task

<InteractiveCanvas slug="fear-versus-data-gains" />

Studies of AI at work do not agree on one number. On tasks that suit AI, workers often finish more, and faster. In a 2023 study of 758 consultants, AI users did that. On one task outside what the tool handled well, they were less often right. The answer depends on the task.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: four studies and their limits">

**Support agents.** In a study of 5,179 agents at one company, AI assistance raised issues resolved per hour by about 14% on average. For novice and low-skilled workers, the rise was about 34%. The effect was minimal for experienced workers. The data came from before the 2022 tools.

**Consultants.** In a 2023 working paper, 758 consultants at one firm used GPT-4. On 18 tasks inside the tool's strengths, AI users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with over 40% higher quality. Those below average improved 43%, and those above average improved 17%. On one task outside, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to give a correct answer. The researchers designed the tasks, and some authors work with the firm.

**Freelance programmers.** In a 2023 preprint, 95 freelancers recruited on Upwork built a small web server in JavaScript. The group with Copilot finished 55.8% faster, with a 95% interval from 21% to 89%. It was one task, a small sample and a 2022 tool. It does not show that AI makes everyone 55% faster.

**Field experiments.** Across three experiments with 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 firm, an AI assistant brought 26% more completed tasks. Junior developers gained about 27% to 39% and senior developers about 8% to 13%. The experiments did not measure code quality.

DORA's 2025 survey of about 5,000 technology professionals was sponsored by Google Cloud. 90% used AI and over 80% believed it raised their productivity, yet 30% reported little or no trust in AI-generated code. DORA found that AI now improves software delivery speed but still increases instability, and it calls AI an amplifier of a team's existing strengths and weaknesses.

</GoDeeper>

## A number can go out of date

<MermaidDiagram title="One result, two dates" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["July 2025: 16 experienced developers took 19% longer with AI, but believed they were 20% faster"] --> B["Feb 2026: many developers would not work without AI, so newer data is an unreliable signal"]
  B --> C["Now: probably faster, size unknown"]
`} legend="METR is the research group that ran the trial. The 2025 result is marked out of date on its own page." />

Read the date next to every number. The diagram shows one result at two dates. METR, the group that ran the trial, now marks the first result out of date. It thinks developers are probably faster now, but its newer data is too weak to say by how much.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the 19% result does and does not say">

The trial had 16 experienced developers working on 246 issues in mature repositories they knew well. They expected to be 24% faster, and afterwards believed they had been 20% faster, but the measured time was 19% longer. The tools were early-2025 tools. METR says the trial does not show that AI slows most developers, and its page says the result is out of date.

In February 2026, METR wrote that its newer data was an unreliable signal, because many developers refused to work without AI during the study. It believes developers are probably more sped up now, but the data is only very weak evidence for how much. A survey of 349 technical workers gave self-reported value gains of about 1.4 to 2 times, which METR doubts.

Two lessons last. First, never quote the 19% as a current fact. Second, people can misjudge their own speed, so measure your hours instead of trusting how fast a task felt. Chapter 7.2 returns to that.

</GoDeeper>
