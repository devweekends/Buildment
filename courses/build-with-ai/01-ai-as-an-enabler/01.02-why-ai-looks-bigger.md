In chapter 1.1, Rana made her no-AI rule after reading alarming headlines. She never asked what kind of statement each headline was. A claim about AI is usually one of three kinds: a prediction about the future, a company reporting on itself, or a measurement by people outside the company.

## A launch claim meets an outside test

<InteractiveCanvas slug="claim-type-sorter-devin" />

In March 2024, a company launched Devin as "the first AI software engineer". It reported that Devin solved 13.86% of the issues in a test it ran. In January 2025, a team outside the company tried Devin on 20 real tasks. Three worked, 14 failed and 3 were unclear. Both are reports of what someone saw on their own test, and they answer different questions.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the two results differ">

The launch number is a <GlossaryTerm term="vendor" definition="A company that sells the product it is talking about." /> claim. Cognition, the maker of Devin, chose the test, ran it and published the result. It tested Devin without help, while the models it compared with were given the files to edit. Reporting this way is normal. It is still a report by the seller, and nobody outside had checked it.

The outside test came from Answer.AI, a small team that tried Devin on 20 real tasks. It was a practitioner trial, not a controlled study, and the tool has changed since then. The team wrote that social media excitement and company valuations had "minimal relationship to real-world utility".

Neither result supports "Devin is a fake" or "Devin is an engineer". The first is what the maker reported on a test it chose. The second is what one small team saw on its own tasks. Neither tells you how a tool will do on your work, which is the gap chapter 1.4 is about.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort eight claims by kind

<InteractiveCanvas slug="claim-type-sorter" />

Tap a bucket for each card, or drag the card onto it. A wrong try tells you why. Watch the card about 2035: it comes from an official source, but it still looks ahead. A source can be careful and still be making a prediction.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the three kinds, and what each one cannot tell you">

A **prediction** is a guess about the future with a date on it. In March 2025, the head of Anthropic said in a talk that he expected AI to be writing 90 percent of code within three to six months. He added that within twelve months it could write essentially all code. The three-to-six-month window ended by September 2025. The research for this course found no independent measurement showing that AI writes 90 percent of all code. That does not make the speaker dishonest. It means a prediction is not a result.

A **company claim** is a company reporting on itself. In October 2024, Google's CEO said that more than a quarter of new code at Google was generated by AI, then reviewed and accepted by engineers. The sentence includes the human review step, and it does not define "generated". It tells you what the company says it does. It is not a test.

**Vendor research** is a company running tests on a subject close to its product. In July 2025, the security firm Veracode reported that 45% of code samples from more than 100 models failed its security tests. The tests are real, but the firm sells security tools, and the report does not say how many tasks it used. Write it as "the vendor says".

An **independent measurement** is made by people with no stake in the answer, who state their sample. The Stanford payroll study and the Answer.AI trial are examples. Each has limits: the payroll study covers US workers and calls itself descriptive, and the trial had 20 tasks.

An analyst firm's **forecast** is a prediction too. In June 2025, Gartner said that by 2027, half of the organisations that planned large customer service cuts would drop those plans. It based this on a poll of 163 leaders. It is a forecast, not a fact.

</GoDeeper>

## Smooth text feels true

<MermaidDiagram title="Where a habit can interrupt smooth text" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Smooth, confident AI text"] --> B["It feels true"]
  B --> C["We check it less"]
  C --> D["A mistake gets through"]
  E["Open the source first"] -.->|"interrupts the chain"| C
`} legend="Studies of people link each step. Researchers think AI text gets the same benefit, but no study of language models was found for this course." />

Psychology studies of people find three effects. Easy-to-read text is judged more likely to be true. Things that talk like people are treated as if they understand. Automated help is followed even when it is wrong. Researchers think AI answers get the same benefit. The diagram shows where one habit interrupts it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: four old findings behind this beat">

In 1966, Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA, a program that answered with simple templates. He wrote that once such a program is explained, "its magic crumbles away". People still treated it as if it understood.

A 2007 theory paper by Epley, Waytz and Cacioppo says people give human traits to non-human things more often in three cases. Human-like knowledge is easy to apply, they want to understand the thing, or they lack social connection. A 2009 review by Alter and Oppenheimer found, across many experiments, that things that are easy to process are judged more likely to be true. A 2010 review by Parasuraman and Manzey found that people who use automated decision aids both miss problems the aid does not flag and follow wrong suggestions. It happens to experts, training and instructions do not prevent it, and it happens in teams.

These are studies of people and of older machines. The step to language models is an inference by researchers, not a measured result. Chapter 1.4 explains why an AI answer sounds sure even when it lacks facts, and chapter 3.2 returns to smooth explanations and learning.

</GoDeeper>

## Five questions before you believe a claim

<MermaidDiagram title="Five questions for any claim about AI" chart={`flowchart TD
  C["A claim about AI"] --> Q1["1. Prediction or measurement?"]
  Q1 --> Q2["2. Company or independent?"]
  Q2 --> Q3["3. How many, where, when?"]
  Q3 --> Q4["4. What counts as success?"]
  Q4 --> Q5["5. Who gains if you believe it?"]
`} legend="Ask the questions in order. A question you cannot answer is a reason to wait, not a reason to reject the claim." />

These five questions work on any claim about AI, now or in five years. Ask them before you share a claim, fear it or build on it. You rarely get all five answers. A missing answer tells you how far to trust the claim.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the five questions, with one case each">

**1. Prediction or measurement?** The March 2025 statement about 90 percent of code is a prediction. So is the 2035 job projection. Neither can be checked against today.

**2. Company or independent?** Google's figure comes from Google. The Stanford payroll study does not come from an AI company. Independent is not a yes or no: the DORA survey of about 5,000 technology professionals in 2025 was sponsored by Google Cloud. Sponsored does not mean wrong. It means you read the limits.

**3. How many, where, when?** The Answer.AI trial had 20 tasks. The Stanford, BLS, Indeed and Gallup figures are US data. Say "in US data" whenever you repeat them, and note the date, because figures change every few months.

**4. What counts as success?** Devin's 13.86% means issues solved in one chosen test. A report that says "zero return" needs its definition of return. Without a definition, you cannot compare the number with anything.

**5. Who gains if you believe it?** A seller gains if you buy. A speaker gains attention. A critic gains attention too, so this question applies to fear as much as to hype.

These five questions are the course's method, built from the cases in this chapter. They are not a research finding, and they do not tell you whether a claim is true. They tell you how far to trust it.

</GoDeeper>

## Use the questions on the 95% headline

<MermaidDiagram title="One headline, read slowly" chart={`flowchart TD

  A --> B["Success meant a lasting profit effect"]
  B --> C["Authors: directionally accurate, not exact"]
  C --> D["Critic: unsuccessful is not zero return"]

`} legend="From a 2025 report by MIT's NANDA project and a critique of it. The critic is an industry analyst giving an opinion." />

One famous headline, read slowly. The diagram follows it back to what the report measured, then to what its authors and a critic said. Where did the 95% come from, and what did success mean? Ask before you repeat it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two stories that were retold wrong">

Both stories below are disputed. The wording here is the careful version, with dates.

**Builder.ai.** Builder.ai was a startup, valued at $1.5 billion, that sold apps it said AI helped build. It became insolvent in May 2025 after a lender took its cash and its revenue figures were restated. A viral story said it had faked AI with 700 engineers. An engineering newsletter that spoke to former engineers says that is false. About 15 engineers worked on its code-generation product, and outsourced networks of 500 to 1,000 contract engineers also existed. A separate investigation says former staff described AI doing basic tasks while people and outsourced partners did most of the work. The last CEO denied misleading anyone, and the allegations are not court findings. The fair summary is that human work behind an "AI" label was reported, and the "all fake" story was overstated.

**Amazon Just Walk Out.** In April 2024, reports said about 1,000 people in India reviewed shopping videos for Amazon's cashierless stores. Amazon called this untrue. It said human reviewers annotate data, "no different than any other AI system that places a high value on accuracy". Amazon also said it was moving large grocery stores toward its Dash Cart. The original report was behind a paywall, so this course relies on the Verge's account of the dispute.

In both cases, the fast version of the story left out a limit. That is what the five questions are for.

</GoDeeper>

```quiz
question: "Khalil is choosing a note-taking app for his study group. The app's own page says: 'Students who use our app study 40% faster.' The maker sent a survey to 60 customers. Only customers who still pay for the app answered. The page does not say how 'faster' was measured. How should Khalil describe the claim?"
options:
  - "An independent measurement made by the app's own paying customers"
  - "A prediction about how fast students will study with the app"
  - "The maker's own claim, to compare with a measurement made by others"
  - "A result that describes the study habits of students at large"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "The maker sent the survey, so the maker ran it. Customers answering does not make it independent."
  - "The survey reports what customers said already. It does not forecast anything, so it is not a prediction."
  - "Right. The maker ran the survey, only paying customers answered, and the measure is not stated. A measurement by someone without a stake would be stronger evidence."
  - "Only customers who kept paying answered, and nobody knows what was measured. The number cannot speak for students at large."
```
