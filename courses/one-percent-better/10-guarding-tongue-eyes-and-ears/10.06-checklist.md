## Module 10 checklist

- [ ] I use "say good or be silent" (Sahih Muslim 47) as a question before speaking
- [ ] I know that backbiting includes true things a person would dislike (Sahih Muslim 2589)
- [ ] I have one planned sentence to stop backbiting, and I do not pass on tales (Sahih al-Bukhari 6056)
- [ ] I do not mock, use offensive nicknames, or tell crude or double-meaning jokes (Quran 49:11)
- [ ] I replace small convenient lies with the truth (Sahih al-Bukhari 6094)
- [ ] I turn my eyes away after a sudden glance (Sahih Muslim 2159) and leave harmful talk calmly (Quran 28:55)
- [ ] I verify before forwarding and do not pass on everything I hear (Quran 49:6; Sunan Abi Dawud 4992)
- [ ] I respect private messages and photos, and I have limits and filters on my phone
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 10.2 to 10.4, and finished the quiz in 10.5
- [ ] I submitted my plan below

## Learning log

1. **Your lunch table:** Where in your week does the talk most often turn to people who are absent? What sentence will you use?
2. **Small lies:** What small, convenient untruth do you say most often? What is the true version?
3. **What gets in:** Which chat, account or app brings most of the harmful content into your day?
4. **Forwards:** When did you last forward something without checking it?

## Your plan for the three gates

This task turns module 10 into three planned responses, one for each gate, and a few settings for the phone. Planned responses work because the moments come without warning. If you decide now what you will say or do, the decision is already made when the moment arrives.

You may write about your own life, or about the provided case below. Both count exactly the same. Nothing personal is graded.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Ayesha, a teacher, writes: "Tongue: in the staff room, talk often turns to a difficult colleague. My sentence: 'Let's give her the benefit of the doubt; how was your weekend?' Source: Sahih Muslim 2589. Small lies: I often say 'I didn't see your message' when I did. True version: 'I saw it and haven't had time; I'll reply tonight.' Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 6094. Eyes and ears: one parents' group often forwards gossip about teachers. I will mute it and check it once a day for school news only. Source: Quran 28:55. The phone: no forwarding without checking (Quran 49:6; Sunan Abi Dawud 4992); a 30-minute daily limit on short videos (Sahih al-Bukhari 6412); filters on my phone and the home router." Each response is specific to where the test happens in her week.

Look at the shape of her answer. Each gate has one planned response tied to a real place and a real moment, not a general wish. The lie she chose is the one she says most. The chat she mutes is the one that actually brings harm. And the phone settings are things she can do today.

A weak answer would say: "I will guard my tongue, eyes and ears, and use my phone less." It is sincere, but it names no moment, no sentence and no setting.

Remember the warning in chapter 10.1: guarding your own gates is the habit, not policing other people's. Your planned sentences should be gentle and should offer somewhere else to go. A plan that relies on lecturing others usually fails, and often causes new harm.

If you live with children, consider one shared family rule from this module, such as "no forwarding without a source" or "phones charge outside bedrooms". Shared rules are easier to keep than private ones, and children who help enforce them, as Musa does, learn them deeply.

Expect the first use of a planned sentence to feel awkward, as it did for Hamza at the Friday lunch. Most people find that the awkward pause lasts a few seconds, and that someone else at the table is relieved. By the third time, the sentence feels natural, and people around you start to expect it. Some may even start using it themselves.

For the phone, choose settings you will actually keep. A limit that is too strict will be switched off on the first evening. Start with something modest, such as a little less than you use now, and reduce it later. The 1% way applies to screens too. A small limit kept for a month does more than a strict one kept for two days, and you can always tighten it once it feels easy.

**The provided case.** If you prefer not to use your own life, use this one. Junaid is a 19-year-old student. His friends' group chat is full of jokes about a classmate's weight, and Junaid usually sends laughing emojis. He often says he "forgot" assignments when he chose not to do them. He scrolls short videos for about two hours a night, and some of what he sees he regrets. He sometimes forwards political rumours to his family. Write Junaid's plan.

## How your mentor will read it

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tongue | A planned sentence for a real moment, and one small lie replaced | One of the two | Neither |
| Eyes and ears | A specific chat, account or setting changed, and the turn-away habit | A general wish to look away | None |
| The phone | Verify before forwarding, privacy, and at least one limit or filter | One or two of these | None |
| Gentleness | Responses that are calm and offer an alternative | Responses that lecture | Responses that attack others |
| Sources | A source for each response | Some sources | None |

**Pass rule:** your plan passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

Your mentor then replies with one or two short questions. You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate.
