## Learning log

1. **Your exit:** What do you usually do in the first minute after the salam? What pulls you up?
2. **Forgiveness first:** How does it feel to know that the Prophet began by asking forgiveness, even after his prayer?
3. **Mu'adh's dua:** Which of its three requests, to remember, to thank, to worship well, do you most need help with now?
4. **The angels' dua:** How might Sahih al-Bukhari 659 change how you feel about staying seated for three minutes?

## Your after-prayer plan

This task turns module 5 into a habit that grows from one or two prayers to all five. It is the easiest habit in the course to stack, because the cue comes by itself. What a plan adds is the order you will grow it in, and what you will do on the days you cannot stay seated.

You may write about your own prayers, or about the provided case below. Both count exactly the same. Nothing personal is graded.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Asif is a teacher. He prays Fajr and Isha at the mosque near his home and the other three at school. His plan starts with Isha and Fajr in week one, because he is never rushed after them. In week two he adds Maghrib. In week three he adds Dhuhr and Asr at school. His set is the one from chapter 5.2: astaghfirullah three times and "Allahumma antas-salam" (Sahih Muslim 591), the hundred (Sahih Muslim 597), and Mu'adh's dua (Sunan Abi Dawud 1522). His walking version is for Dhuhr, when a class starts ten minutes after the prayer: he says the hundred on the walk to the classroom and Mu'adh's dua at the door. He writes the hundred's last line on a card until he knows it by heart. His intention line: "To remember Allah after His prayer, as the Prophet did."

Look at the shape of Asif's answer. It starts where staying is easy and names the order of the other prayers, week by week. Each part of his set has its source. His walking version names the prayer and the moment it ends. And his card fills the one gap in his memory.

A weak answer would say: "I will do the adhkar after every prayer from now on, inshallah." It is sincere, but it names no first prayers, no order, no set and no plan for the rushed ones. On the first busy day at school, it would quietly stop.

Expect the hundred to feel long for the first week, while you are still counting carefully and checking the card. After a week or two of daily practice, most people find it settles into a calm rhythm of a little under two minutes. If it still feels too long, start with the three istighfar, "Allahumma antas-salam" and Mu'adh's dua, and add the hundred a week later.

If you already say some adhkar after the prayer, start there. Write what you already say, check its source, and plan only what you will add. The habit grows from what you have, not from zero.

There is one more choice to make. Some people prefer to stay in their row after the salam; others move to the side or to the back so that people can pass. Either is fine. What matters is that you have a place where you will sit for three minutes, and that you know where it is before the prayer ends.

**The provided case.** If you prefer not to use your own prayers, use this one. Huma is a 26-year-old software tester who works in an office three days a week and from home two days. She prays all five prayers at home on home days, and at the office prayer room on office days. After every prayer she stands up within seconds. She knows SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah and Allahu akbar, but not the order or the number. After Dhuhr on office days she has a daily stand-up meeting five minutes after the prayer. Write Huma's after-prayer plan.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your plan with the table below. The rubric grades your plan only, never your faith.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| First prayers | One or two prayers where you are not rushed, with the reason | First prayers named, but no reason | All five at once, or none |
| Order of growth | When each remaining prayer joins the habit | "The rest later" | None |
| The set and its sources | The adhkar you will say, each with its source | Adhkar named, some without a source | No adhkar named |
| Walking version | The prayer it is for, and what you will say while walking | A walking version, but no prayer named | None, or a plan to skip rushed prayers |
| Learning aid | A card, or a way to learn what you do not yet know | A wish to learn, no aid | None |

**Pass rule:** your plan passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

Your mentor then replies with one or two short questions. You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate.
