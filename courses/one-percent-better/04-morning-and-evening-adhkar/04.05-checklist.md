## Module 4 checklist

- [ ] I have chosen a starter set of about three sourced adhkar, and I know the source of each
- [ ] I know the meaning of each item in a language I think in
- [ ] I say the same set in the morning and the evening, at two fixed anchors
- [ ] I carry a card, or a bookmark, until the set is learned by heart
- [ ] I have a short minimum for my busiest days
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 4.2 and 4.3, and finished the quiz in 4.4
- [ ] I submitted my adhkar plan below

## Learning log

1. **The long list:** Have you ever started a long list of adhkar and stopped? What ended it? If you prefer, answer about Hamza's book in September.
2. **Meaning:** Which line of the master of seeking forgiveness speaks to you most, and why?
3. **Your moment:** Which moment in your morning needs your body but not your mind? What makes it a good anchor, or a weak one?
4. **The minimum:** Which item will be your minimum, and on what kind of day will you use it?

## Your adhkar plan

This task turns module 4 into a habit you can keep for years. The plan does not need to be long. A small, sound set that you say every morning and evening, at moments you already have, with a card and a minimum, is a habit that grows by itself. In a year it can become ten adhkar you know by heart, with their meanings, said daily.

You may write about your own adhkar, or about the provided case below. Both count exactly the same. Nothing personal is graded, and your mentor will not ask which adhkar you say.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Nadia is an accountant who works from home. Her starter set is three items, each with its source: the master of seeking forgiveness (Sahih al-Bukhari 6306), "Bismillahilladhi" three times (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3388), and "SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi" 33 times, growing to 100 (Sahih Muslim 2692). Her morning anchor is folding her prayer mat after Fajr; she sits for four minutes before she stands. Her evening anchor is the same, after Asr. Her card sits under her prayer mat, Arabic on one side and the meaning on the other. Her minimum is "Bismillahilladhi" three times, for mornings her son wakes early and needs her. Her growing rule: when all three feel easy on most days for two weeks, she will add the words of Sahih Muslim 2723. She also writes one line on why: "To start and end my day with Allah."

Look at the shape of Nadia's answer. Each item has its source, so she knows she is saying something sound. Each anchor is a moment that happens every day in the same place. The card lives at the anchor, so starting takes one step. The minimum names its item and its day, and the growing rule names a time and the next item.

A weak answer would say: "I will say the morning and evening adhkar every day from my book." It is sincere, but it names no items, no anchor, no minimum and no way to grow. It is Hamza's September book.

Before you write, check your sources. Use only adhkar whose source you can name: the collection and the number, and the grading if it is outside the two Sahih collections. The starter set in chapter 4.2 is a good place to begin. If you add something from a booklet or an app, look up its source first, or ask a teacher you trust.

Think, too, about how you will learn to pronounce each item. Arabic letters that do not exist in Urdu or English are easy to say wrongly from a transliteration alone. Ten minutes with a teacher, a family member who reads well, or a trusted recording at home is enough to check your set. Then the card and the daily practice do the rest.

**The provided case.** If you prefer not to use your own adhkar, use this one. Kamal is a 52-year-old taxi driver. He drives from 7:00 to 19:00, with breaks for prayers. He prays Fajr in the mosque near his home and walks back, about five minutes. He knows Ayat al-Kursi and "SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah, Allahu akbar" by heart. He cannot read long texts easily while working, and he does not want to look at a phone while driving. He wants to start the morning and evening adhkar. Write Kamal's adhkar plan.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your plan with the table below. The rubric grades your plan only, never your faith.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The set and its sources | About three items, each with a named source | Items named, but some without a source | No items, or items with no sources at all |
| Meaning | The meaning of each item in a language you think in | Meaning for some items | No meaning |
| Two anchors | A morning and an evening moment that happen every day | One anchor, or a vague time ("in the morning") | No anchor |
| Card and minimum | A card or bookmark at the anchor, and a named minimum | One of the two | Neither |
| Growing rule | When you will add the next item, and which | "More later" | None |

**Pass rule:** your plan passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

Your mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example, they may ask what you will do when you travel, or how you will learn the pronunciation. You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate.
