## Learning log

1. **Your honest week:** If you wrote down your prayers for a few days, as Hamza did, what surprised you? If you prefer, answer about Hamza's week in chapter 2.1.
2. **The cue:** For the prayer you most often join late, what are you usually doing when the adhan comes? What would "stop at the adhan" look like in that moment?
3. **Calm:** How does it change your feeling about lateness to know that the Sunnah says walk, join and complete, rather than run?
4. **The evening loop:** What one thing about your evenings most affects your Fajr?

## Your plan for your hardest prayer

This task brings the module together on one prayer. Everyone has one prayer that slips most. For many people it is Fajr; for others it is Asr at work, or Maghrib on the commute. Your plan shows that you can find the real obstacle, choose a cue that comes early enough, and write the small steps that follow it, with a plan for the late day.

You may write about your own prayer, or about the provided case below. Both count exactly the same. Your mentor will not ask about your prayer record, and nothing personal is graded.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Junaid is a university student. His hardest prayer is Asr, because it falls during his afternoon lab, which runs from 14:00 to 17:00. He writes that the obstacle is not the distance, since the campus mosque is three minutes from the lab, but that he does not want to leave in the middle of an experiment. His cue is the adhan on his phone at about 15:45. His if-then line: "If I hear the adhan for Asr, then I finish the step I am on, tell my lab partner, and walk to the mosque." He adds: "On days I have a step that cannot stop, I pray Asr in the campus mosque straight after the lab, before 17:15, and I ask the teaching assistant next week whether lab breaks can move to 15:45." His late-day line: "If I arrive after the iqamah, I walk in calmly, join and complete." And his intention: "To guard the middle prayer, as Allah asked."

Look at the shape of Junaid's answer. The obstacle is specific, and it is the real one. The cue comes early enough to change the outcome. The steps after the cue are short and named. There is a plan for the hard day, and there is one small request to change his surroundings. The source he names is tied to the prayer he chose.

A weak answer to the same prayer would say: "I will try my best to pray Asr on time in the mosque, inshallah." It is sincere, but it names no obstacle, no cue and no steps. On the first busy lab day it would leave Junaid where he started.

One more thing. If the prayer you choose is often missed altogether, not only joined late, start your plan with the night or the hour before it, not with the prayer itself. The obstacle is usually earlier than the prayer, as Fajr showed.

Remember too that prayer times move through the year. A plan that works in October may need a new leaving time in January, when Maghrib comes earlier, or in June, when Isha comes late. Write your plan for this month, and put a reminder in your calendar to check it on the first day of next month.

**The provided case.** If you prefer not to use your own prayer, use this one. Rashid is 35, an accountant who works in an office twenty minutes from home by bus. He lives with his wife and two children, five minutes from a mosque. He prays Fajr and Isha in the mosque most days. Maghrib is his problem: he leaves the office at 17:30, and in October Maghrib's adhan is at about 17:45, so he is on the bus at the adhan. On most days he prays Maghrib at home at about 18:20, alone and late. His office has a small prayer room where two colleagues pray. Write Rashid's plan for Maghrib.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your plan with the table below. The rubric grades your plan only, never your faith or your prayer record.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The obstacle | The real, specific obstacle for this prayer | An obstacle, but vague ("I am busy") | No obstacle named |
| The cue | A cue early enough to change the outcome, such as the adhan or earlier | A cue that comes too late, such as the iqamah | No cue |
| The steps | Two or more short, named steps after the cue | One step, or steps that are vague | No steps |
| The late day | What you will do when you are late anyway, with calm | A late-day plan that rushes, or skips the prayer | None |
| A source | A sound source tied to this prayer or this habit | A source named but not tied to the plan | None, or only Jami' at-Tirmidhi 241 presented as certain |

**Pass rule:** your plan passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

Your mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example, they may ask what happens when the prayer time moves with the season, or who at work could join you. You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate.
