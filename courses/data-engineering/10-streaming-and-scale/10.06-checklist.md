## Learning log

1. **A request for live:** Think of a time when someone asked you for something live or instant, at work or in study. What did they really need, and how soon?
2. **Lost or repeated:** When a message arrives twice, or does not arrive at all, which is worse for you? Give one real example, such as a payment message or a reminder.
3. **Habit or measurement:** Remember a time when you chose a tool because everyone used it. Would a simpler tool have been enough, and how could you have checked?
4. **Your Mizan pipeline:** Which of your tasks would be unsafe to run twice, and how would you know? If none, say why.

## Your streaming-or-batch decision

A decision note is a short written answer to a request, for a person who has to act on it. It does not say which tool is best. It says what the need is, what each design would cost, and what you chose, with numbers that anyone can check. A manager can read it in two minutes. A colleague can disagree with it, because every claim has a number behind it.

Write one note for one request. Use these parts. First, the request in one sentence, and who will act on the data. Second, how soon that person must act, with a number. Third, the volume: readings per hour and per day. Fourth, the batch option, with its schedule and the worst-case age of the data, which is the schedule interval plus the run time. Fifth, the streaming option, with what it would add and what it would cost to keep running. Sixth, your decision, the number that would change it, and what protects you from lost or repeated messages.

Here is a strong answer about a fictional person. Rawan works at a pharmacy that wants a live view of its vaccine fridge. The pharmacist says the stock spoils if the fridge stays too warm for 30 minutes. Rawan's note says that the person who acts is the pharmacist, who must be alerted within 10 minutes of the temperature going above the limit. One sensor sends a reading every minute, which is 60 an hour and 1,440 a day. A batch every 5 minutes that takes 1 minute gives data that is at most 6 minutes old, inside the 10 minutes, and it runs 288 times a day. Streaming would add a service that runs all day and has to restart after a power cut, and it would not make the pharmacist act sooner. She chooses the batch. She adds that if the pharmacist wanted an alarm within 30 seconds, she would look at shorter batches first and a stream last. She protects against repeats by keying the table on the sensor id and the time of the reading. She also adds a check that 60 readings arrive each hour, so a missing batch is noticed.

Look at what makes it strong. The need comes first, with a number. The arithmetic can be checked: 60 times 24 is 1,440, and 24 times 12 is 288. Both options are described with their costs. The decision names the number that would change it. And the note says what happens when a message is lost or repeated, which is the part that people forget.

Compare it with a weak answer: "Live data is better, and streaming is the modern way, so we should use Kafka." Nobody can check it. It has no need, no number and no cost, and it picks a tool before it asks a question. A reader learns only that the writer likes the tool.

If you prefer not to use your own pipeline, use this provided case. Basel looks after a cooperative that delivers water to 25 rooftop tanks. Each tank has a level sensor that sends a reading every 2 minutes. The manager wants a live screen, and wants the driver called when a tank falls below 25 percent. In this example, a tank at 25 percent lasts about 4 hours at normal use, and the truck needs up to 2 hours to reach any tank. The office computer runs day and night on a battery backup. Write the decision note for Basel. Work out the readings per hour and per day, the worst-case age of the data for a batch you choose, and the number of runs a day. Say what you choose, what would change your mind, and how you protect the table from repeated readings.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your note as a manager would, looking for the number behind each claim. There is no single right design. A note that chooses streaming can pass, if it shows a need that a batch cannot meet. What matters is that the reasons can be checked.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The need | Says who acts, what they do and how soon, with a number | Says who acts and what, with no number for how soon | No need, or only "live" |
| The arithmetic | Readings per hour and per day are right, and the worst-case age of a batch is the interval plus the run time | One of these is right, or a small slip with the method clear | No numbers, or numbers that cannot be checked |
| The comparison | Batch and streaming are both described with a cost for each | Only one option is described in detail | One tool is named with no comparison |
| The decision | A clear choice and the number that would change it | A clear choice with no number | No choice, or "it depends" with nothing more |
| Lost or repeated messages | Says what happens on a crash and how the table stays correct, for example a key and a replace | Mentions repeats or loss, with no fix | Not mentioned |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three of the five parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What would you do if the sensors sent a reading every second?" or "How would you notice that a batch did not run?" Write your answer in plain English, or send a voice note. Short sentences are fine. Your reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
