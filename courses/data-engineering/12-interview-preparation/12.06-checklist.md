## Learning log

1. **The round you fear most:** Which round worries you most, and what is one thing you will do about it this week?
2. **What you heard:** When you listened to a practice answer, or when your friend answered, where did someone say "we" for everything, or give a number with nothing to compare it with?
3. **When the power is out:** How will you practise on a day with no power or no connection? Name one way that needs neither.
4. **What your friend caught:** What did your friend, or a second listen to your recording, show you that you had not noticed?

## Record your mock answer

Reading about an interview is not the same as speaking in one. A recording gives you something a book cannot: you hear what the interviewer would hear. Most people find three things to fix the first time they listen. They start too late, they skip the question stage, or they stop without a clear end. This gate asks you to record a short answer, so that a mentor can listen and give you one or two things to improve before a real interview.

The task is to answer this design question aloud, in three to four minutes, as if in a design round. Yahya runs a print shop. Every evening, his billing program exports a file of the day's jobs and invoices. Each row has the customer's name and phone number, the customer type (a school, a shop or a person), the invoice amount and a status of paid or unpaid. People pay days later, so an invoice's status changes after the first export. Yahya wants a report every Monday that shows the money invoiced and the money still unpaid, by customer type. Design the pipeline. At the end, add one sentence that gives a real number from your own project and says what it counts. If you have no project yet, use a number from the case.

You may record audio only. A voice recorder on a phone works without a connection, and an audio file of four minutes is small enough to upload on a weak line. Do not worry about your accent or your grammar. A mentor listens for content: what you ask, what you decide, and what you name. If you cannot record, write the same answer as text. You might have no quiet room, or no power. Write about 300 words, which is what you can say in two minutes. Put the text in the answer box, and paste a link to your project's README in the link field instead of a recording.

Here is what a strong answer sounds like, told about a fictional student called Mais who answers the same question. She does not draw anything first. She asks who reads the report and when. The answer is Yahya, on Mondays. She asks how many rows there are, and the answer is a few dozen a day. She asks what unpaid means when payments arrive late. She chooses a daily batch and says why: the report is read once a week, so even a slow run is enough. She walks the path in order. The file lands in a folder for its date. Bad rows go to quarantine. One row per invoice is kept, with its latest status. The report is built from that table. Then she names failure modes unprompted. A day may have no file. A rerun must not double the money. A status may change after the export. The names and phones stay in a restricted folder, so the report has only the customer type. She ends with a trade-off, a daily batch against a live feed, and says why she rejected the second. Her last sentence is a number from her own pipeline: 2,712 lines became 2,620 reports.

Here is a weak answer. A student says: "I would use a streaming tool to move the data into a cloud database and make a dashboard, and it would be scalable." The answer names tools and no reasons. It starts with a shape nobody asked for, and the need is a weekly report. It mentions no failure mode, no number and no alternative. It sounds like a design, but nothing in it can be checked, and the interviewer cannot tell whether the student has run anything.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will listen to your recording or read your text with the table below. There is no single right design. A daily batch with a clear reason can pass as easily as a more complex one. What matters is that each part is something the mentor can hear or read.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Questions first | Asks at least two clarifying questions, or states two assumptions, before choosing a design | Asks or assumes one thing | Starts with a design, no question |
| The shape | Names a daily batch, an hourly micro-batch or streaming, and gives a reason tied to a stated need | Names a shape, but gives no reason | No shape named |
| The path | Walks from source to report in order, in four steps or more | Two or three steps, or out of order | A list of tools, not a path |
| Failure modes | Names three or more, such as late data, reruns, backfills, schema change, bad rows, privacy or checks, each with a fix | Names one or two | None, or only when asked |
| Trade-off and a number | Names one alternative that was rejected and why, and gives one real number with what it counts | Gives one of the two | Gives neither |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three of the five parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What would you do if the export for Tuesday never arrived?" or "How do you know the unpaid total is not counted twice?" Write your answer in plain English, or record a short voice note. Short sentences are fine. Your reply to the mentor is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit. When you pass, you have something to repeat in the capstone defence, where you answer questions on the pipeline you build.
