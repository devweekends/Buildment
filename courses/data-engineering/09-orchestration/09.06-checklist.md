## Learning log

1. **Who is the clock?** Think of a job in your own work or study that happens only when you remember it. What would break if you were away for a week?
2. **Order:** Think of a task you do in a fixed order. What goes wrong if you do the steps in another order, and how would you notice?
3. **Retry or stop:** Write one failure from Mizan, or from your own work, that waiting and trying again would fix, and one that it would not.
4. **Missed days:** When you miss a few days of study or work, what do you do to catch up? How is that like a backfill, and what makes it safe to do?

## Your runbook entry

A runbook entry is a short note for the person who is on duty when a run fails. That person may be you at six in the morning, with the power just back and a head teacher or a programme officer waiting for a report. They do not need a history lesson. They need to know what they are looking at, what to check first, what to do and how to be sure it worked. A good entry saves them from guessing, and it stops two people from fixing the same problem in different ways.

Write one entry for one kind of failure. Use these parts. First, the title and the symptom: the exit code and the log line that tell you this failure has happened. Second, the likely causes, sorted into transient and permanent, with the sign that tells them apart. Third, the first thing to check or do. Fourth, how to run the date again safely, with the exact command, and why running it twice does no harm. Fifth, who to tell and what to tell them. Sixth, how to confirm that it is fixed.

Here is a strong answer about a fictional person. Zaid keeps a runbook for his bakery's nightly sales job. His entry for a failed `load` task is titled "Nightly sales job ended with exit code 1: load failed". The symptom is in the last lines of the log: the status failed for load, and skipped for report. His causes are in two groups. A locked file is transient, and the sign is the word "lock" in the detail, together with retry lines before the failed line. A renamed column is permanent, and the sign is one failed line and no retry lines. His first action is to read the first failed line, not the skipped lines. His fix for the lock is to close the program that holds the file and run the same date again with one command. His fix for the column is to tell the owner of the export and run the date again after the owner has fixed it. He notes that the rerun is safe because the load replaces the day instead of adding to it. He says who to tell: the bakery's owner, with one sentence about which day the report will be late. And he says how to confirm: the log shows ok for every task on that date, and the report page shows the date.

Look at what makes it strong. It starts from what the person sees, not from what the author knows. It separates the two kinds of failure and gives the sign for each. It tells the reader where to look first. It gives a command that can be copied, and a reason that the command is safe. It says who to tell, and it ends with a check that the problem is gone.

Compare it with a weak entry: "If the job fails, check the logs and fix it, then rerun." Every word of it is true and none of it helps. It does not say which log line, which fixes exist, which command to rerun or why it is safe. A tired reader at six in the morning learns nothing from it.

If you prefer not to use your own pipeline, use this provided case. Hanan, a teacher who looks after the computers at a small school, built a nightly job. It reads the day's attendance sheet, loads it, checks the number of rows and builds a one-page report for the head teacher, who reads it at 07:00. The school has 14 classes and 412 students, so the sheet normally has 412 rows. One morning the job ended with exit code 1. The log shows that `load` failed after one attempt, with the detail "ValueError: class 7B not found in the class list". The tasks `check` and `report` are skipped. Class 7B was renamed 7C on Sunday, and the sheet still has 31 rows for 7B. Write the runbook entry for this failure. Say which kind of failure it is and how you know, what to do first, what to tell the head teacher, and how to confirm the fix.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your entry as if it was the only thing they had at six in the morning. There is no single right entry. An entry for your own Mizan runner can pass as easily as one for Hanan's job. What matters is that a stranger could follow it.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The symptom | Names the exit code and the log line (task and status) that show this failure | Names one of them, or describes the symptom in general words | No symptom, or only "the job fails" |
| The cause sorting | Names at least one transient and one permanent cause, with the sign that tells them apart | Names the causes but not the sign, or only one kind | No sorting, or every failure is called a retry |
| The first action | One clear first step, such as reading the first failed line, and the fix for each cause | A first step with no fix, or fixes with no order | "Check the logs" with nothing more |
| The safe rerun | The exact command and one sentence on why running it twice is safe | The command, with no reason | No command |
| Telling and confirming | Says who to tell and what to say, and how to confirm the fix from the log or the report | One of the two | Neither |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three of the five parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What would you do if the same error came back after the rerun?" or "How would the head teacher find out that the report is late?" Write your answer in plain English, or send a voice note. Short sentences are fine. Your reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
