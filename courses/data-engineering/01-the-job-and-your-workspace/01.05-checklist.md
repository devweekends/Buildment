## Learning log

1. **A number you had to trust:** Think of a number you used recently, such as a price, a mark or a balance, that you could not check. What would you have wanted to see behind it?
2. **Your own small team:** In your work or study, which of the five roles do you already do, even a little? Which stage would you find hardest to check?
3. **How fresh is enough:** Pick one report you read. How old can its data be before it stops being useful, and how would you find out?

## Explain the role and the pipeline

This task is the first time you put the whole module in your own words. It is also the answer you will give in many interviews and first calls with a client: what do you do, and how would you build this? You will come back to your answer in the last gate of the course and ask what you would explain differently by then.

You may write about a pipeline from your own life or work, or use the provided case. The provided case is Adam. Adam is a freelancer who works remotely. A bakery with two shops hires him to produce daily sales numbers. At closing time each shop's till writes a file of the day's sales. The owner wants a one-page report each morning, so that she can decide how much to bake for the next day.

Your answer has four parts. First, say what a data engineer would do for this bakery, and which parts of the work belong to neighbouring roles. Second, name the stages the sales data passes through, from the till to the page, in order, and say who owns each stage. Third, decide how fresh the numbers must be, give the number in hours, and check a schedule against it with the gap plus the run time. Fourth, describe one wrong number that could appear, the first stage where it would show, and the count that proves it.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional student, Salma, who wrote about the bakery. She begins with the job. Adam builds and looks after the path from the tills to the report and keeps the data correct on the way. The till software belongs to someone else, and Adam cannot change it. On a job this small, Adam also builds the report, so he does the analyst's work as well.

Salma then names four stages: the till files, a copy job that brings them in, a sales table with one row per sale, and the one-page report. She says who checks each one. For freshness she asks the owner when she reads the report and what she decides. The owner reads it at 08:00 and needs yesterday's sales complete. The files arrive by 22:00, and a nightly job takes 20 minutes. Salma works out a worst case of 24 hours plus 20 minutes, which is 24.33 hours, and she says that is enough for a need of 36 hours. She adds that an hourly job would add 23 runs a day and change nothing for the owner.

For the wrong number, Salma picks a shop total that looks double. She would check the report against the table. Then she would count the rows in the table against the rows in the till file, and find a file that was copied twice. She says the fix belongs in the copy job, so that a second run replaces the day.

Now compare a weak answer. It reads: "A data engineer uses Spark and Kafka to process big data in real time and builds dashboards." It lists tools and not stages. It does not say who owns anything. It says "real time" with no question to the reader, and no number of hours. It names no way to find a fault, so a wrong number would leave the person stuck. Each sentence may be true somewhere, but none of them helps the bakery.

Most real answers sit between the two. A middle answer names the stages but gives no owners, or says the owner needs "daily" data without checking a schedule. The mentor would mark that part as Partly, and the usual repair is small: add one name and one number. You do not need perfect English to pass. Short, clear sentences with the right numbers are better than long ones, and a voice note is welcome if speaking is faster than writing for you.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer with the table below. There is no single right pipeline, and a case from a shop, a clinic, a school or a freelance client can pass as easily as the bakery. What matters is that you describe the job, the stages, the freshness and a trace.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The role | Says what the job is and names at least two neighbouring roles and what each does | Describes the job, but names no neighbours | A list of tools only |
| The stages | Names at least four stages in order from the source to the report, with an owner for each | Names three stages, or gives no owners | No stages |
| Freshness | Gives a number of hours, says who needs it, and checks a schedule with the gap plus the run time | Gives a number but does not check a schedule | "Real time" or "as fresh as possible" with no number |
| The trace | Describes one wrong number, the first stage where it shows and the count that proves it | Names a fault but no check | No trace |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three of the four parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What if the till file arrives at midnight instead of 22:00?" or "Which count would catch a file copied twice?" Write your answer in plain English. Short sentences are fine. You may add a voice note link as an extra. Your reply to the mentor is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
