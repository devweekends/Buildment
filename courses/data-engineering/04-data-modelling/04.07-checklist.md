## Defend a grain and a history choice

The task is to design a small star schema on paper and to defend two choices in it: the grain, and the way you keep history. It is the task of a modelling round in an interview. It is also the task of a first meeting with a client who says "I want a report of attendance". The goal is not a perfect design. The goal is a design whose choices you can explain, with a number that checks each one.

You can use your own data or a case from work. If you have none, use Hiba's school, which is described below. Write the grain as one sentence. Name the fact and the dimensions. Say which attributes keep history and which do not, and say which question decides each. Then show what would go wrong with the other choice, using a number from the case.

A useful habit is to draw the design before you write about it. Put the fact table in the middle of a page and the dimensions around it, and write the key on each line. If you cannot draw a line between two tables with a key on it, the design has a gap. A pencil sketch on paper is enough, and it works well when the power is off.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional freelancer. Mazen designs tables for a small language school. He writes the grain first: one row is one lesson that one student attended. He checks it with a count, because the number of rows must equal the number of attendance records in the school's sheet. His fact is the attended lesson, with a length in minutes. His dimensions are the student, the teacher and the date. He gives the student's level type 2, because the school wants to see how many lessons each student attended at each level. He gives a teacher's spelling correction type 1, because the old spelling was never true. And he shows the cost of the wrong choice. With type 1 on the level, every old lesson of a student who moved up would be counted at the new level. The level report would then be wrong for past months.

Look at what makes his answer strong. The grain is one sentence, and it comes with a check. The history choice follows from a question that the school really asks. The mistake in the other choice is not a general warning but a specific result. And he treats the spelling correction and the level change differently, for a reason.

Compare it with a weak answer: "The table has students, classes and dates, and I will keep history of everything." There is no grain, so nobody can say what a row means. "Everything" is not a choice, because a spelling mistake kept as a version creates two names for one person. There is no number, no question, and no mention of what the design cannot answer. The answer may sound careful, and it cannot be tested.

If you prefer to use a provided case, use Hiba's. Hiba teaches at a school with 240 students in 8 classes and about 180 school days in a term. She records attendance once for each student and each day, which makes up to 43,200 rows. In the middle of the term, student S117 moves from class 5A to class 6B on 9 February. Before the move, 22 days of S117 were recorded in 5A. The head teacher asks two questions: how many students were absent in each class on each day, and what was the attendance rate of each class in February.

Write about this case as if you were explaining your design to the head teacher. State the grain. Say what the fact is and what the dimensions are. Say why the class needs type 2, and what would happen to the 22 days of S117 under type 1. Then name one question that your design cannot answer, such as why a student was absent, and say what you would add to answer it.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. There is no single right design. A school, a shop, a clinic or a water round can pass as easily as Hiba's case. What matters is that your grain is checkable and that each history choice follows from a question. The mentor also checks your numbers against the case, so show the arithmetic: 240 students times 180 days is 43,200. A number with its working is easy to check, and a number without it is only a claim.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The grain | One sentence of the form "one row is one ...", with a count that checks it | A grain sentence, but no check | No grain, or a grain that is not true of the table |
| Fact and dimensions | The fact is a measurement, and the dimensions are the context, each with a key | Fact and dimensions named, but keys or measure unclear | Descriptions inside the fact, or no dimensions |
| The history choice | Type 1 or type 2 for each changing attribute, each with the question that decides it | A choice, but the question is vague | "Keep history of everything", or no choice |
| What breaks otherwise | A specific wrong result with a number | A general warning | None |
| A limit | One question the design cannot answer, and what to add | A limit, but no remedy | None |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three of the five parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What is the count that checks your grain?" or "How would the design change if S117 came back to 5A?" Answer in plain English. Short sentences are fine. You may add a voice note link as an extra. Your reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
