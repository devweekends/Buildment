## Explain a plan to a teammate

The task is to explain a query plan to a teammate who writes code but has not read a plan before. The goal is not to impress anyone. A plan is evidence, and your job is to turn the evidence into a decision that someone else can check. In an interview, the same skill appears when you are asked why a query is slow. At work, it appears whenever a client asks why a page takes so long.

Choose one query that is slow or suspicious. It can be the price history query on `price_log` from chapter 3.5, or a query from your own work. If you have no query of your own, use Tamer's case, which is described below. Write for your teammate in plain English, with short sentences. Say what the query asks and what the plan says before any change. Say why the plan is slow or fast and what you changed. Then say what the plan says after, and give one limit of your fix.

A good way to prepare is to say your explanation aloud once before you write it. If you can say it in under a minute to a friend, the written version will be short and clear. If you get stuck while talking, the stuck point is usually a part you have not fully understood yet, and that is the part to check in the plan. This also works well when the power is on for only an hour: talk it through on paper first, then type it when you have a screen.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional freelancer. Ruba builds an order screen for a bakery. The orders table has 60,000 rows, and the screen finds one order by its number. Ruba writes that the query asks for one order, and that the plan before says `SCAN orders`, so the database reads all 60,000 rows to find one. She creates an index on the order number, and the plan after says `SEARCH orders USING INDEX`. She then times the query three times before and three times after. The times change from run to run, so she compares the slowest before with the slowest after. Her limit is honest: each new order must also update the index, which is fine for the 40 orders a day that the bakery takes. She also says she would not add an index on the status column, because most orders share the same status.

Look at what makes her answer strong. The query is named and its question is one sentence. The plan is quoted, and the words SCAN and SEARCH are explained with a number. The fix comes with proof: a second plan and a timing. And the limit is real, with a reason, and not a general warning. A teammate could repeat every step.

Compare it with a weak answer: "I added an index and now it is faster." There is no query, so nobody knows what was slow. There is no plan, so the claim "faster" cannot be checked. There is no number and no limit. The answer may even be true, and it still teaches the teammate nothing. A weak answer fails because it cannot be tested, not because it is short.

If you prefer to use a provided case, use Tamer's. Tamer is a freelancer who looks after a school's attendance sheet in SQLite. The table `attendance` has 90,000 rows: 300 students over 300 school days. His screen counts the absent students of class 7 on one day, and it is slow.

```text
SELECT count(*) FROM attendance
WHERE class_id = 7 AND day = '2026-03-10' AND status = 'absent';

Plan before:  SCAN attendance
Rows with class_id = 7 and that day: 30

CREATE INDEX idx_attendance_class_day ON attendance (class_id, day);

Plan after:   SEARCH attendance USING INDEX idx_attendance_class_day (class_id=? AND day=?)
```

Write about this case as if you were explaining it to Tamer's colleague. Say what the first plan means with the numbers 90,000 and 30. Say why the index lists `class_id` and `day` and not `status`. And name one limit or risk of your own, for example what you would check before the school adds more columns to the table.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. There is no single right query. A slow lookup, a slow filter or a query on your own project can pass as easily as Tamer's. What matters is that a teammate could follow your reasoning and repeat your check. The mentor reads for evidence and not for length. A short answer with a plan, a number and a limit can pass easily, and a long answer that never quotes the plan cannot.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The query and its question | Named in one sentence, with the table and the filter | Named, but the filter or the table is missing | No query, or only "a slow query" |
| The plan before | Quotes SCAN or SEARCH and says what it means for the rows read | Quotes the plan but does not explain it | No plan |
| The reason, with a number | Gives one number from the case and links it to the plan | A reason, but no number | No reason |
| The change and the proof | Names the change and quotes the plan after | Names the change but not the new plan | "It is faster" with nothing else |
| One honest limit | A limit with a reason, such as write cost or column order | A general warning | No limit |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three of the five parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What would the plan say if the filter had only the day?" or "How would you know the index was worth its cost?" Answer in plain English. Short sentences are fine. You may add a voice note link as an extra. Your reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
