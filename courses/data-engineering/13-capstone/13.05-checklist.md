## Learning log

1. **The hardest bug:** Which failure took you longest to find in the capstone? What did the run log, a test or the plan show that finally pointed to the cause?
2. **A trade-off you chose:** Name one decision, such as a full rebuild or an incremental model, the tolerance of a check, or the field that makes your key. What did you give up?
3. **A hundred times the data:** What would you measure first, and what would you change last? Or answer for Ziad's water round below.
4. **How you work now:** What do you do before you write code that you did not do in module 1?

## Your data engineering toolkit

Write one line for each skill, in your own words, with a place in your capstone where you used it. The lines below show the list. Replace each with your own sentence.

- SQL: joins that keep the row count, NULL rules, windows for the latest row per key and week over week
- Modelling: the grain stated first, facts and dimensions, and type 2 history
- Storage: Parquet files, partitions chosen by the queries you expect, lake and warehouse
- Safe loads: reruns that give the same result, by replacing a partition or merging on a key
- Late data and duplicates: lookback windows, business keys, and resends told apart from real repeats
- Change: contracts, quarantine, schema changes and backfills
- Tests and checks: dbt tests, unit tests, and freshness, volume and schema checks
- Orchestration: tasks and dependencies, retries with a growing wait, and a run log
- Streams and clusters as ideas: logs, offsets, delivery guarantees, and measuring before adding machines
- Privacy and working in the open: minimisation, keys with a secret, zones, retention, Git, a README and a runbook

## Check your capstone against the ten must-haves

The task for this gate is to mark your own project before your mentor does. Go through the ten must-haves from chapter 13.1 one at a time. For each, write Met, Partly or Not yet and one sentence of evidence: a test name, a command, a number or a file. Honest marking is part of the grade. A project with two honest Partly marks is stronger than a project with ten Met marks and no evidence.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about Hala and her aid distribution project. She marks eight must-haves as Met and two as Partly. For the one-command run she writes that the demo command ran from an empty folder in 2 minutes 50 seconds. For idempotency she writes that the rerun test compares a hash of the whole fact table, not only the count. She marks the type 2 dimension as Partly and says why: the facts join the point's current area, and she lists the fix, which is to join on the date. She marks the runbook as Partly because she has not yet followed the backfill entry in a clean folder. Each mark has evidence and each Partly has a next step.

Compare it with a weak answer: "All ten are Met. Everything works and all tests pass." There is no evidence, so the mentor cannot tell whether the writer checked. A passing test suite is also not proof of every must-have. The privacy test cannot see a name hidden in a longer text, and the course tests do not check that your backfill works or that your runbook is correct.

If you cannot run a capstone, for example because your laptop is unavailable, mark Ziad's project instead. Ziad built the water delivery round you met in chapters 13.2 and 13.3, with 5 routes and 14 days of data. His one command runs from a clean folder in 3 minutes 10 seconds. 16 tests pass: the 15 course tests and one of his own, which shows that a delivery at 23:40 lands on the right day. His README states the grain, "one row is one delivery stop", and `stop_id` is unique. The route dimension has two versions for route R03, with valid_from, valid_to and is_current, but the facts join the current version. Running the last day twice leaves the fact table unchanged, but he never ran a backfill. His fact count equals the generator's count, and the new field is NULL before day 11 and filled after. No personal column is in the shared zone, but one customer phone number appears in a line of `logs/runs.jsonl`. The report is one 6 KB page. The README has all five sections. The runbook has all five headings, but the entry for deleting old personal data says only "run retention", and he never ran it.

## Look back at gate 1.5

In gate 1.5 you explained the role of a data engineer and a pipeline, for Adam's bakery or for a case of your own. Open that answer. Write three sentences: what you got right, what you would explain differently now, and one word you used then that you can now define exactly.

A strong look back, told about Hala, looks like this: "In 1.5 I wrote that a pipeline moves data from A to B. Now I would say it moves data to a place where people can trust the numbers. The hard parts are reruns, late rows and who may see names. I used the word 'clean' and I can now define it. It means rows that pass written rules, with the rest kept in quarantine." A weak look back says "I know more now." It names nothing, so it shows no learning.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your marks next to your repository and your recording. The mentor does not simply accept the marks. The mentor checks a sample against the evidence you wrote, and checks that your look back says something specific.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Your ten marks | All ten marked, each with one sentence of evidence that a mentor can find | Ten marked, but at least three have no evidence | Fewer than ten marked |
| The project itself | At least eight must-haves are Met in the mentor's check, and none is Not yet | Seven Met, or one Not yet | Fewer than seven Met, or two or more Not yet |
| Your honesty | Every mark matches the mentor's check, or is one level higher or lower with a reason | One mark is two levels too high | More than one mark is two levels too high |
| The look back | Three sentences: right, different, and one defined word | Two of the three | Generic, such as "I know more now" |
| The toolkit | Ten lines, each with a place in your capstone | Ten lines, but fewer than five name a place | Fewer than ten lines |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three of the five parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two questions, often the ones from your defence: the grain, a rerun, privacy or a hundred times the data. Answer in writing or in a voice note, in plain sentences. Your reply is part of the gate, so look for it after you submit.
