## Learning log

1. **A rerun you made:** Think of a time you ran something twice, a script, a form or a payment, because you were not sure it had worked. What happened, and what would have made the second run harmless?
2. **The step that worried you most:** Which step of your ingestion did you find hardest to make safe to rerun: landing, publishing, the registry extract or the log? Say why.
3. **A rule you did not expect:** Which rule in this module surprised you, such as writing the file before the watermark, keeping the secret out of every file, or the code that changed when a phone number changed?
4. **A question you would ask a source owner:** If you could ask Karam or Yasmin to change one thing in the registry to make your job safer, what would it be?

## Your rerun explanation

A pipeline is safe to rerun when you can show it, not when you say so. This task asks you to explain, in plain English and with numbers, why your ingestion can be run twice. The numbers are the evidence. A reader should be able to see what you ran, how many rows each run gave and why the second run left everything as it was.

You can use your own pipeline from chapters 5.5 and 6.5 or the provided case. For your own pipeline, run the landing job and the publish step twice for 2 March. Count the rows and add up the prices after each run. Then run the registry extract twice. For the provided case, use Karim's data below.

Here is the provided case. Karim looks after the data for a small pharmacy. Each evening the pharmacy system exports a file for the day, with one line for each item sold. The export for 4 May has 180 lines, and their prices add up to 4,320.50 shekels. Karim's first job added the lines of each export to a table called `sales`. On 4 May the power failed at 22:10 and he ran it again, and the table then held 360 lines for that day and 8,641.00 shekels. He rewrote the job so that it replaces the partition for the date it is given. He also copies the pharmacy's customer list each night with a watermark on `updated_at`. Once a month a customer asks to be removed, and the pharmacy deletes the row.

Do the two runs before you write a word. Copy the counts into a small table with the headings run one and run two, and only then write the sentences around them. If your numbers differ between the runs, you have found a real problem, and that is a better result for this gate than a tidy explanation of a job that is not safe. Write what you found and what you changed.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional freelancer. Osama loads a courier company's parcel list. His job writes the folder for the date it is given. It uses a temporary file that it swaps in, so a power cut leaves the old file or the new one. He ran it twice for 12 June and counted both times: 220 rows and a total of 3,904.00 shekels, and one file in the folder. He shows the two numbers side by side. He also says what happens at each point of failure. If the job stops before the swap, the old file is still there. If it stops after, the day is done and a rerun writes the same file again. He saves the watermark of the courier's customer list after he writes the file, and he explains that a crash between the two steps repeats some rows and loses none. Last, he names a gap. The courier deletes customers, a watermark cannot see a delete, and he plans to ask for a flag with a date and an updated time instead of a deletion.

Look at what makes his answer strong. The evidence is a pair of numbers from two runs, and it fits in two lines. He goes through the failure points one by one, so the reader sees that he thought about the power cut and not only about the second run. He names the order of file and watermark. And he states a limit and a plan, which shows that he understands the method and does not only use it.

A weak answer is "My job is safe to rerun because I only run it once, and I added a comment at the top." It gives no evidence. It treats idempotent as if it meant run once, which is the wrong way round. A comment is not a test, and a rule that depends on a person remembering is the rule that failed in chapter 6.1. A mentor cannot tell whether the job would survive a power cut.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your explanation with the table below. There is no single right design. Replace, merge and a watermark can all pass, if the evidence and the reasons fit together. A claim with no numbers cannot pass.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The rule | Names the write mode of each step (replace, merge or watermark) and why it fits | Names the mode for some steps, or names it without a reason | No write mode named, or "I run it once" |
| The evidence | Rows and total after run one and after run two, side by side | Counts for one run only, or counts without a total | No numbers |
| Failure points | Says what the data looks like if the job stops before and after its last step | Mentions a power cut without walking through it | Not mentioned |
| The known gap | Names one thing the method cannot see, such as a delete, and a plan | Names a gap with no plan | No gap named |
| Personal data | Says where the name and phone stop, and where the secret lives | Mentions only one of the two | Not mentioned |

**Pass rule:** your explanation passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least four of the five parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example, the mentor may ask: "What would your job do if the power failed after the watermark was saved and before the file was written?" Or: "How would you test the delete gap?" Write your answer in plain English. Short sentences are fine. You may add a voice note link as an extra. Your reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
