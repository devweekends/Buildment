## Three quiet days

On Wednesday 11 March, Omar writes to Hala. Market M05 has shown nothing in the weekly numbers since Monday. The other seven markets have reports for every day. He asks whether M05 has stopped selling food. Omar is the programme officer who reads the report, and he is not technical. All the people in this module are fictional, and the data is synthetic.

Hala asks Yasmin, the volunteer coordinator. Yasmin phones the volunteer at M05 and hears the answer. The mobile connection at that market has been down since Monday. The volunteer has kept recording prices every day. The phone form stores each report and will upload them all when the connection returns. Nothing is lost. It is only late.

From the volunteer's side, nothing looks wrong. On Monday the form shows a message that the report is saved and will be sent later. The volunteer records the flour price, the rice price and the other ten items, as on every other day. In the evening the power comes on for a few hours, and the phone charges. The reports wait inside it, each with the time it was made.

This is how the form was built, and Karam, who built it, chose it on purpose. A volunteer in a market with a weak connection should still record every price. So the form saves a report on the phone and sends it later. The price of this design is that the pipeline sees a report only when it arrives, and that can be days after the report was made.

## The morning of 12 March

On Thursday morning the connection comes back. The phone sends everything it kept. The upload folder for 12 March holds 137 lines, more than any other day, and 37 of them come from M05. They are reports dated 9, 10 and 11 March. In all, they hold 35 different reports. Two of the 37 lines are resends of a report that was already in the folder.

Hala's pipeline runs every morning. In its first version, the fact table reads only the reports dated on the newest day in the data, and replaces those rows. That seemed natural: each day brings the reports of that day. On 12 March the newest day is 12 March. Think about what the pipeline does with 35 reports dated 9 to 11 March.

## What Hala finds

Hala checks the fact table on 12 March. For M05, the dates 9, 10 and 11 March have no rows at all. The reports are in the lake, in the folder for 12 March, and the fact table has no way to take them in. The week looks thin for M05, and no test fails, because nothing in the table is wrong. The rows that should be there are simply not there.

At the end of the month she checks every market. The raw files hold 2,620 good reports, but the fact table holds 2,412. That is 208 missing reports, about 8 percent. The M05 outage is only the largest cause. In this data, 211 of the 2,633 reports arrive after the day they were made, 1 to 3 days late. Reports from every market arrive late for small reasons: a phone that was off, a tunnel, a volunteer who uploads on the way home.

Hala thinks about three ways to react. She could ignore late reports and accept the loss. She could wait a week before publishing any number. Or she could make each run read a few days back, so that late reports are merged in when they arrive. The first loses data. The second makes the report slow for every market, including those that upload on time. The third costs some extra work on every run, and the next chapter measures that cost.

The loss reaches the weekly report. With the late reports missing, 7 of the 38 market and week rows of the weekly mart disappear. A day only counts when all 12 items are present, and a late report breaks that. Another 29 rows show a different number. Only 2 rows are unchanged. These counts come from running the reference pipeline with a window of one day. Every figure on Omar's page is a little wrong, in different ways.

## Why late data is hard

A bug that raises an error is easy to find. Late data raises nothing. The pipeline runs, the tests pass, the report is published, and it is wrong in a way that only a person who knows the markets might notice. Omar noticed because M05 was quiet. If one market had sent a single late report each day, nobody would notice for months.

A published number can also change. On Wednesday the week for M05 looks thin, and on Thursday it grows. Omar asks when he can trust Monday's figure. The honest answer is a rule and not a guess: once the window has passed, a day is final. Hala will need to say that rule to Omar in plain words.

There are two things to decide. The first is how long the pipeline waits for late reports, and how much extra work it does to find them. The second is what the pipeline does when something arrives later than it expected. Both decisions need numbers from the data, and both have a cost. Chapter 8.2 uses the numbers of this month to make them.

Late data comes with companions. The same phone that sends late may also send twice, when it is not sure the first upload worked. In this data, 79 lines are repeats of a report that was already sent, 3.0 percent of the 2,633 reports. Some days a volunteer prices an item twice on purpose, and those two reports are both real. And on 15 March the form gains a new field. A pipeline that handles late data but not these three will still give wrong numbers.

## Why this matters for the job

Pipelines in real organisations receive data from phones, partners and old systems, and all of them are late or repeated sometimes. Interview guides describe a pipeline design round. The interviewer expects you to ask about late data, repeated runs, backfills and failures before you draw the design. Rounds and order vary by employer, so treat the guides as indicative. What does not vary is the daily work: when a number looks wrong, the cause is often a record that arrived late, twice or in a new shape.

Hala sees the same pattern in her freelance work. A client with an online shop receives orders that are edited, refunded and sent twice by a payment tool. A clinic's booking system sends visits that were typed at the desk two days after they happened. The settings change and the shape does not. Data arrives late, twice or in a new form, and the pipeline has to keep its numbers right.

## What you build in this module

By the end of this module, the dbt project from module 7 handles all four problems. A lookback window re-reads the last few days on every run, so late reports are merged in. A business key removes resends and keeps real repeats. A written contract accepts a new optional field and stops a batch that loses a required one. Rows that break a rule go to a quarantine folder with the reason. A backfill command reruns a range of past days, and running it twice changes nothing.

You prove each of these with tests. One test runs the pipeline one upload day at a time and checks that the result equals one big run. Another sets the window to zero and shows that M05's reports are lost. Those tests are the evidence that a pipeline survives its data.
