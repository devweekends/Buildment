## Explain a double-counting bug and its fix

This task asks you to explain a common kind of quiet mistake in reporting: a number that is too big because a join repeated rows. If you can explain one clearly, to a person who does not write SQL, you can find the next one. It is also a question that comes up in SQL interviews, where the interviewer cares more about how you check than about the exact query.

You may write about a bug from your own work or study, or use the provided case. The provided case is Salma. Salma works at the front desk of a clinic. Her monthly report says that the clinic had 160 appointments last month, and the booking system says 80. Each appointment has a fee of 50 shekels, so the fee total should be 4,000, and the report shows 8,000. Last week Salma added the doctor's name to the report by joining the appointments to a doctors table. That table has one row for each doctor and each site where the doctor works. There are 5 doctors, and every doctor works at two sites, so the table has 10 rows.

Your answer has four parts. First, explain what went wrong. Use the rule for the rows of a join: say what one row of the second table stands for, and work out the row count. Second, describe a fix and say why it works. Third, show how you would check the fix with numbers, before and after. Fourth, name one thing you would test so that the mistake cannot come back unnoticed.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional student, Bilal, who wrote about Salma's case. She starts with the table. One row of the doctors table is a doctor at a site, not a doctor. Each doctor has two rows, so each appointment matches two rows, and 80 x 2 is 160. The fee total is wrong in the same way, 80 x 50 = 4,000 doubled to 8,000. She notes that `COUNT(DISTINCT appointment_id)` would give 80 and would look like a fix. But it would leave the fee total at 8,000, so it only covers up the problem.

Bilal then gives a fix in two named steps. The first step, `doctor_names`, reduces the doctors table to one row for each doctor. The second joins the appointments to that step. She checks it with a count: the joined result must have 80 rows, the same as the appointments, and the fee total must be 4,000. Last, she suggests a test: after every join, the number of rows must equal the number of appointments, and the test fails when it does not.

Now compare a weak answer. It reads: "I added DISTINCT and the count became 80, so it is fixed." It gives no cause, because it never says what a row of the doctors table stands for. It checks one number and ignores the fee total, which is still 8,000. It gives no row counts before and after. A weak answer may be honest, but it would leave Salma with a wrong fee report, and it would repeat the mistake on the next join.

Most real answers sit in the middle. A middle answer names the join as the cause and gives a fix that works. But it shows no count, or it says "check it" without any numbers. The mentor would mark the check as Partly. The repair is small: add the two row counts and the two totals.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer with the table below. There is no single right fix, and a case from a shop, a school or a freelance client can pass as easily as Salma's. What matters is that you find the cause with a row count, fix it at the join, and prove the fix with numbers.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The cause | Says what one row of the second table stands for and shows with a row count that the join multiplied rows | Names the join as the cause but gives no counts | No cause, or blames a person or the data |
| The fix | A fix that returns one row for each appointment, and says why it works | A fix that corrects the count but not every total, such as DISTINCT alone | No fix, or a fix made by hand |
| The check | Compares row counts or totals before and after, with numbers | Says to check the result but gives no numbers | No check |
| The explanation | Plain words and short steps in order, so a person who does not write SQL can follow | Right ideas, but hard to follow | Too unclear to judge |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three of the four parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example, the mentor may ask: "What happens to an appointment whose doctor has no row in the doctors table?" Or: "How would you test this so that it cannot come back?" Write your answer in plain English. Short sentences are fine. You may add a voice note link as an extra. Your reply to the mentor is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
