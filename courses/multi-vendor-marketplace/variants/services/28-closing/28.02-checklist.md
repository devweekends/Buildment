# Five-minute demo and capstone defense

This gate is an hour of speaking, and it is graded on what you can say without notes. The demo takes five minutes and shows four things in this order: the same appointment in two time zones, one payment split across two providers, twenty requests against one slot with one winner, and a cancellation and reschedule that move money and reminders correctly. Then the defense begins, and the questions are about your decisions rather than your features.

Rehearse the demo twice before the real one. The first run finds the parts that take longer than you thought. The second run is the recording, if your mentor wants a video. A demo performed for the first time in front of a reviewer is a debugging session with an audience, and you already know how that feels.

The script lives in `docs/DEMO.md` from lesson 28.1. Follow it exactly, including the timing, and update the file afterwards with anything that turned out to be wrong. A demo script nobody corrected after a dry run is a script nobody used.

## The demo is a five minute argument

The demo is not a tour. Each moment answers one question that a skeptical mentor would ask, and the sentence you say at each moment names the question. If you find yourself explaining the navigation, you have lost a minute that the defense will need.

The first moment is the provider view: weekly hours written in Gaza local time, and one blocked afternoon because of a power cut. The sentence is that the provider never types UTC and never sees it. The second moment is the Berlin client looking at the same tutor, with the Berlin label first and the Gaza label beside it. The sentence is that one stored instant produces both labels, and that changing the client zone changes only the label.

The third moment is the expensive one. Put a tutor and a designer in one cart, pay once with a test card, and show one order per provider and one booking per slot. The sentence is that the client paid once and each provider received their own commercial obligation with the price and policy frozen at booking time. Keep the payment confirmation screens visible for a second before navigating away, because that is the moment a reviewer is most likely to doubt.

The fourth moment is the terminal. Run the race script and read the tally out loud: twenty attempts, one `201`, nineteen `409`, zero other. Then show one line of the saved output file, so the reviewer sees that the run was recorded rather than typed. If the demo terminal is small, print only the five summary lines; the full JSON can be shown on request.

The fifth and sixth moments are the change operations. Reschedule one booking and show that the old start is offered again and that the reminder time moved. Then cancel the other booking inside the late window and show the partial refund on the order share, with the arithmetic in minor units. These two are the proof that the calendar and the money are both managed, not just the first booking.

```text
0:00 provider hours in Gaza time, one blocked afternoon
0:45 client in Berlin, both labels on one card
1:45 tutor plus designer in one cart, one card payment, two orders
2:45 race script: 20 attempts, one 201, nineteen 409
3:30 reschedule: old slot offered again, reminder moved
4:20 cancel inside the late window: partial refund on the order
```

Those six lines are the whole demo. Say them in that order, and stop talking at 4:20 long enough to let the refund appear. A demo that ends with thirty seconds of silence while a page loads looks calm. A demo that ends with a narrator filling the silence looks uncertain.

## Recover when the live system fails

Something will fail. The API may be asleep on a free host, the test card may require authentication on a new device, or the worker may be down and the reminder will not move. Decide the recovery behaviour now, so that the failure costs ten seconds rather than the demo.

If the API does not answer within a few seconds, say so and switch to the run you recorded in `docs/EVIDENCE.md`. Read the command and the tally, then continue. That is honest and it still answers the reviewer's question. Do not spend two minutes restarting a service while the reviewer waits in silence.

If the payment needs an extra authentication step, complete it and name it: the card flow required an additional check, and the hold is still live until its deadline. This is a chance to show that you understand the hold, not a problem to hide. If a reminder does not move, do not narrate the worker as if it worked; open the job log and show that the old job is cancelled and the new one is queued, or say plainly that the move is implemented and the worker is down in this environment.

A calm, specific recovery is itself evidence. A learner who says "the worker is down, here is the cancelled job and here is the replacement, and the run log from yesterday shows both" demonstrates more than a learner whose happy path happened to work that afternoon.

## The defense

The defense has eight questions. Each answer should take thirty to sixty seconds, start with what the user experiences, then name the mechanism. Rehearse them out loud, not in your head, because the first sentence is the part that fails under pressure. Use your own field names; a general answer that could belong to any booking product will not pass.

**Why store a UTC instant plus an IANA zone, and not a local time with an offset?** A passing answer separates the repeating rule from the dated promise, explains that an offset is a fact about one moment rather than a place, and names a failure that follows from treating an offset as permanent. Mention the weekly rule stored as local fields with the provider zone, the booking stored as `startAt` in UTC, and the two zone snapshots kept for explanation.

**Why one claim per 15-minute block instead of an overlap query before insert?** A passing answer describes the read-then-write race in one sentence, then explains that the unique index on `{ providerId, blockStartAt }` makes the database decide rather than the application. Name the block count for a 45-minute service with a 15-minute buffer, and state why a unique index on the booking start alone would allow an overlapping booking at 15:30.

**What happens when a payment succeeds after the hold expired and someone else took the time?** A passing answer refuses the simple options: you cannot confirm without claims, and you cannot delete the new client's claim. Describe the reconciliation path, the re-claim attempt per line, and the refund for the line that cannot be restored, with the amount taken from the booking snapshot rather than today's price.

**Why is a booking its own collection when an order line could be embedded?** A passing answer points at the lifecycles. A line inside an order is a receipt. A booking has its own states, its own claims, its own reschedule history and its own cancellation, and a provider's agenda reads it directly without loading the payment. Mention that the order share still holds the money side, so embedding the booking would mix two lifecycles in one document.

**How do you refund one booking from a payment that covered two providers?** A passing answer starts with the payment intent and the per-booking charged amount, then names a `Refund` record with its own idempotency key and the partial amount in minor units. Explain that the other provider's share is untouched, that the order shows retained and refunded amounts, and that the refund call happens outside the database transaction.

**If Redis is down or stale, can a double booking happen?** A passing answer says no, and names the reason: the cache only decides what is displayed, and checkout re-validates and attempts the claim insert regardless. Add the cost of a stale cache, which is a 409 to a client who saw a green card, and the fallback behaviour when Redis does not answer.

**A provider moves from Gaza to Doha and changes their zone. What happens to next week's rules and to existing bookings?** A passing answer splits the two. Future rule occurrences are re-resolved in the new zone, so the unbooked openings move with the provider. Existing bookings keep their stored `startAt` and their zone snapshots, and only their labels change. Say which screen warns the provider before the change, and what a client sees afterwards.

**How do you stop one client from holding every free hour of a popular tutor?** A passing answer names the active hold cap per client, the rate limit on new checkout attempts, and the short hold window. Then it explains the distinction between a new attempt and a retry of an existing checkout ID, because a cap that also blocks recovery would strand a client after a lost response.

An answer that drifts into a feature tour is a weak answer. If you cannot name the field, the index or the record that makes the claim true, say what you would inspect and continue. "I would check whether the claim insert or the state predicate failed, and here is the log line" is a better answer than a confident sentence that turns out to be wrong.

## What this gate does not certify

A finished demo and eight good answers do not make the SkillSlot variant ready for learners. Readiness needs the whole roadmap path authored, a runnable reference build that matches the lessons, a technical validation pass on every command in the course, and instructor review of the resolved text. This gate certifies one learner's project at one point in time, on one environment.

Say that plainly if a reviewer asks whether the variant is ready. The honest answer is that this module is complete, the evidence is committed, and readiness is a separate decision with separate evidence. Claiming more would put the next learner in a course whose commands nobody has run.

## After the viva

Keep the repository as it is for a week before changing anything. Interviewers and mentors may open it without warning, and a half-finished refactor in the default branch costs you the demonstration you just recorded. If you must continue, work on a branch and leave the demonstrated commit tagged.

Rotate the Stripe test keys and the database password once the demo is over, because a recording is a public artifact and a screen share can leak more than you notice. Keep the evidence file; the run logs stay valid after the keys change, and they are what a reviewer reads when the live URL is asleep or the free tier has paused.

Then start the next project with the same shape: a rule, a stored instant, a claim, a state machine, evidence and a script. That shape is what you learned here, and it transfers to any product where two people can ask for the same thing at the same time.
