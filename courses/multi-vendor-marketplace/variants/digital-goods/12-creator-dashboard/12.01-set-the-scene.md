# The creator's back office

Nour sells a font family and two CV templates on Midad. It is 21:40, the power has already dropped twice today, and her phone has twenty minutes of battery left. Three things need her. A draft ebook cannot publish because it has no license option. A font carries a personal price she changed on paper and never changed in the system. A template advertises a commercial license she has decided to stop offering. She does not want to browse her own catalogue. She wants one screen that answers a single question: what needs me now.

Module 11 gave her routes: create a product, read it, change it, publish it, and the isolation tests that stop another creator from touching it. A set of routes is not a workspace. A creator with twelve products cannot hold twelve identifiers in her head, and she should not have to. This module builds the workspace on top of those routes: a product table grouped by status, a price column that tells the truth about a product sold under more than one license, and a license editor that adds, edits and removes options in one form.

The surface itself is small: one page, one form, three actions. The difficulty is not the number of screens. It is deciding what each screen may read. A creator dashboard is the first place in Midad where a logged in person sees a list built by a query, and a list built by the wrong query looks completely normal on screen. Nobody notices the extra rows until a creator sees a product that belongs to someone else.

## Three statuses, one lifecycle

Fix the vocabulary before the components. A Product is in one of three states: `draft`, `published` or `archived`. A draft is work in progress. It may be missing a description, a preview or a license option, and it is invisible to buyers. A published product appears in the public catalogue with its options and can be bought. An archived product is withdrawn: it leaves the catalogue, keeps its record, and stops taking new sales.

The interesting question is what each status allows a creator to change. A draft is open: she can rewrite the title, add a second license option, change a price and fix a typo in the description. A published product still needs edits, because prices move and terms get clarified, but every edit has to respect the rules that made it publishable in the first place. An archived product is closed. It is kept for the buyers and orders that point at it, and it can be restored to draft when the creator decides to sell it again.

<StateMachine title="Which product statuses accept a creator's edit" chart={`stateDiagram-v2
  [*] --> draft: create product
  draft --> draft: edit, add or remove an option
  draft --> published: publish rules pass
  published --> published: edit title or option price
  published --> archived: archive
  archived --> draft: restore for editing
  archived --> archived: edit refused
`} />

Read the diagram from left to right and notice that only two states write. Draft and published both accept changes, which is why the update route cannot check a single status. Archived refuses everything until a restore moves the product back to draft. That refusal is the reason a creator who archived the wrong product can recover it, while a buyer's receipt still finds a document that exists.

Why not offer delete? A Product is evidence. Module 18 stores an OrderLine that names the product title, the option key, the license type and the price at the moment of sale, and a License later points into that line. If Nour deletes a product, a buyer's library loses the record that explains what was bought and under which terms. The alternative is a soft delete flag on every query in the codebase, which is the same archive with a worse name. Archive and restore are the honest pair.

## A product is not sold at one price

Nour's font has a personal option at 1,200 minor units and a commercial option at 4,800. A table cell that prints `1,200` is wrong twice. It hides the commercial offer, so she cannot check the number a business buyer will pay, and it invites her to edit the wrong option when a customer complains. The row shows every option: personal at 1,200, commercial at 4,800, each with its seat limit and download limit beside the price.

The public card is allowed to say "from 1,200". A buyer scanning a grid needs one number to decide whether to open the product, and the full comparison belongs on the product page in Module 15. That is not the same truth as the dashboard row, and the two should not share a serializer. One answers "can I afford to look", the other answers "what am I charging for each contract".

```predict
prompt: "Nour archives a published font. A buyer who paid for it last month opens her library and asks for a fresh download link. What should happen?"
options:
  - id: refused
    label: "The link is refused because the product is archived, and the library entry should disappear with it"
  - id: served
    label: "The link is issued because her license is still active, and archive only stops new sales"
correctOptionId: served
explanation: "Archive is a catalogue decision, not a refund. Existing licenses keep their rights, and only a verified refund or an expired license may stop new download grants. If archiving silently revoked access, every creator would be afraid to tidy their catalogue."
allowRetry: true
```

Keep that distinction in mind for the rest of the course. Access is granted by a paid order line and withdrawn by a refund. Everything else, including archive, publication and price changes, edits the offer, not the entitlement.

## Every read starts at the session

The dashboard route takes no identifiers. It resolves the signed in creator, then the Studio that creator owns, then the products of that Studio. The same rule holds for the table, the counts and the form. A route that accepts a `studioId` query parameter and compares it to the session Studio is safe in the narrow sense that it refuses a mismatch, and it is still a worse design, because the next person to copy the route will forget the comparison.

Client side filtering is worse again. A page that fetches every product and hides the rest in React has already sent the data. The browser holds it, the network panel shows it, and any buyer with a stale tab can read it. The list that arrives in the browser must already be the caller's list, and the count above each tab must come from the same scoped query.

The dashboard also needs a rule about what it must never carry. Rows show the title, category, format, status, options, the last update time and whether a released private file exists. They do not show storage identifiers, checksums, license keys or buyer names. Module 13 adds previews and private deliverables, and the dashboard will show that a deliverable is present without showing where it lives. Module 21 adds a sales page, and that page gets its own route with its own scope rather than widening this one.

## What the screen must survive

Three conditions decide whether the dashboard is usable rather than merely correct. The first is a weak connection. The table is one request, not one request per row, and the response stays small enough to load on a phone with expensive data. A creator who opens the page on a bad line should see the table or a clear failure, never twelve spinners.

The second is an interrupted edit. The form saves a draft product, so a power cut between two fields costs typing, not a lost listing. Publication is a separate, deliberate action with its own checks, and it is the only moment where the product makes a promise to a buyer. Keeping the two moments apart is what lets Nour build a listing across an evening of outages.

The third is an error the creator can act on. A refused save must name the field and the reason: a price that is not a positive integer, a duplicate option key, a missing personal seat limit, an option that a paid order already references. A generic "something went wrong" on a form teaches the creator to guess, and guessing on a pricing form is how a font ends up on sale for twelve minor units.

This module does not build uploads, buyer orders or sales totals. Module 13 adds previews and the private deliverable. Module 21 adds the sales page. What this module builds is the surface a creator opens every day, and the next lesson builds it: the table first, then the license editor, then the actions that move a product between statuses.
