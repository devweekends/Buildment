# Comparing licences before buying

Amina designs CVs for small businesses. She has a client meeting at ten, and she needs a template she can adapt for four different clients over the next month. She finds a promising one on Midad on her phone, 360 CSS pixels wide, with 600 megabytes of data left this month. The page tells her the template costs 4,800. What it does not tell her is whether 4,800 lets her use it for paying clients, how many people may work on it, and how many times she can download it.

That gap is the whole lesson. A price is the easiest fact to show and the least useful one on its own. The buyer's real question has four parts: may I use it the way I plan, how many people does it cover, how many downloads do I get, and what does it cost. Each part maps to a field the catalogue already stores, and the product page has to show all four before anybody presses a button.

## Four questions, four fields

The first question is allowed use, and it is the one that decides the purchase. Midad sells two license types in the core path. A personal license covers one person for non-commercial use: a portfolio, a personal site, a study project. A commercial license covers paid work, including work delivered to a client, for the number of named seats the option allows. The terms text says this in plain sentences, and it is a sample for the course, not legal advice.

The second question is seats. A personal option has exactly one seat, because a personal license is one person. A commercial option can carry several, and the seats are named, so the buyer knows whether a colleague may also open the files. The third question is downloads, and the option stores a limit. In Module 21 that limit becomes a count of issued download links, and the buyer should learn that reading here rather than after a failed attempt.

The fourth question is price in minor units with its currency. It is the only one of the four that a grid can answer honestly, which is why the grid shows a "from" amount and nothing else. The product page is where the other three arrive, and the comparison has to make them readable together rather than scattering them through a description.

## A table that survives a phone

The comparison has two columns of data and five rows: allowed use, seats, downloads, price, and the terms version with a link to the text. On a laptop that is a small table. At 360 CSS pixels it is wider than the screen, and a table that scrolls sideways inside a phone page is a table nobody finishes reading.

The layout therefore changes shape, not content. Above roughly 640 pixels, the comparison is a table with one column per option. Below that, each option becomes a block: the license type as a heading, then one labelled row per fact, in the same order for every option. The buyer reads one option, then the other, and the labels repeat so nothing depends on remembering a column header.

The label is what makes the stacked layout work. A block that shows `3`, `5` and `4,800` without the words seats and downloads is a puzzle. The words add nothing to the request and they are the whole interface at 360 pixels.

One option is still a comparison. A product that offers a single personal option keeps the same rows, because the buyer still needs to read what the license allows and how many downloads it carries. Removing the block whenever there is nothing to compare would delete the facts that decide the purchase, and in the seeded catalogue a single option is the common case rather than the edge case.

The grid amount and the page amount are different numbers on purpose. A card says "from 1,200" and the page lists 1,200 and 4,800. A buyer who feels misled by that jump has usually been shown a bare amount with no word in front of it. The word "from" is doing real work, and the comparison below it is where the second number stops being a surprise.

## What the page must not do

Four patterns look reasonable and all four mislead. The first is a single buy button above the comparison, which invites a purchase before the buyer knows what the purchase allows. The second is a preselected option, because whatever default you choose becomes the license most people buy, and the cheap default is the one that causes disputes.

The third is a footnote. Allowed use is not a note at the bottom of the page; it is the first row of the comparison, in the same type size as the price. The fourth is a hidden limit: a download count that appears only after the first attempt has failed. A buyer who reads "5 downloads" before paying has been told the truth, and the library in Module 21 can then say "3 of 5 used" without surprising anyone.

The page also has a weight budget. Amina is on a metered connection, so the product page loads one JSON document, renders the text first, and lets the preview images arrive after it. Alt text comes from the server with each preview, so the gallery is usable while the images are still missing. A gallery that loads six full photographs before the price appears is a page that never finishes on a weak line.
