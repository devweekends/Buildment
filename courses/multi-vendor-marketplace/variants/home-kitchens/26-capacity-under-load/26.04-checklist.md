# Capacity evidence gate

Capacity is the part of Sufra that a reviewer should be able to check without trusting you. This gate asks for three pieces of evidence from the deployed app, one planted defect that the reconciliation job found by itself, and a short write-up that says what the numbers mean. A video of a busy window is not evidence. A log with timestamps, status codes, claim IDs and a final counter is.

Start by naming the release. The API revision, the worker revision and the date of the run belong at the top of `docs/capacity-evidence.md`, together with the live URL. A number that cannot be tied to a revision cannot be reproduced, and a result you cannot reproduce is a story.

## The load run

The first evidence set is the parallel checkout run from 26.03 against a window with ten units. The log must show every request with its own line, the status, the code, the units granted, the attempt identity and the counter after the response. Exactly five of the twenty requests should succeed for a two-unit dish, fifteen should receive `WINDOW_FULL`, and the granted units should total ten.

Three assertions matter more than the table. The final `remainingUnits` is zero. No sample taken during the run is negative. And the sum of granted units equals the capacity, not the requested total. If you paste only the final counter you have shown a number; if you paste the samples you have shown that no request passed through a negative state on the way there.

Run the mixed burst too, with three-unit and two-unit items, and show the leftover unit if your run produced one. Then show that the leftover is unusable: one more falafel request receives a conflict while the counter reads one, and browse reports no orderable dish for that window. That pair of facts is the clearest proof that capacity and orderability are different questions.

## The cutoff race

The second evidence set is the clock test. At one second before the dish cutoff, the checkout succeeds and the claim's `expiresAt` is at or before the cutoff. At exactly the cutoff, the checkout fails with the cutoff code, no claim is written and the counter is unchanged. The third line is the one that shows you understood the boundary: take the claim created at the first instant, advance the clock, run expiry, and show the units returning exactly once, then show that a verified payment arriving afterwards reaches the refund path rather than a placed order.

If your run produced a different result, keep it. A failed race that you diagnosed and fixed is stronger evidence than a clean one you cannot explain, and this gate also asks for the failing output of the hold-expiry test from 26.02. The failure where a retried checkout pushes a hold fifteen minutes past its first deadline is the defect this module removes; show it, then show the same test passing.

## The reconciliation report

The third evidence set is the job. Show a report over a day of windows with the counts of findings by action, and show the planted defect. Add two units to a scratch window's counter by hand, run the job in dry-run mode, paste the finding, then run it in repair mode and paste the audit row with the before and after values. That sequence proves three things at once: the scan reads claims rather than trusting the counter, the repair is deliberate, and the job records what it did.

Also show the guarded-write test. The repair must not overwrite a claim that lands between the read and the write, and the failing output from 26.03 is the evidence that you tested it rather than assuming it. If your first implementation used an absolute `$set`, say so and show the test that caught it.

The report should end with no unexplained findings for the windows you touched. That is a specific claim: every remaining finding is either a released claim with a reason, a held claim already queued for expiry, or an escalation you can describe. "The job reported nothing" is weaker, because a broken scan also reports nothing; the planted defect is what separates the two.

## The write-up

The write-up is short and answers four questions in plain sentences. What did the load run prove about the claim filter? What did the cutoff race prove about the boundary in both directions? What did the reconciliation job find, and what did it refuse to fix without a person? What would you watch during a real Friday lunch, and which number would tell you first that something was drifting?

Answer the last question with a metric, not a feeling. The oldest overdue held claim, the count of windows with findings, and the number of escalations are all observable. A dashboard that shows only successful checkouts will look healthy while the window slowly fills with units that nobody can use.

## Gate checklist

- [ ] `docs/capacity-evidence.md` names the live URL, the API and worker revisions, and the dates of each run.
- [ ] Twenty-request load log from the deployed app, one line per request, with status, code, units, attempt ID and counter after each response.
- [ ] Exactly the units that fit were granted, the final counter is zero, and no sample during the run was negative.
- [ ] Mixed burst with three-unit and two-unit dishes, including the leftover unit and the conflict that proves nothing could use it.
- [ ] Browse shows no orderable dish for a window whose counter is one, when every dish costs two or more.
- [ ] Cutoff run: success at one second before the boundary, with `expiresAt` at or before the cutoff.
- [ ] Cutoff run: refusal at exactly the boundary, no claim written, counter unchanged.
- [ ] Expiry after the boundary restores units once, and a later verified payment reaches the refund path, not a placed order.
- [ ] Reconciliation report over at least one day of windows, with finding counts by action.
- [ ] One planted drift on a scratch window detected in dry-run mode and repaired with an audit row.
- [ ] Guarded-write test showing that a claim landing after the scan is not erased by the repair.
- [ ] Hold-expiry test: the failing output from before the fix and the passing output after it.
- [ ] The write-up answers all four questions and names the metric you would watch during a rush.
- [ ] No counter on a window used by real checkouts was edited by hand; the only hand edit is the planted drift on the scratch window.

## Learning log

1. In your load run, which request was accepted last, and what did the counter read immediately before and after it?
2. Which direction of drift is more dangerous for Sufra, and which one is harder to notice from a phone?
3. What did your repair refuse to change, and where is that refusal recorded?
4. A held claim is four minutes past its deadline and the worker is down. What does a diner see, what does the counter say, and what does the reconciliation job do about it?
5. Why does the cutoff race run on a controlled clock in a non-production environment, and what would break if the override were public?
6. If Friday lunch fills every window in the `Asia/Gaza` kitchens, which two numbers tell you the system is healthy rather than merely busy?

## What this gate does not prove

It does not measure throughput. Twenty requests and a ten-unit window are enough to expose a race, not enough to size a server. It does not prove behaviour across two API instances, because Sufra runs one, and a second instance would need the same claim filter and a shared pub/sub before any of this evidence transferred.

It also does not prove payments under load. The load run stops before the card step on purpose, so it never depends on the payment provider's availability. The payment boundary was proven in M20 with a single attempt, and the late-payment branch is proven here with a fake clock rather than a real bank.

Leave the gate open if any of the three evidence sets is missing, if the reconciliation report has an unexplained finding, or if the cutoff refusal at the boundary depends on a manual step. The closing module asks you to defend these decisions without notes, and a number you cannot explain under questioning is worse than a gap you can name.
