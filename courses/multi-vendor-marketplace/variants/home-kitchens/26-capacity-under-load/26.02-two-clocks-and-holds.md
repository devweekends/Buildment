# Payment time, cutoff time and hold expiry

Lina's thumb hits confirm at 11:09:58. The maqluba cutoff for Umm Yousef's 13:00 window is 11:10:00. Her bank takes a few seconds to approve, Stripe records the charge at 11:10:01, and the signed event reaches Sufra at 11:10:06. Meanwhile the hold she created at 11:09:20 expires at 11:10:00, exactly because her dish closed then. Two true facts now disagree: the diner acted before the cutoff, and the evidence arrived after it.

Every capacity system meets this case, and most get it wrong in one of two directions. A team that honours the late payment quietly breaks the kitchen's promise. A team that refuses without explaining breaks the diner's trust. Sufra's answer depends on separating two clocks that look like one: the kitchen's cutoff and the payment network's timing.

## The cutoff is kitchen physics

A dish cutoff is the last instant at which accepting an order still leaves time to cook and deliver it. For maqluba it is the window start minus ninety minutes of preparation minus a twenty-minute travel buffer, which for a 13:00 window lands at 11:10. For falafel it is 12:00, because thirty minutes of preparation leaves plenty of room and the general window cutoff binds instead. Both numbers come from data: `Dish.prepMinutes`, `Kitchen.travelBufferMinutes`, and `DeliveryWindow.cutoffAt`, computed in the kitchen's IANA zone such as `Asia/Gaza` and stored as UTC instants.

Nothing about that arithmetic involves money. The cutoff would exist if Sufra took cash at the door. It is the point after which a yes becomes a lie, because the food will not be ready when the diner arrives. That is why M14 tests it on a fake clock and why M19 re-checks it inside the claim rather than trusting the cart.

## Payment time is a network event

The second clock belongs to other people. A card authorisation passes through a bank, sometimes a second factor, and always a network. Stripe records an instant, then sends `payment_intent.succeeded` to your webhook. That event can arrive in two seconds or in two minutes, can be retried, and can arrive twice. Your handler verifies the signature and applies the event idempotently, which M20 already built.

The important distinction is between the charge instant and the evidence instant. The charge instant is what the provider says happened. The evidence instant is when Sufra learned about it. A production system has both, and they are not interchangeable. A charge instant is a third-party claim that can be missing for some payment methods; an evidence instant is a local fact that says what Sufra could have known at that moment.

## The claim is the promise, the charge is the evidence

Sufra stores the promise in the `CapacityClaim`: an `id`, an `attemptId`, a `units` count, a `state` of `held`, `confirmed` or `released`, and an `expiresAt` instant. The confirmation path only moves a claim from `held` to `confirmed`. If a release won the race, the confirmation matches nothing, and M20's reconciliation moves the attempt to `refund_required` with a full refund.

That rule is deliberately unfriendly to the diner in the rare case, and it exists because the alternative is worse. A unit restored to a window can be claimed by the next diner within a second. Reviving Lina's claim would take that unit back from someone who has also paid, or overbook the kitchen. Sufra cannot honour both, so it honours the promise it made and returns the money for the one it could not.

Notice what Sufra does not store: a field called "paid before cutoff." There is no such state, because the system cannot observe it reliably. What it stores is whether the claim was still held when the verified event was applied. That is observable, idempotent and testable.

## Decide the late-payment case

The case is precise: the hold was created before the cutoff, and the payment evidence lands after the hold's expiry. Four policies are credible, and only one survives the constraints.

The first is to extend the hold past the cutoff until payment settles. It keeps every diner happy who eventually pays, and it costs the kitchen its schedule: a unit stays out of circulation after the moment it became useless, and if the diner never pays, that unit was wasted for nothing. It also lets the counter describe a possibility rather than a plan, which is the drift this module is trying to remove.

The second is to honour the payment if units happen to be free at that moment. This is the near-miss that looks generous. It silently creates a second capacity decision that Lina never reviewed, possibly with a different price or a later window, and it can take the last unit from a diner whose checkout is in flight. It also turns the cutoff into advice.

The third is to honour any payment whose charge instant is before the cutoff, whatever the claim state. It sounds fair and it is the hardest to implement, because honouring requires capacity that may already be promised. Doing it properly means reserving past the cutoff, which is the first policy with extra steps. It also makes the promise depend on a timestamp supplied by a third party, which is available for card payments and not always for others.

The fourth is no extension. The claim's state at the moment the verified event is applied decides. A released claim produces a refund with a reason the diner can read. This is Sufra's choice, and it matches M20's rule instead of quietly replacing it. Its cost is real: a small number of diners who meant to pay get their money back and must order again. Its benefit is that no cook is ever asked to cook food for a time that has passed.

If the verified event had been applied at 11:09:59, the same trace would end with `confirmed` and a placed order, because the claim was still held. That is the whole race: not a decision about fairness, but a conditional update that one writer wins.

## Shrink the gap instead of bending the deadline

The right response to a rare unfair outcome is to make it rarer, not to move the boundary. Three levers reduce the time between charge and evidence without changing any promise.

The first is a payment timer that is shorter than the time remaining to the cutoff. M19 already takes the minimum of the checkout duration and the earliest dish cutoff, so a diner who starts fifteen minutes before the cutoff gets fifteen minutes only when the cutoff is farther away than that. Near the boundary the cutoff binds, which is why the tail exists at all: a hold created at 11:09 lives for forty seconds, not fifteen minutes.

The second is webhook latency. Put the API and the database in the same region, keep the handler's work small, and let a worker do anything slow. A handler that also sends a notification before answering adds seconds to every event and widens the tail for everyone.

The third is a reconciliation poll for attempts that are still `awaiting_payment` but whose intent reports success. Every thirty seconds, retrieve intents in that state and apply the same idempotent confirmation. This catches a lost event rather than waiting for a retry that may be minutes away. It does not change the claim rule: the poll applies the same conditional confirmation, and it loses the same race when the claim is already released.

What you must not do is widen the hold to cover your own latency. That converts an operational problem into a business rule, and the cook pays for it.

## Bound every hold by the window it serves

A hold is useful only while the kitchen can still use the unit. That gives one rule with four terms. For each kitchen group, the expiry is the earliest of four instants: the checkout timer measured from the attempt's creation, the earliest effective dish cutoff in that group, the window's general cutoff, and the window start. For a cart with two kitchens, the attempt's expiry is the minimum across groups, because the purchase is all or nothing. The last two terms are redundant when every dish cutoff was computed correctly, and they are the guard that catches a window edited after generation or a cutoff stored by an older formula.

Store the result on each claim as `expiresAt`, together with `createdAt` and a short policy string such as `expiresAtRule: "min(timer, dishCutoff, windowCutoff, windowStart):v1"`. The policy string is not decoration. When you change the formula in six months, the stored value tells you which rule produced it, and a reconciliation scan can find claims that predate the change.

Three invariants follow, and each one has a test. First, a claim's `expiresAt` is never later than the effective cutoff of the dish it contains, and never later than the window start. Second, `expiresAt` never moves after creation: a retried checkout returns the same deadline, and a reconnecting phone does not renew anything. Third, `expiresAt` is strictly after the creation instant, or the attempt is refused with the cutoff code rather than a payment form.

The boundary is strict at both ends. A claim is created only when the server time is before the cutoff, so an order at exactly the cutoff is refused. A claim is expired when the server time is at or past `expiresAt`, so a release at exactly that instant is valid.

The schema fields below are the shape to add, not a full model. The claims array already exists from M19; these fields extend it.

```js
// DeliveryWindow.claims[] entry, extended in this module
{
  id: String,
  attemptId: ObjectId,
  units: Number,                 // immutable after creation
  state: 'held' | 'confirmed' | 'released',
  createdAt: Date,
  expiresAt: Date,               // min(timer, dish cutoff, window cutoff, start)
  expiresAtRule: String,         // policy version that produced expiresAt
  releasedReason: String | null  // 'EXPIRED' | 'CHECKOUT_ABORTED' | 'ORDER_REJECTED'
}
```

M19 already takes the minimum of the checkout timer and the dish cutoffs when it creates a hold. This module closes the two gaps that remain. The value is not treated as final afterwards, so a retried checkout can recompute it from the new request time and push a hold past the cutoff. And the claim does not record which rule produced the value, so nobody can tell an old hold from a new one after the formula changes.

Run the test below against your M19 implementation before you change anything. If it passes, your retry path already reuses the stored claim and the fields above are the remaining work. If it fails, you have found the gap that makes a held unit outlive its window.

```js
// test: a retried checkout cannot move a claim expiry
test('a retried checkout keeps the original expiry', async () => {
  const dishId = falafelId; // cutoff 12:00, so the 15 minute timer binds
  const args = { windowId, dishId, dinerId, key: 'checkout-attempt-1', timerMinutes: 15 };
  const first = await startCheckout({ ...args, now: '11:09:20' });
  const second = await startCheckout({ ...args, now: '11:20:00' });
  assert.equal(second.claims[0].expiresAt.toISOString(),
               first.claims[0].expiresAt.toISOString());
  assert.equal(second.claims[0].expiresAtRule, first.claims[0].expiresAtRule);
});
```

A recomputing implementation fails with a number that is exactly one timer late, which is the signature of the bug. The phone that retried did not buy another fifteen minutes; it asked about the same purchase.

```text
FAIL a retried checkout keeps the original expiry
  expected 2026-02-06T11:24:20.000Z, received 2026-02-06T11:35:00.000Z
  the retry recomputed the deadline from the new request time
```

That failure is worth reading carefully. The second request did not extend the deadline by a second because of clock skew; it moved it by the full timer, because it recomputed from the retry time and ignored the claim that already existed. Fixing it means the creation path writes the deadline once and the retry path reads the stored claim, plus the fields above so the rule is auditable.

## Use the canvas to separate the two reasons

The window fill canvas from M14 models one window with a general cutoff, two dishes and a capacity counter. This time, use it to answer a question about reasons rather than about timing: when an order is refused, was it closed or full?

Start with the four default arrivals. The maqluba at 11:05 takes three units, the second maqluba at 11:31 is refused by its 11:10 cutoff, the falafel at 11:31 takes the remaining two, and the falafel at 11:45 finds no capacity. Move the accepted falafel from 11:31 to exactly 12:00 and it is refused at the boundary, so its two units stay in the counter and the last falafel now fits. That single move shows both rules at once: the boundary is strict, and a unit left behind can still be enough for a smaller dish.

Now raise the capacity slider to six and keep the same four arrivals. The maqluba at 11:31 is still refused while three units are free, which is the signature of a time refusal rather than a capacity refusal. The two falafels take four units, and the window ends with one unit that no dish can use. That is the scene's arithmetic in the model: a counter can read one while the window is effectively full.

Its limits matter here. The model has no payment, no holds, no concurrency and no server restarts, and it does not convert an IANA zone. Those are precisely the parts this module tests on the deployed app, which is why the canvas is a thinking aid and not evidence.

## The decision to carry forward

The rule to write in `docs/capacity-evidence.md` is short: a claim is honoured only while it is held, a released claim is refunded rather than revived, and no hold may extend past the cutoff of the earliest dish it contains. Everything in the next lesson tests whether the code and the database agree with that sentence under parallel checkouts, a restart and a clock you control.

```quiz
type: mcq

question: A hold was created at 11:09:20 for a dish with an 11:10 cutoff. The verified payment event arrives at 11:10:06 and the claim is already released. What does Sufra do?

  - Re-claim the units if the window still has any free
  - Confirm the claim because the charge happened at 11:10:01
  - Refund the charge and keep the order unplaced
  - Extend the expiry and wait for a second event
correct: 2
explanations:
  - A second claim is a new capacity decision the diner never reviewed.
  - The claim state decides, not the timestamp on the charge.
  - The promise ended at the cutoff, so the money returns with a reason.
  - Rewriting a deadline would let a released unit be reserved twice.
```

The two questions test the same rule from different directions: one about a released claim, one about the arithmetic. If you can answer both without looking, the next lesson's failing tests will read as familiar rather than surprising.
