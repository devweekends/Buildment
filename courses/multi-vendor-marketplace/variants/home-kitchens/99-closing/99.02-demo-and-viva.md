# Five-minute demo and capstone defense

The demo is the product seen from outside. The defense is the product seen from inside. A reviewer who watches only the video learns that Sufra works; a reviewer who asks questions learns whether you built it or followed steps. This gate needs both, and it needs the second one without notes.

Record the demo from the deployed app, not from localhost. Use two devices, or two browser windows if that is all you have, but keep the diner and the cook separate so the screen shows two people rather than two tabs of the same session. Put the recording link in the README above the stack section, alongside the live URL, and set the video to unlisted rather than private so a mentor can open it without an account.

## What the recording must contain

Five beats, in this order, about one minute each. Keep the whole recording between four and six minutes. A longer demo usually means you are explaining the code, which belongs in the defense.

The first beat is the window. Open today's 13:00 delivery window in `Asia/Gaza` and show a slow dish already past its cutoff while fast dishes in the same window remain orderable. Say the arithmetic out loud: the maqluba closed at 11:10 because ninety minutes of preparation and a twenty-minute travel buffer sit between that instant and 13:00, and spare capacity does not reopen it. This beat proves that the cutoff is data, not a client timer.

The second beat is the split purchase. Add a dish from two different kitchens, pay once with a Stripe test card, and show one payment producing two kitchen orders, each with its own frozen dish name, price and preparation time. Say why the snapshot exists: a cook who edits a price tomorrow must not change a receipt from today.

The third beat is the two cooks. The first cook accepts the order. The second cook does nothing, and the order auto-rejects at its `acceptBy` instant, with a partial refund that you show in the Stripe test dashboard. This is the beat that separates a demo from a status badge, because it shows a deadline enforced by a job and money moving without a person.

The fourth beat is the weak connection. The first cook moves the order to cooking, you switch the diner's phone to airplane mode for about thirty seconds, the cook marks the order ready and then out for delivery, and the phone comes back. The timeline catches up in order with no duplicate rows, and the connection label tells the truth while it is offline. This is the hero feature, and it should be the calmest part of the recording.

The fifth beat is capacity. Run the parallel checkout script against a freshly seeded window and show the counter filling exactly to capacity, with clean conflicts for the requests that arrived too late. Read the line that says how many succeeded and point at the final counter. Do not narrate the script's code; the evidence file covers that.

Do not cut the recording to hide a failure, and do not record over a beat that went wrong without saying what happened. A visible reconnect or a slow first job is more convincing than a polished sequence that never shows the system under stress. If a beat fails completely, stop the take and start again, and keep the failed take out of the repository.

## Answer without notes

The defense is seven questions answered in thirty to sixty seconds each. No notes, no slide, no reading from the README. The shape of a strong answer is always the same: what the diner or cook experiences, then what the server does, then the record that proves it. If you cannot answer, say what you know and name the file or log you would open. That is an engineering answer; a confident wrong one is not.

Have a mentor ask the questions in a live call, or record the answers in one unbroken take and note which format you used. The gate accepts either, but it does not accept written answers, because writing gives you time that a viva does not.

## The seven questions

**Why does a dish have no stock field, and what plays the role of stock?** A strong answer names the dated window as the thing that runs out and the load units as the quantity. It says that a dish has `prepMinutes` and `loadUnits` because one tray of maqluba consumes more kitchen effort than one wrap, and that the same dish can be orderable in one window and closed in another. It finishes by naming the claim as the thing that reduces availability, and the counter as the summary of claims.

**Who decides that an order is too late, and why not the phone?** The server clock decides, because a phone can be minutes off, can be in another time zone, and can be changed by its owner. A strong answer mentions the kitchen's IANA zone, the UTC instants stored in MongoDB, and the injectable clock that makes the boundary testable. It also mentions that the client shows a countdown for courtesy and never for authority.

**Walk through the claim query. What stops two checkouts from taking the last units?** The important words are conditional and single document. The update matches a window whose remaining units are at least the requested units, whose cutoff is still in the future, and then decrements in the same write. Two requests cannot both match a counter that only one of them can decrement. A strong answer adds what happens to the loser: a clean conflict code, no PaymentIntent, no order, and a suggestion to pick another window.

**Why SSE and not WebSockets? When would you switch?** Updates flow one way, from Sufra to the diner, and commands already travel as ordinary authenticated POST requests. SSE is plain HTTP, reconnects by itself, and passes through proxies that would need extra configuration for an upgrade. A strong answer names the switch condition: two-way collaboration such as live chat with a cook, binary frames, or a client that needs to push frequently.

**A Redis pub/sub message is lost. What does the diner see, and why is nothing lost for good?** The diner sees a stale screen until the next event or the next reconnect, because the published message is a notification and not the record. Nothing is lost because the order's timeline is in MongoDB and a snapshot is sent on every connect, and because the polling fallback can fetch events after a known sequence. A strong answer says that the stream is a delivery mechanism and the database is the truth.

**A cook ignores an order. Walk through every system that changes.** The deadline job rejects the order with a system actor, appends one timeline event, releases the capacity claim so the units return to the window once, and requests a partial refund for that kitchen's share. The sibling kitchen keeps its own order, claim and timeline. A strong answer notes that a replay of the job changes nothing, and that the diner sees a rejection with a refund status rather than a cancellation of the whole purchase.

**What happens on the day the clocks change?** The kitchen's zone has a daylight saving transition, so one local label is ambiguous or missing, but every stored instant is UTC and every cutoff is computed with a time zone library from the zone name. A strong answer mentions a test on the transition day, the rule that the server clock decides, and the fact that a job scheduled by delay is a hint rather than the definition of a deadline.

## Two more about the rush

Expect two more questions from M26. The first is the two clocks: what happens when a hold was made before a cutoff and the verified payment lands after it. The answer is that no hold outlives the cutoff of its earliest dish, the claim state at confirmation decides, and a released claim is refunded rather than revived.

The second is the reconciliation job: what it repairs and what it refuses to repair. A repaired counter is one that the claims explain. An overbook is escalated rather than cancelled, because the promises already made belong to diners and cooks. A held claim past its deadline is released through the same path as expiry, never by editing the counter.

## Gate checklist

- [ ] Demo recording link is in the README, unlisted, between four and six minutes, with audible narration.
- [ ] The recording uses the deployed app on two devices or two windows, with no localhost requests visible.
- [ ] Beat 1: a slow dish past its cutoff while fast dishes remain orderable in the same window.
- [ ] Beat 2: one payment, two kitchen orders, with frozen names, prices and preparation times.
- [ ] Beat 3: one cook accepts, one cook ignores, auto-rejection and a partial refund shown in the Stripe test dashboard.
- [ ] Beat 4: the diner goes offline, the cook advances two steps, and the timeline catches up in order with no duplicates.
- [ ] Beat 5: the parallel checkout run fills a window exactly to capacity with clean conflicts.
- [ ] `docs/DEMO.md` matches the recording beat for beat, including the timings.
- [ ] All seven defense questions answered live or in one unbroken recorded take, without notes.
- [ ] Answers name the record that proves each claim, and at least one answer names a limit or a trade-off honestly.
- [ ] `docs/EVIDENCE.md` has no row marked proven without a link, a command or a file behind it.
- [ ] README, `.env.example`, architecture, lifecycle diagram and evidence index are all committed.
- [ ] The live URL still works on a phone at the time of the gate, with the worker running.

6. What would you tell the next fellow to build first, and what would you tell them to skip?

## What this gate does not prove

It does not prove that Sufra is ready for real money, real food safety rules or real support load. The payments are test mode, the notifications are logs, the delivery is status only, and one API instance serves everyone. Saying that in the viva is part of the defense, not a weakness in it.

It also does not close the variant. Other modules in this path are authored and reviewed separately, and this gate speaks only for the deploy, capacity and closing lessons you completed here. A learner who can record this demo and answer these questions has met the bar for the work in front of them; the course creator decides when the whole track is ready for new learners.
