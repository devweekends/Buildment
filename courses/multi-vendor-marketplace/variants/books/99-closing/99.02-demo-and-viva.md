# Five-minute demo and capstone defense

The demo is the product seen from outside. The defense is the product seen from inside. A reviewer who watches only the video learns that Hamesh works. A reviewer who asks questions learns whether you built it or followed steps. This gate needs both, and it needs the second one without notes.

Record from the deployed app, not from localhost. Use two devices, or two browser windows if that is what you have, but keep the student and the shop owner separate so the screen shows two people rather than two tabs of one session. Put the link in the README above the stack section, set the video to unlisted, and check that it plays on a phone.

## What the recording must contain

Five beats in this order, about one minute each, and a total between four and six minutes. A longer demo usually means you are explaining code, which belongs in the defense.

The first beat is the reading list. The student copies the shop published list for a first-year biology course and sets a grade floor of Good. The matcher fills three of four titles from two shops and shows one title unmatched. Point at the reason line for one chosen copy and read it aloud, then point at the unmatched title and say why no copy qualified.

The second beat is the missing title. The student saves the unmatched item as a want entry with a price ceiling. In the other window, a shop owner types that book's ten digit ISBN. Hamesh converts it to thirteen digits, finds the shared edition, and the owner lists a Very Good copy with a photo and a suggested price. Say the sentence that matters: the edition was already shared, so this copy joined a page that students were already watching.

The third beat is the alert. Within seconds the student's inbox shows one alert for that copy. Then republish the same copy with a small price change and show that no second alert appears. This is the beat that separates a working feature from a duplicate-spamming one, and it is the one to rehearse most.

The fourth beat is the purchase. The student adds the copy, pays with a Stripe test card, and sees two orders with their grade snapshots and one collection code each. The shop marks one order collected with the code. Say why the snapshot exists: the shop can regrade its copy tomorrow and this receipt will not change.

The fifth beat is the race. Two tabs go for the last copy of another edition. One pays, the other receives a conflict that offers a substitute copy of the same edition at the same grade or better. Do not narrate the script that opens the tabs; the evidence file covers it.

Do not cut the recording to hide a failure, and do not record over a failed beat without saying what happened. A visible retry or a slow first job is more convincing than a polished sequence that never shows the system under load. If a beat fails completely, stop the take and start again.

## Answer without notes

The defense is seven questions answered in thirty to sixty seconds each, with no notes, no slide and no reading from the README. The shape of a strong answer is always the same: what the student or the shop owner experiences, then what the server does, then the record that proves it. If you cannot answer, say what you do know and name the file or log you would open. That is an engineering answer. A confident wrong one is not.

Have a mentor ask the questions in a live call, or record the answers in one unbroken take and note which format you used. The gate accepts either. It does not accept written answers, because writing gives you time that a viva does not.

## The seven questions

**Why is the edition shared and not owned by a shop, and who may change it?** A strong answer starts with the student: three shops may hold copies of one required textbook, and one catalogue page is what lets her compare them. It then says that a shop owns its copy and its price, while the edition belongs to the platform, and that once any copy references an edition the fields are locked for shops. Only the admin may correct them, through a route that writes an audit entry naming the old value, the new value and the actor. Finish with the failure the lock prevents: a title change would move every offer card and every reading list at once.

**Why is the grade stored as a number as well as a label?** The label is for people and the rank is for queries. A student who asks for Good or better is asking for a rank of at least two, which is one indexed comparison, while a label comparison would be a text match that also matches the word good inside a defect note. A strong answer adds where the label and rank come from: one server side grade map, so the pair cannot disagree, and a validation rule that rejects a mismatched pair. Mention that the grade sits on the copy, not the edition, because two copies of one book are damaged differently.

**What guarantees one edition per ISBN when two shops create it at the same moment?** The unique index on the canonical thirteen digit ISBN, and nothing else. A prior lookup in the browser or in the route is useful but it is not a guarantee, because both requests can read "not found" and both can insert. A strong answer describes the losing path: the second insert fails with error 11000, the route reads the winning edition, and that shop continues with its own listing under the shared record. The stored title stays the winner's, and a later request does not overwrite it.

**What happens to want-list alerts if the worker is down when a copy is listed? What if the job runs twice?** A down worker delays the alert and loses nothing, because the job sits in Redis until a worker consumes it, and the copy is already stored in MongoDB. A strong answer describes the restart test: publish with the worker stopped, start it, and each alert arrives once. A duplicate job is harmless because the alert has a unique key on the want entry and the listing, and the job carries a stable id, so the second write is refused rather than repeated. Finish by naming what a learner sees: an inbox with one row per copy, not two.

**Why do ISBN thirteen numbers that start with 979 have no ISBN ten?** Because the ten digit format encodes a publisher prefix from a range that has no nine seven nine equivalent. The International ISBN Agency states that a 979 prefix has no backward compatible ten digit form, so there is nothing to convert. A strong answer says what the code does instead: it stores the ten digit field as absent, uses a partial unique index so absent values do not collide, and never manufactures a ten digit number to fill the gap. That decision is why a partial index exists at all.

**What did you snapshot on the order line, and what goes wrong if you skip the grade?** The line copies the title, the ISBN, the edition label, the grade code, the defect summary, the first condition photo URL and the price in minor units, plus the listing and shop IDs for support. Skipping the grade means a shop that regrades a sold copy tomorrow rewrites what the student believed she bought, and a dispute becomes one person's memory against another's. A strong answer connects it to the matcher: the student chose that grade under her floor, so the grade is part of the agreement rather than a decoration on the receipt.

**Why did you not build recommendations as the main feature?** Because the course had one hero and it had to survive a weak connection and a real race. The reading-list matcher plus the standing want entry is the thing a student cannot do by hand, and it can be tested with seeded data. Recommendations would need behaviour data this marketplace does not have, and they would be untestable at this scale. A strong answer names what the product does instead for discovery: exact ISBN lookup and indexed title search, which are honest and provable. It also names the co-occurrence suggestion as a stretch lab rather than a core promise.

## Two more about the edges

Expect a question from the grading work: what stops a shop from listing a Good copy and shipping an Acceptable one? The honest answer is that the grade is a claim backed by photos and defect notes, that the snapshot preserves what was claimed, and that the remedy is a refund or a dispute, not a silent edit of the order. Saying where the limit is belongs in the defense.

Expect a second one about the last copy. Walk through the claim: a conditional update from available to held on one listing document, the matched count deciding the winner, the loser receiving a conflict with a substitute from the same edition at the same grade or better, and the payment intent never created for the loser. A race test that runs both tabs in the reverse order is stronger evidence than one lucky sequence.

## Gate checklist

- [ ] The demo link is in the README, unlisted, between four and six minutes, with audible narration.
- [ ] The recording uses the deployed app in two windows, with no localhost request visible.
- [ ] Beat 1: a reading list with three matches, one unmatched title and a readable reason per choice.
- [ ] Beat 2: a typed ten digit ISBN becomes one shared edition, with a graded copy and a suggested price.
- [ ] Beat 3: exactly one alert for the new copy, and no second alert after a republish.
- [ ] Beat 4: one payment, two orders, grade snapshots and one collection code per order.
- [ ] Beat 5: two tabs, one sale, one conflict that offers a substitute of the same edition.
- [ ] `docs/DEMO.md` matches the recording beat for beat, including the timings.
- [ ] All seven questions answered live or in one unbroken take, without notes.
- [ ] Every answer names the record or command that proves the claim.
- [ ] At least one answer names a limit or a trade-off honestly, such as the grade being a claim or the refund path being manual.
- [ ] `docs/EVIDENCE.md` has no row marked proven without a link, a command or a file behind it.
- [ ] README, `.env.example`, `docs/erd.md`, `docs/architecture.md` and the evidence index are committed.
- [ ] The live URL still works on a phone at the time of the gate, with the worker running.

## Learning log

1. Which beat was hardest to record, and what did that difficulty tell you about the system?
2. Which answer did you give weakest, and which file or test would you read again before a real interview?
3. What did you leave marked partial in `docs/EVIDENCE.md`, and what would it take to make it proven?
4. If a mentor opened your live URL with no context, what would they understand in the first minute, and what would they still have to ask?
5. Which single change would you make first if a third shop joined next week, and which part of the design would absorb it?
6. What would you tell the next fellow to build first, and what would you tell them to skip?

## What this gate does not prove

It does not prove that Hamesh is ready for real money, real consumer protection rules or real support load. Payments are in test mode, alerts arrive in an inbox rather than a message, pickup is coordinated by people, and one API process serves everyone. Saying that in the viva is part of the defense, not a weakness in it.

It also does not close the variant. Other modules in this path are authored and reviewed separately, and this gate speaks only for the deploy and closing work you completed here. A learner who can record this demo and answer these seven questions has met the bar for the work in front of them. Whether the whole track is ready for new learners is a decision for the course creator, not for this lesson.
