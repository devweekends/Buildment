```quiz
type: mcq
question: "In this made-up case, with made-up numbers, Lubaba is a backend developer who wrote a post about a 30-second cache she added to her team's search page. Which opening keeps a busy reader?"
options:
  - "Open with how she became interested in speed and the first project where she tried a cache, then turn to the search page"
  - "Open with the problem and result: the search page took 4 s, a 30-second cache cut it to 0.3 s, then the test"
  - "Open with a general line on why speed matters to every product today, then introduce the search page she worked on"
  - "Open with a short definition of caching and the main kinds of cache, then describe the search page she worked on"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "This is the history of her interest, which the reader has not asked about yet. The problem and the result are still far down the page, and a busy reader may leave first."
  - "Right. It starts where the reader's question is: what was wrong and what changed. The test comes next, so the reader has a reason to keep going."
  - "A general line about speed could open any post about any product. It tells the reader nothing about her work, and it delays the problem and the result."
  - "A definition teaches the idea of a cache but says nothing about her search page. A reader who wants her result must read through the textbook part first."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Qusay is a data engineer who publishes one article on his own site, on DEV and on Medium. He sets a canonical link (a tag that names the original page) on each copy. What does Google do, as taught in chapter 8.3 (checked 2026-10)?"
options:
  - "It penalises him for the duplicate copies, so his own page falls in the results and the platform copies take its place"
  - "It shows all three versions in the order they were published, one under another, so every copy gets its own place in the results"
  - "It always shows the original on his own site, because he published it first and set the canonical link on each copy"
  - "It picks one version to show, which may be another site's copy, and treats a canonical link as a hint and not a rule"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Google's own pages describe choosing one version of the same text, not punishing copies. One platform's help page uses the word penalised, but this course follows Google's wording."
  - "Google does not list every copy in order. It picks one version to show, so most readers meet only one of the three."
  - "A canonical link is a hint, not a rule. Google may still pick a platform's copy, so the original cannot be guaranteed to win."
  - "Right. Google picks one version and may pick another site's copy. A canonical link states a preference, and Google treats it as a hint, so it helps without a guarantee."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Samiha is a mobile developer. She wants to share her article on a platform that has no canonical setting she can confirm. What is the honest way to share there, as taught in chapter 8.3?"
options:
  - "Paste the full text and leave out the link, since readers on that platform will rarely leave it to visit her site"
  - "Skip the platform, because duplicate copies are penalised and would pull down her own page in the results"
  - "Share a short version or a link, with a 'First published at' line and a link to her page at the top of the post"
  - "Paste the full text and write a canonical tag in the body of the post, so that search follows the tag to her page"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "A full copy with no link gives readers no way to find her original. It also makes the platform's copy the only place her work lives."
  - "Copies are not penalised. Google picks one version to show. Skipping the platform also gives up readers who are already there."
  - "Right. A short version or a link keeps the platform useful for readers, and the 'First published at' line with a link works even where no setting exists."
  - "A canonical tag only works in a page's head section, not in the body of a post. Pasting it in the text does nothing for search and shows odd code to readers."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Bader is a DevOps engineer who is planning the home page of his personal site. Which element best shows first-hand work?"
options:
  - "A before and after screenshot of his own pipeline, with the date and where the number comes from"
  - "A stock photo of a laptop on a desk, with a line that says he is a passionate problem solver"
  - "A paragraph about his skills that he ran through a humaniser tool so that it looks hand-written"
  - "A grid of 25 technology logos, shown in the order that he learned them over his first years"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. It is his own work, dated, with a source for the number. A visitor can look at it and ask about it. Nobody else could have put it on a page."
  - "A stock photo and a slogan could sit on anyone's site. A visitor cannot check either, so neither shows that Bader did any work."
  - "A humaniser changes the style of a paragraph, not the facts. It adds nothing a visitor can check, and passing a detector is not the goal."
  - "A grid of logos shows what he has touched, not what he did or what changed. It names no result and no date that a visitor could look at."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Naji is a QA engineer. He asks an AI tool how to make his new site pass AI detectors. It answers: 'Run all your text through a humaniser so that detectors do not flag it.' What is wrong with this advice?"
options:
  - "Nothing is wrong, because detectors are accurate and a humaniser is a sound way to stay safe from a flag"
  - "Nothing is wrong, as long as Naji adds a note on his site that tells visitors he used a humaniser tool"
  - "It is wrong because detectors cannot flag any text at all, so there is nothing to avoid with a humaniser"
  - "Detectors misjudge second-language English and humanisers add no facts; specifics make the site his"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Detectors are not accurate. Studies have found that they often mislabel writing by people who learned English as a second language."
  - "A note does not fix the problem. A humaniser changes the style of the text, not its facts, so the site still gives a visitor nothing new to check."
  - "Detectors can and do flag text, sometimes wrongly. The problem is that they are unreliable, not that they never act."
  - "Right. Detectors often misjudge second-language English, and a humaniser adds no information. What makes a site his is specifics and checked facts."
```
