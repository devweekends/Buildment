Diala's lost post was good because it showed how she worked. This chapter is about writing a post like that, with your own work. Areej is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative. You will move her post into the right order, and later write your own.

## A visitor judges you by your writing

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m8-visitor-judges" />

A visitor opens one post. In a few minutes they see the problem, how you tested it and what it cost. From those three things they form a view of how you think. They have not read a resume line yet. A good post is a sample of your work that a stranger can judge for themselves.

<GoDeeper title="Why one post counts">

A resume line says what you did. A post shows how you think while you did it. That is why founders who read a post learn things a resume cannot tell them: which problem you chose, how you tested your fix, and what you gave up.

Google's guidance on helpful content (checked 2026-10) asks whether a page gives original information or analysis, and whether it shows first-hand experience. That guidance is about search results, not hiring. But a founder asks nearly the same things. Did this person really do it? Can I see how they decided?

A post is also different from the case study you wrote in module 5. The case study is the finished story of one project in a fixed shape. A post is shorter and can be about one decision, one test or one mistake. On your site, the case study sits on the Work page and your posts sit on the Writing page. Both answer the same question, "can I believe this person?", in different ways.

</GoDeeper>

## Problem, what I tried, numbers, change

<MermaidDiagram title="The four parts of Areej's post" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["The problem: a slow events list"] --> B["What I tried: a 30-second cache"]
  B --> C["The numbers: p95 820 ms to 95 ms, same test"]
  C --> D["What I would change: test on production-like data"]
`} legend="Four boxes, in order. The numbers come from Areej's ledger, so each one has a source." />

Areej's post is about a load test on her study project. It has four parts: the problem, what she tried, the numbers with their source, and what she would change. A post with all four is complete. If one is missing, the reader is left with a question. Her title names the result: "Same script, same data: what a 30-second cache did to our p95".

<GoDeeper title="Areej's post, part by part">

Here is what goes in each part. The p95 is the time that 95 out of 100 requests beat. A request is one call to the server.

**The problem.** The events list endpoint of Campus Board, her four-person study project, was slow under load. She says who wrote what: a teammate wrote the endpoint, and she added the cache and wrote the test.

**What she tried.** An in-memory cache that keeps each answer for 30 seconds. She also describes the test. It used the same k6 script before and after. The load was a constant 50 requests per second for 2 minutes. The database was the same seeded one of 10,000 events, on the same laptop. The target was p95 under 200 ms.

**The numbers.** Before, p95 was 820 ms and the target failed. After, p95 was 95 ms and the target passed. The error rate stayed under 1 percent in both runs. Every number comes from a row in her ledger, with its source.

**What she would change.** The post says the cache can show a list that is up to 30 seconds old. It also says she tested on a laptop with seeded data, so next time she would test against a copy of production data.

Notice what is not here: a long history of the project and a thank-you list. They can come last, or not at all.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Diala's lost post, rebuilt in four parts">

Diala can rebuild her post from her notes. She adds no new number.

The problem: the team's CI pipeline took 22 minutes, so each change waited. What she tried: the steps her notes list, one by one, with the reason for each. The numbers: 22 minutes before and 9 minutes after. She must also write how she measured them, which runs she counted and from which log. That detail is in her own ledger, and the post is not finished without it. What she would change: one thing she would do differently, taken from her notes.

Her result does not change. The post only becomes checkable once the source and the window are written next to it.

</GoDeeper>

## Start where the reader's question is

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m8-open-with-problem" />

Six paragraphs of Areej's post start with history and thanks. A busy reader has 10 lines of room, and it runs out before the problem. Tap a paragraph, then move it up or down. Put the problem and the result at the top. Nothing is cut. The history and the thanks stay in the post, after the reader has a reason to care.

<GoDeeper title="How to open a post">

Open with two or three sentences: the problem, then the result, then a promise to show how you know. Areej could start: "My events list took 820 ms at p95 under load. A 30-second cache cut it to 95 ms. Here is the test that shows it."

After that, tell it in the order it happened if you like. Set-up, then numbers, then the trade-off, then what you would change. The top answers "why read this?" and the rest answers "how do you know?".

The line counts in the picture are illustrative. No study gives a best opening length, so do not count words. Ask one question: does the first paragraph answer the reader's question?

A common worry is "but I want to tell the story in order". You can. Just put one line of the ending first.

</GoDeeper>

## Plain words, short sentences

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m8-plain-words" />

Write the way you would explain it to a tired colleague. Use short words, active verbs and one idea per sentence. "Conduct an analysis of" becomes "analyse". Plain does not mean childish. Keep the exact term where it is needed, like p95, and say once what it means.

<GoDeeper title="Plain language in practice">

Government plain-language guides (Digital.gov, checked 2026-10) say that content is easier to understand with shorter words, short sections, active voice and present tense. They warn against hidden verbs, such as "conduct an analysis of" in place of "analyse". This is government guidance, not a trial, but it matches what readers tell us.

Here is a before and after from Areej's test. Before: "An analysis of the response times of the events endpoint was conducted by us under a constant load." After: "We ran the same test twice. We kept the load at 50 requests a second. We compared the response times."

The three short sentences are not simpler in meaning. They just do not make the reader hunt for the verb.

If English is your second language, plain writing is an advantage. Short sentences are easier to check, easier to translate in a reader's head and harder to get wrong. Read your post aloud. If you run out of breath in a sentence, cut it in two.

</GoDeeper>

## The say-it-back test

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m8-say-it-back" />

Ask a person to read your post, close it, and say it back in their own words. Areej's classmate said: "She made the list faster by keeping a copy for 30 seconds, and proved it with the same test before and after." One piece was missing, why the copy can be stale. She added a sentence. If they cannot say it back, rewrite. Test at least twice.

<GoDeeper title="How to run the test">

Pick a listener who did not work on your project. Do not help them while they read. When they finish, ask: "Tell me what this says in three sentences." Write their words down, exactly.

Then compare their three sentences with the three you meant. Mark every gap. A gap is not the listener's mistake. It is a place where your post did not carry the idea. Fix the biggest gap, then test again with a second person.

Your listener may say it back in any language they like. You are testing the idea, not their English. If nobody is near, send the post and ask for a voice note.

Use a person, not a tool. A tool already knows what you meant, so it will fill the gaps for you. A person shows you what the page really says. Plain-language guidance calls this paraphrase testing, and suggests planning to test at least twice. It suits short documents like a post.

</GoDeeper>

## Say when AI helped

<MermaidDiagram title="Where the AI line goes" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Your notes and numbers"] --> B["AI tool: restructure into sections"]
  B --> C["You check every number and decision"]
  C --> D["Last line: say how AI helped"]
`} legend="The line about AI comes after your own checks." />

If an AI tool helped, say so in one honest line at the end. Areej's line: "I used an AI tool to restructure my notes into sections; every number and decision is mine and I checked each one." Write it only if it is true.

<GoDeeper title="What the AI line must not hide">

Google's guidance on AI-made content (checked 2026-10) says to fact-check it, to add value of your own, and to consider saying how it was made. Your reader has the same reasons. They want to know that a person checked the numbers.

Do not use a "humaniser" tool to hide that AI helped. Detectors have been shown to mislabel second-language English, so passing one is not the goal. The honest fix is simple: say how the tool helped, and own every number.

AI is good at restructuring your notes into sections, and at pointing to long sentences. It is not allowed to add a number, a result or an experience you do not have. Course 13, "Learn and Build with AI", shows how to turn a week of commits into an article and how to say when AI helped.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

The idea that content should show original information and first-hand experience comes from Google Search Central's guidance on helpful content and on AI-made content (checked 2026-10). The plain-language advice comes from the US General Services Administration's Digital.gov plain language guides (checked 2026-10). The finding that detectors misjudge second-language writing is from Liang and colleagues (2023). The four-part post and the say-it-back routine are this course's method, built on that guidance.
