## Module 8 checklist

- [ ] My post has the four parts (the problem, what I tried, the numbers, what I would change), and every number is in my ledger
- [ ] I ran the say-it-back test with a person and changed something because of it
- [ ] My channel plan lists the channels I can reach (about ten is a guide), each with a version and a link line, and I tried each one on my phone
- [ ] My website plan has five pages, a free host with a check date, and at least four specifics that make it mine
- [ ] If I published a copy, it says where it was first published
- [ ] I finished the quick checks in chapters 8.2 to 8.4 and the quiz in 8.7

## Learning log

1. **Say it back:** What did your listener say back, and what did you change? Or use Areej's case: what did her classmate say, and what did she add?
2. **Your channels:** Which channel matters most for the readers you want, and why? Or use Areej's case: which of her ten channels would you keep first?

## Your task: your post, your test and your two plans

This task shows three things. You can write about your work so a stranger can judge it. You can share it so readers can find the original. You can plan a home for it. It does not grade how impressive your project is. A small project, clearly told, passes.

Give your report in four short parts. First, paste the link to your post, or its text. Second, record your say-it-back test. Say who read it (a role is enough, such as "my cousin, a nurse"). Say what they said back in their own words, and the change you made because of it. Third, give your channel plan in one line per channel, with the version and the link line. About ten channels is a guide, and fewer is fine if those are the ones you can reach. The link line can be a canonical link (a setting that names the original page) or a "First published at" line. Fourth, describe your website plan: the five pages, your free host with the date you checked its limits, and your specifics. If you published a copy, add its link. Write about 160 words, not counting the plan lines. Use your own work, or Areej's material if you have none.

A strong answer is easier to see than to describe, so here is one from Jawdat, a made-up Python developer. He wrote: "My post is 'Splitting a nightly export across four workers: 42 minutes to 14' (text pasted below). I tested it on my cousin, who is a nurse. She said back: 'He made the report faster by running four copies at once, and he timed ten nights before and ten after.' She missed that one of the four copies once failed, so I added a paragraph on the retry I wrote. Channel 1 is my site, the original. Channel 2 is DEV, a full copy with canonical_url set. Channel 3 is LinkedIn, a short post and the link. Channel 4 is my repository README, with one line and the link. Channel 5 is the team chat, with one line and the link, and my lead agreed. Channel 6 is Medium, a full copy with 'originally published elsewhere' set. Channel 7 is my GitHub profile README, with a few lines and the link. Channel 8 is a public forum thread about batch jobs, with a short answer and the link, where the forum allows links. Every copy says 'First published at' with my address, and I opened each platform on my phone on 3 November. Site: Home, Work, Writing, About, Contact on GitHub Pages, limits checked 2026-10 (1 GB; no shops). Specifics: a screenshot of the scheduler log with the date. The 42 to 14 minute result with its ten-night window. My mistake, that I tested on one machine. A date on every post. Contact is email only."

What to copy from his answer: the listener's own words are quoted, and the change is named. Every channel has a version and a way home. The host has a date and a limit. The specifics are things nobody else could put on their site. He did not claim a result the post cannot back up, and he named a mistake.

A weak answer reads very differently. "I wrote a good post about my project and my friend said she liked it. I will share it on every social network I can find. My website will use a nice template with my skills and a photo of a laptop." It does not say what the friend said back, so there is no test and no change. It gives no versions and no way home for any copy. It names no host and no check date, and its "specifics" are a template and a stock photo. A mentor cannot ask "how was that measured?" because no number appears. It would also fail "what was your part?", because the post is not described.

If you prefer Areej's case, use these facts. Her post is "Same script, same data: what a 30-second cache did to our p95", about 800 words. Its numbers are p95 (the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within) from 820 ms to 95 ms. The load was a constant 50 requests per second for 2 minutes. The database was the same seeded one (filled with demo data made up on purpose) of 10,000 events, on the same laptop. Its limits are that lists can be 30 seconds old and that she tested on seeded data, so she would test on a copy of production data. Her classmate said: "She made the list faster by keeping a copy for 30 seconds, and proved it with the same test before and after." She added one sentence on why the copy can be stale. Her ten channels are in chapter 8.3, and her website plan is in chapter 8.6.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your report part by part. Each part is Met, Partly or Not yet. A small project told honestly is a full pass.

**Part 1: Post.**

- **Met:** All four parts are present: the problem, what you tried, numbers with their sources, and what you would change. Every number names a ledger row or says "not measured". The words are plain, with no empty phrases from the module 6 list. An AI-use line is present if AI helped.
- **Partly:** One part is missing, or one number has no ledger row.
- **Not yet:** There is no post.

**Part 2: Say-it-back.**

- **Met:** You name the listener (a role is enough), quote what they said back, and state one change you made because of it.
- **Partly:** The test was done, but no change was made.
- **Not yet:** The test was not done.

**Part 3: Channel plan.**

- **Met:** Every channel you list has a version and a link line. The first row is your own page, or a repository README until your site exists. You tried each channel on your phone, with a date. There is no minimum number of channels.
- **Partly:** Some link lines are missing, or some channels were not tried on a phone.
- **Not yet:** There is no plan.

**Part 4: Website plan.**

- **Met:** Five pages are planned. There is a free host with the date you checked its limits. At least four specifics make the site yours. A privacy rule is stated (email only).
- **Partly:** One of those four things is missing.
- **Not yet:** There is no plan.

**Pass rule:** every part is at least Partly, and the Post part is Met. A live site is not required. If you put one live, add its address, and the mentor will open it on a phone.

Your mentor then replies with one to three follow-up questions. They may ask what your listener got wrong and what that tells you about the post. They may ask which channel gets only a link, and why. Or they may ask what on your website only you could have written. You answer in writing or in a voice note. Your answers are the last step of the gate, so look for the questions after you submit.
