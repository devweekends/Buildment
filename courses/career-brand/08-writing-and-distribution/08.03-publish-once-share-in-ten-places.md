Diala's post had one home, and she did not control it. This chapter is about the opposite: one original on a page you own, and copies that lead readers back to it. Areej's channel plan is an example, not a rule.

## Your own page is the original

<MermaidDiagram title="Your page is the original" chart={`flowchart BT
  L["LinkedIn: short post and a link"] --> H["Your own page: the original"]
  D["DEV: full copy with canonical_url"] --> H
  M["Medium: full copy, set as originally published elsewhere"] --> H
  G["Repository README: one line and a link"] --> H
`} legend="The original is at the top. Each arrow is a copy that sends readers back to it." />

Publish on a page you own first. Then share copies that lead back to it. Other platforms can change their rules or lock an account, as Diala found. People call this idea POSSE: publish on your own site, syndicate elsewhere. To syndicate means to share a copy somewhere else. Use the other places to bring readers to your page.

<GoDeeper title="Why the original goes first">

POSSE comes from the IndieWeb community, which keeps a public wiki about it (checked 2026-10). It is a community practice, not research. The wiki gives two reasons: you own the original, and every copy can link back to it.

In plain words: your own page is the one you control. LinkedIn, DEV and Medium can change their rules or lock your account. Keep the original on a page of yours, and use the other places to bring readers to it.

"Your own page" can be your site (chapters 8.4 and 8.6). Until your site exists, it can be the README of a GitHub repository, or one simple page. What matters is that you control it and the address does not change.

Why make copies at all? Because readers are on those platforms. A copy brings them to your work. It is for readers, not a trick for search results. Course 7, "Professional & Business Communication", covers the basic manners of posting and messaging on LinkedIn, so this chapter does not repeat them.

</GoDeeper>

## Search picks one copy, with no penalty

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m8-search-picks-one" />

Google sees the same article at three addresses. It picks one version to show. That may be a platform's copy, not your original. A canonical link tells Google which version you prefer. Google calls this a hint, not a rule, so expect it to help, not to be guaranteed. Copies are not punished. Readers still need a way home.

<GoDeeper title="What Google says (checked 2026-10)">

Google Search Central says you can state which address you prefer in three ways. One is a redirect. One is a `rel="canonical"` link on the page. One is a sitemap, which is a list of your pages. The first two are strong signals. The sitemap is a weak one. It also says a canonical preference is a hint and not a rule. If you give no preference, a site will likely do fine.

For copies on other people's sites, Google says the canonical link is not the best tool. The most effective fix is for the partner site to block search engines from listing its copy. A fellow cannot make a platform do that, so the honest summary is this. Publish on your own site first. Set the canonical where the platform lets you. Accept that Google may still show another copy.

One platform's help page says sites with a lot of duplicate content and no canonical link "may be penalized". Google's own pages describe choosing one version, not a penalty. This course follows Google's wording: Google picks one version and may pick the other site's copy.

</GoDeeper>

## Where a canonical field exists

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m8-canonical-fields" />

Each platform points back in its own way. DEV has a `canonical_url` field at the top of a post. Medium has a setting that marks a story as originally published elsewhere, and sets it for you when you import. LinkedIn gets a short post and a link. Where you cannot confirm a setting, use the "first published at" line. Checked 2026-10.

<GoDeeper title="Platform by platform">

**DEV.** The editor guide describes a front matter field called `canonical_url`. Front matter is a small block of settings at the top of the post. You put the address of your original there.

**Medium.** The help centre says importing a story sets the source as the canonical link automatically. You can also set it by hand in the editor, under advanced settings, with the option "this story was originally published elsewhere". Only the author can set it. The label on your screen may differ.

**LinkedIn.** The help page for article SEO describes only an SEO title and a description. We read no canonical field. So give LinkedIn a short post with a link to your page.

**Hashnode and Substack.** We could not confirm how their canonical settings behave. Do not rely on them. Use the "first published at" line.

**Can you reach them?** Access to DEV, Medium and other platforms from where you are is not confirmed. Try each on your phone before the gate. If one will not open or will not let you sign up, skip it and use your site and your repository README. Ask your mentor if you are unsure.

If your screen differs from this list, follow your screen.

</GoDeeper>

## One honest line on every copy

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m8-first-published-line" />

At the top or bottom of every copy, write one line: "First published at [your site]." with a link. It costs nothing and it is true. It works everywhere, even where you cannot confirm a canonical setting. Readers use it even if search engines do not.

<GoDeeper title="Writing the line">

Keep it short and the same on every copy. For example: "First published at [your site address], on [date]." Put it where a reader sees it before they leave, usually the first line.

Do not change the title to hide that a copy is a copy. Do not ask a platform to hide your original. Do not pretend the copy is the original. The line is honest because it says which page came first.

The page it points to must exist and keep its address. If you move or rename the page later, every copy breaks, as Diala's resume link did. Module 5 covers keeping links working over time.

</GoDeeper>
