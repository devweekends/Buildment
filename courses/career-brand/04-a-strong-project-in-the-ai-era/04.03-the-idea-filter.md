Sireen's app failed a test that nobody had asked her to run. This chapter gives you four questions to ask before you build anything. Sireen and Areej are fictional fellows. By the end you will sort eight ideas, and know when a small open source contribution is the better path.

## Four questions before you build

<MermaidDiagram title="The idea filter" chart={`flowchart TD
  I["An idea"] --> Q1{"Useful on day one to one known person or group?"}
  Q1 -->|yes| Q2{"A result you can measure?"}
  Q2 -->|yes| Q3{"Depth you can explain in writing?"}
  Q3 -->|yes| Q4{"No need for two sides at once?"}
  Q4 -->|yes| P["Build it"]
`} legend="An idea passes only when every answer is yes. A no sends you back to reshape the idea." />

An idea passes only when all four answers are yes. Is it useful on day one to one person, or to a small group you know? Can you measure a result? Is there depth you can explain in writing? Can it work without two sides arriving at the same time? Stop at the first no, and reshape the idea.

<GoDeeper title="Why these four, and how to answer them">

These four questions are this course's method. They are not a law. Each one has a job.

Question 1 gives you a real user, so there is someone to ask whether it helped. "A small known group" means a group you can name: the print shop owner, or a bus driver and 14 families. "Small businesses" is not a group you can name.

Question 2 gives you a number to move, with a baseline. If you cannot say what you would count, you cannot show that the project worked.

Question 3 asks for depth you can write about, such as a hard part, or what breaks when many people use it at once. Writing the depth down is how a reader sees that you understood what you built. Module 8 teaches the writing.

Question 4 removes the cold start. If the idea needs buyers and sellers, or drivers and riders, then two groups must arrive at the same time.

Write a reason for every yes. A bare "yes" is not an answer. A good reason names a person, a number or a topic.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Areej's Raqim Stock through the four questions">

Areej's idea is Raqim Stock, a small stock and invoice tool for the print shop owner. Here are her four answers, each with its reason.

1. **Useful on day one?** Yes. The shop owner is the real user, and Areej already works with the shop.
2. **A result you can measure?** Yes. Stockouts per month. The baseline is 5 stockouts in August and September 2025, from the owner's notebook.
3. **Depth to write about?** Yes. Reorder levels and stock updates must not double count when two sales happen together.
4. **No two sides?** Yes. There is one kind of user, the owner. Later, two other print shops she knows could join. That is a small known group, not a marketplace.

All four are yes, so it passes. She wrote a reason for each, and each reason can be checked.

</GoDeeper>

## Why two-sided apps stall

<MermaidDiagram title="The two-sided waiting loop" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["No drivers"] --> B["Riders do not join"]
  B --> C["No riders"]
  C --> D["Drivers do not join"]
  D --> A
`} legend="Each side waits for the other. The loop never starts by itself." />

Sireen's app needs drivers and riders. With no drivers, riders do not join. With no riders, drivers do not join. Each side waits for the other. Economists call the point where each side is large enough to attract the other "critical mass". A new two-sided app must reach it on both sides at once.

<GoDeeper title="What the research says, and what it does not">

Two economists, Evans and Schmalensee, wrote about why new platforms fail to launch. Their point is that a new two-sided platform has to reach critical mass on both sides at the same time. That is the cold start problem: the first step is hard, because each side waits for the other.

From this it is reasonable to think that a marketplace side project often gets no users. But we found no data that counts side projects, so this is reasoning, not a statistic. Do not repeat "most side projects fail" as a fact.

Here is a safe way to say it: "Two-sided apps need both sides to show up at the same time. That is why a small tool that is useful to one person usually beats a marketplace with no sellers."

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Ways a two-sided idea can start smaller">

You can often keep the idea and change where it starts. Instead of a marketplace for plumbers and customers, build a job list for one plumber you know. Instead of an app that matches tutors and students, build a booking tool for one tutor. The tool works for that person alone, and nobody has to wait.

Notice what you give up. You give up the large audience, for now. What you gain is a real user, a real number and a reason to keep going. If the small tool works, the idea can grow later, and you will have evidence when you ask a second group to join.

</GoDeeper>

## Useful with one user

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-bus-schedule" />

Watch who has to wait. Sireen reshapes her idea into a pickup schedule for her cousin's school bus run. There is one driver she knows and 14 families. The schedule is useful on day one. Nobody waits for a second group. A number to measure could be missed pickups, and the driver can tell her how many there were.

<GoDeeper title="Single-player first">

One practitioner essay argues that many network products began with a mode that was useful to a single person, with no one else on the network. It is an essay, not a study, so we treat it as an idea and not as proof. The idea fits Sireen's case well.

There is a second safe shape: a small group you already know, on the first day. A driver and 14 families is that shape. You can ask them. You can count what happens.

A small group is a start, not a result. "14 families use it" is true on day one. Whether it helped needs a number, such as missed pickups before and after.

</GoDeeper>

## Run eight ideas through the filter

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-run-the-filter" />

Eight ideas, three bins. For each card, ask which question the idea fails first, or whether it passes all four. Tap the bin, or drag the card onto it. A wrong try says why. Four ideas pass, for different reasons: a small known group, depth in writing, a fix a maintainer can merge, and one shop with a number to count.

<GoDeeper title="The community cloud, as one example idea">

One card describes a community cloud. Here is what the words mean. NIST, a United States standards body, defines a community cloud as computing infrastructure reserved for a specific group of organisations that share concerns, run by one or more of them or by a third party. In plain words: a pool of computers that a group with a common purpose shares.

Pooling spare computing already works in another form. A project called BOINC lets people donate spare computing to science, and about 30 science projects use it (checked 2026-10).

Picture a community, or a company, that pools its spare servers so its own people can run jobs on them. Does that pass the filter? It can, under three conditions. It starts with a defined small group that you can name. It has a real workload, such as jobs those people already run. And it has a result you can measure: jobs run, compute hours used, or cost avoided. Without these three, it is a big infrastructure idea with no user.

This is one example of an idea that fits the filter. It is not anyone's project in this course, and it does not claim that any group has built one. Use it to practise the four questions.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="The URL shortener deep dive, and a fix a maintainer can merge">

A URL shortener may get only a few real users. Its strength is depth. You can ask what breaks when many people click at once, measure it with a load test, and write it up as a blog post. Module 8 teaches the writing. Course 13, "Learn and Build with AI", runs a URL shortener lab you can start from. This is the "go deep and show the depth in writing" shape. Your first reader is you, and later the people who read the post.

A small documentation fix in an open source library is a third shape. Real users read that documentation. A maintainer can merge the fix without waiting for anyone else, and a merged change is a result you can show.

Now look at the cards that fail. A clone of a famous app in a weekend has no real user and nothing to measure. A to-do app like the hundred others has the same problem. A marketplace with no sellers fails question 4.

</GoDeeper>

## When open source is the better path

<MermaidDiagram title="A first open source contribution" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Find an issue labelled for newcomers"] --> B["Check the project is still maintained"]
  B --> C["Read CONTRIBUTING"]
  C --> D["Open an issue first"]
  D --> E["Make a small docs or test fix"]
`} legend="Five steps, from finding an issue to a small fix. Open source is optional in this course." />

Open source is an optional path. Find an issue labelled for newcomers. Check that the project is still active. Read its contributing guide, and open an issue before you start. Then make a small docs or test fix. Not everyone has the time or the freedom to do this, and that is fine. Do the quick check below, then go to chapter 4.4.

<GoDeeper title="The steps, and what GitHub says (checked 2026-10)">

GitHub's help pages suggest looking for issues labelled "good first issue" or "help wanted". They also suggest checking that a project is active, for example on its Pulse page, and opening an issue before you start large work. Do these in order.

First, find an issue that fits a first change. Second, look at recent activity. If nobody has answered anything for a year, choose another project. Third, read the CONTRIBUTING file, which says how the project wants changes to arrive. Fourth, comment on the issue or open one, and say what you plan to do. Fifth, make a small fix: a clearer sentence in the docs, a missing test.

Documentation, translations, triage and review all count as contributions. You do not need to write new features.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Why it is hard, and why it is optional">

A study of newcomers to open source organised 58 barriers, and 13 of them were social, such as not knowing who to ask. So the first contribution is often hard for reasons that have little to do with code. A small fix, discussed in an issue first, lowers the risk.

You stay responsible for work an AI tool helped to write. Read it, test it and be ready to explain it.

Open source is not possible for everyone. A company that publishes how it hires says open source and prior code are not feasible for every applicant, for example when your work code is private. So it is an option here, not a requirement. The belief "you must contribute to a famous project to be taken seriously" is not true.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Areej's documentation fix, kept for later">

Areej considered a documentation fix in the PDF library that her invoice script uses. She chose Raqim Stock first, because the print shop owner gives her a real user and a number to move. She keeps the documentation fix as a later small step. Choosing one path first is not a failure to do the other.

</GoDeeper>
