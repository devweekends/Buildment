## Fix the time, flex the scope

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-fixed-box" />

Watch which feature the plan accepts. Areej fixes her time box first: 6 weeks at about 5 hours a week, so 30 hours. Then she shapes the scope to fit. The core flow goes in. Supplier orders do not fit, so they are cut for later. The cut feature was not bad. It simply did not fit the box.

<GoDeeper title="Appetite, not estimate">

A book on shaping work, Shape Up, separates two ideas. An estimate asks how long a piece of work will take. An appetite asks how much time the problem deserves. You decide the appetite first. Then you shape a solution that fits it. Time is fixed and scope can change. In one line: fix a time box first, then fit the scope to it.

Why does this help? A plan with no time box grows until it never ends. With a box of 6 weeks at 5 hours, a feature that does not fit is out of scope, not forgotten. You can write it in a list called "later".

This is a planning habit. It is not a promise that you will finish on time. If you lose a week, the box shrinks to 25 hours (5 weeks at 5 hours), and the scope shrinks with it. The course Learn and Build with AI teaches a weekly learning plan around a project over six months. A time box like this one sits inside that plan.

</GoDeeper>

## Set the appetite, watch scope fall

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-scope-sentence" />

Here is a plan in one sentence. It promises too much. Tap each part that does not fit a time box, or that nobody could check. Each tap says why and shows Areej's choice: one flow for one shop, a stated time box, and a number to count. The fix for each part is smaller, not bigger.

<GoDeeper title="How to make the scope fit your hours">

Follow these steps on your own idea. First, write the core flow in one line. For Areej it is: record a sale, stock goes down, a warning appears. Second, list every other feature you would like. Third, write a guess in hours next to each one. Fourth, add them up with the core flow. Fifth, if the total is more than your box, cut the feature that is worth least to your user, or make it smaller.

Here is a small example with made-up numbers. A box is 30 hours. The core flow, with its low-stock alert inside it, is a guess of 18 hours. A CSV export of all sales for an accountant is 7 and a second shop is 14. The total is 18 + 7 + 14 = 39 hours, which is 9 hours over the box. Cut the second shop, and the total is 25 hours. That fits, and 5 hours are left. Leave room, because guesses are often too low.

Areej's own cuts are supplier ordering, many shops and a mobile app. Write at least two items in an "out of scope" list, so your decision is visible to a reader.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Why a modest sentence is easier to check">

A reader can check "one flow, six weeks, count stockouts". A reader cannot check "make the shop much better". As the sentence gets smaller, it gets more checkable. That is the idea behind the counter in the picture.

A modest plan is also kinder to you. When you reach week 4 and the core flow works, you have something real. A huge plan at week 4 has many half-built parts and nothing you can show.

</GoDeeper>

## One user, one flow, one number

<MermaidDiagram title="Can a reader check the plan?" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["A project plan"] --> Q{"Can a reader check it?"}
  Q -->|"Many users, many features"| X["Too big to check"]
  Q -->|"One user, one flow, one number with a baseline"| Y["Each part can be asked about"]
`} legend="Two plans for the same idea. A plan with many users and many features gives a reader nothing to check. A plan with one user, one flow and one number gives a question for each part." />

A plan card has three slots. One user: the print shop owner. One flow: record a sale, stock goes down, a warning appears. One number: stockouts per month. A stockout is a paper type that runs out before it is reordered. The number needs a baseline with a source: 5 in August and September 2025, from the owner's notebook.

<GoDeeper title="The baseline, and a target written honestly">

A baseline is the number before your change. Without it, "stockouts fell" means nothing. Areej's baseline is 5 stockouts in two months, which is 2.5 a month. It comes from a notebook the owner already keeps, so a reader can ask to see it.

Write a target as a target, never as a result. "I will count stockouts and compare them with the 5" is honest. "This will cut stockouts by 80 percent" is a promise written as if it were a fact. A reader will ask how it was measured, and before launch there is no answer.

You may have met SMART goals: specific, measurable, assignable, realistic and time-related. The idea is often traced to a 1981 management article. One source says its author meant it as a writing aid and not as a strict rule. We could check this only through secondary sources, so treat the history lightly. The habit is useful: a specific number and a date.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="If there is no baseline yet">

Sometimes nobody has counted. Then your plan has a different first step: get the baseline before you build. For two weeks, ask your user to note each time the problem happens, or ask for old records. Write in the plan that the baseline comes first and that the build starts after it.

A plan that says "baseline first" passes. A plan with a number and no source does not. Never invent a baseline to make the plan look complete.

</GoDeeper>

## Plan how you will measure it

<MermaidDiagram title="Is the comparison fair?" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Baseline: 5 stockouts, Aug and Sep 2025"] --> C{"Same definition, same length?"}
  B["After launch: the next two months"] --> C
  C -->|"Yes"| D["Compare, and say what else changed"]
  C -->|"No"| E["Not a fair comparison"]
`} legend="The plan is a claim card written before the result exists. It is fair only if both counts use the same definition and cover the same length of time." />

The plan is a future claim card from module 3. The tool logs each stockout, so the count after launch uses the same definition as the owner's notebook. Then write what else changed, such as the season or a new supplier. A reader will ask about that last box.

<GoDeeper title="How this fits the claim card and the fair test">

In module 3 a claim card had a metric, a before, an after, a window, a source and your share. Areej's plan fills most of it in advance. The metric is stockouts per month. The before is 5 in two months. The window is two months before and two months after. The sources are the owner's notebook and the tool's own log. Her share is the whole build.

The fair test from module 3 applies too. Compare like with like: two months against two months, the same definition of a stockout. Write what else changed, because a new supplier or a busy season can change stockouts without your tool.

Be honest about size. One shop and two months is a small sample, so a change could be luck. When you report it, write "what I saw", not "proof".

</GoDeeper>

## The first real user milestone

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-first-real-user" />

Watch the flag at week 3. By then the owner records one real week of sales in Raqim Stock. That is the first proof that the tool is used. A project that was fast to build proves less than a project someone used. Find one real user before you build the second feature.

<GoDeeper title="Why week 3, and how to reach your first user">

Week 3 is the middle of a 6-week box. You still have three weeks to change what you learn. It is a date, not a hope. Write the date in your plan.

How do you reach a first user? Ask in person if you can. Show a rough version early, before it is polished. Sit beside the person the first time they use it and watch where they stop. Ask for one week of real use, not a promise for ever. If meeting is hard, a short voice note and a screen recording can do the same job.

A first user is a start, not a result. The number to move still needs its two months of data.

</GoDeeper>
