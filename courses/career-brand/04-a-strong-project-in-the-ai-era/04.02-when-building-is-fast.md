Sireen's app ran, and it still proved little. This chapter asks why, and what a reader can trust instead. The studies are real. By the end you will fill in the project page for Areej's planned project.

## Studies of AI coding tools disagree

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-studies-disagree" />

Four teams tested AI coding tools, and they did not agree. Watch their answers arrive in turn. One small task went faster. Company experiments saw more tasks finished. Experienced developers on their own code took longer. A follow-up was too weak to size anything. They measured different tasks, people and tools. So do not promise a speed number. Promise a record of what you checked.

<GoDeeper title="The four studies, with their limits">

**Study 1: one small task.** In a controlled experiment, developers had to write an HTTP server in JavaScript as fast as they could. The group with an AI coding tool finished faster. This was one well-defined task with one tool, in early 2023. The result is real, and it is narrow.

**Study 2: three company experiments.** Three field experiments (tests run inside real workplaces, not a lab) ran inside large companies, with 4,867 developers in total. Developers given the tool completed more tasks. The gains were larger for less experienced developers. It is a peer-reviewed paper. The count of developers differs a little between versions of it.

**Study 3: experienced developers on their own code.** Sixteen experienced open source developers worked on 246 real issues in repositories they knew well. With early-2025 tools they took longer. Before the trial they had forecast that the tools would make them faster. Afterwards they still believed they had been faster. The authors say they do not claim that AI slows most developers. They also note that people's own feelings about their speed can be very wrong.

**Study 4: the follow-up.** The same group tried again with later tools. They called their own data very weak evidence, because many developers refused to work without AI and time was hard to record. They could not size the effect, so we do not quote its numbers.

These results do not contradict each other. A small task, a company's usual work and an expert's own codebase are different things, and the tools changed between studies.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What to say when someone asks how much faster AI makes you">

You do not need a number. You can say what you did. Once Areej has built her stock tool, she plans to answer like this: "I used an AI tool to draft the first version of the stock update. I wrote a test for two sales at once, checked the count, and fixed what was wrong. My log shows what I asked, what I used and what I checked." That answer stays true whatever the studies find next year. It is a plan for later, because she has not built the tool yet.

Be careful with answers from AI tools too. A tool may state a speed figure with great confidence. If it is repeating anything, it is repeating a number from one study. In the quiz you will meet a case like this.

For your own plan, do not write "AI will let me build this in half the time". Write your time box in hours, and keep a log of your checks. If you want to know your own speed, time yourself on small tasks with and without the tool, and keep the notes. That measures one person on small tasks, and you can say exactly that.

</GoDeeper>

## Speed is not understanding

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-speed-vs-understanding" />

Watch the two scores arrive. Two groups learned the same new library, one with AI help and one without. A quiz straight after gave the AI group a lower score than the other group. The gap was biggest on debugging questions. The AI group was a little faster, but not by enough to count. A reader who asks "how does this part work?" is testing this.

<GoDeeper title="The trial and its limits">

The Anthropic research team ran a randomised trial (people were put in groups by chance) with 52 mostly junior engineers. Each person learned a new Python library. Some had an AI assistant and some did not. The AI group finished a little faster, which was not a clear difference. On the quiz that followed, they scored lower than the other group. The gap was largest on debugging questions.

Read the limits before you repeat it. It was a small group. The quiz came straight after, so it says nothing about what people remember a month later. The assistant was a chat sidebar, not an agent that edits many files. It was one library. People who asked the AI for explanations scored higher than people who handed it the code. But those groups had only 2 to 7 people. The study cannot say that asking caused the higher score.

So here is a fair summary. In one small trial, handing the work to the tool cost some understanding, most of all in debugging. It does not say that AI help always hurts learning.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Why a reader asks how it works">

A reader who sees a project wants to know if you can fix it when it breaks. The quickest test is a question about one part. A reader might ask "How does the matching work?" or "Why did you store the stock this way?"

If you wrote the part, or read it closely and tested it, you can answer. If you pasted it, you cannot.

You can prepare. Pick the hardest part of your project and write three sentences about it in your own words. Then break it on purpose, find the bug, fix it and note what you learned. The companion course, The Founder Interview, practises defending a number or a story aloud. Here the aim is smaller: be able to explain the part you are proudest of.

</GoDeeper>

## A working app alone proves less

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-pile-of-apps" />

Watch what a reader does with a pile of weekend apps. They look alike, so the reader looks at the next one. One project gives the reader three things to ask about: a real user, a number and a decision with its reason. This is an inference, not a measured finding. If a working app is cheap to make, it says less about the maker.

<GoDeeper title="Sireen's project page, before">

Here is Sireen's page as a reader found it. It had screenshots of the screens and one line: "Ride-sharing app built with Flutter in a weekend." That was all.

Now ask what a reader could ask. Who used it? There was no answer. What changed because of it? No number. Why did you match riders and drivers this way? No written reason. What went wrong while you built it? No bug, no log. What was the hardest part? She could not say. Every question had no answer. The code ran, so the page could say "I built it", and nothing more.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What is hard to fake, and why this is an inference">

Here is the reasoning in steps. First, the studies show that results with AI tools vary, and that speed can come without understanding. Second, module 1 showed that when smooth writing is cheap, a smooth sentence says little. Third, if anyone can make a working app in a weekend, a working app says less about the person who made it. We call this an inference. We found no study of how readers weigh AI-assisted code.

What stays hard to fake is a short list. A real person who used the project, and who could say so. A measured result with a baseline and a source. Decisions with reasons, so you can answer "why this way and not that way?". A record of what you checked, such as a test or a bug you found and fixed. An honest note of what AI wrote.

None of these needs a big app. None proves skill alone. Together they let a reader ask questions and get true answers. That is what "you are the product" means here: the reader judges how you think and check, not how many lines of code you wrote.

</GoDeeper>

## What still counts on a project page

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m4-project-page" />

Here is the project page for Areej's planned project, Raqim Stock, a stock tool for a print shop. Tap a chip, then the field it fills. Six chips are evidence a reader can ask about. Three are not: a feature count, speed and looks. The page shows what the finished page will hold, not results she already has.

<GoDeeper title="The six fields, one by one">

**Real user.** The print shop owner, from day one. A person who uses it, not a count of downloads.

**Result.** Stockouts per month. A stockout is a paper type that runs out before it is reordered. The baseline is 5 stockouts in two months, August and September 2025, from the owner's notebook.

**Decision (planned).** Stock updates run in one <GlossaryTerm term="transaction" definition="A group of database steps that either all happen or none of them happen, so two changes made at the same moment cannot leave a wrong number behind." />. The reason is that two sales at the same moment must not break the count.

**Bug fixed (what she will log).** She plans to test two sales at once. If the count goes wrong, she will fix it with a transaction and write the test down. Once she has done it, a reader can ask to see the test.

**AI-use log (what she will log).** The date, what she asked, what she used, what she checked, and what the tool got wrong.

**Hard part.** Reorder levels that never double count. She writes it in her own words, in a few sentences.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Use it on your own project today">

Open your best project. For each of the six fields, write the evidence, or write "none yet". A "none yet" is a to-do, not a failure. If a project has no user yet, say so on the page. Module 5 shows how to show it honestly.

Two fields are cheap to start today, even on an old project. Write three sentences about the hardest part, in your own words. And write down one bug you found and fixed. If you are writing it from memory, say so. A short true note is better than a long polished one that you cannot defend.

</GoDeeper>

## The AI-use log

<MermaidDiagram title="The five parts of a log line" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["The AI gives you code"] --> B["1 Date"]
  B --> C["2 What I asked"]
  C --> D["3 What I used"]
  D --> E["4 What I checked"]
  E --> F["5 One thing it got wrong"]
  F --> G["You can show what you checked"]
`} legend="The five parts of one log line, in a fixed order. If you paste the code and never check it, box 4 stays empty, and box 4 is the part a reader trusts most." />

Read the five boxes in order. Areej plans to log this in week 2. The tool suggests a stock update without a transaction. She tests two sales at once, sees the wrong count and adds a transaction. If you paste code without checking it, you have nothing to write in box 4, and no answer for a reader.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's planned log line in full">

| Date | What I asked | What I used | What I checked | One thing it got wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Week 2, Tuesday (planned) | How to subtract stock when a sale is saved | Its query, wrapped in a transaction | Ran two sales at once on a test copy and compared the stock count | It updated the stock without a transaction, so the count was wrong |

Each column has a job. The date lets someone find the session. "What I asked" shows what you wanted. "What I used" says how much of the answer you kept. "What I checked" is the column most often empty, and it is the one a reader trusts most. "One thing it got wrong" proves you read the answer closely.

If you cannot find anything wrong, do not invent a mistake. Write "nothing found" and say what you tried. A log is a record, not a performance. The course Learn and Build with AI covers how to say when AI helped in your public writing.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Why keep a log at all">

Two reasons. The first is responsibility. A guide to contributing to open source says that you stay responsible for work an AI tool helped to write. If it breaks, it is your change that broke. The second is understanding. In the trial above, the biggest gap was on debugging. A log makes you stop and test before you move on.

Some people believe that if AI wrote the code, they do not need to understand it. The trial points the other way. So does every reader who asks "how does this part work?".

</GoDeeper>
