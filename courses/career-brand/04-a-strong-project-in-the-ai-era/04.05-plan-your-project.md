## Why plan before you build

Chapters 4.2 to 4.4 gave you the ideas. This chapter turns them into one page of your own: a project plan. It takes about 70 minutes, and most people need two sittings. You will not build the project in this course. You plan it, and the plan is what you keep.

There are three reasons to plan first. A plan makes you pass the idea filter before you spend a weekend on code, which is the step Sireen skipped. A plan gives you a user, a number and a date, and those are the things a reader can check later. And a plan gives the next modules something to point to. In module 5 you decide what to show. In modules 6, 7 and 8 you write about work that is done. A plan is not done work, so it never goes on your resume as a result. What it does is tell you which evidence to collect while you build.

The arrows show the order. Each later piece needs something the plan asks for today: a user you can quote, a number with a baseline, and a log of what you checked. If you skip those now, you cannot add them later, because nobody can go back and count what happened in the weeks you did not record.

## Choose your route

There are four routes, and any one completes this chapter.

The first route is your own idea. Use it if you have an idea that you think can pass the four questions. The second route is for people with no idea yet. List three tasks that people near you repeat every week and find annoying. Pick the one where you can count something, such as missed appointments or late replies, and shape the plan like Areej's. The third route is a plan for a first open source contribution instead of a project. It is optional, and the template for it is near the end of this page. The fourth route is to study Areej's plan below and adapt its shape to a problem from your own life. You may use her case for any task in this course, as long as you say that you did. Do not copy her numbers into a plan as if they were yours.

Do not choose an idea because it sounds impressive. Choose one where a person you can name will use it within the next month.

## The template

Copy this into a private note on your phone or laptop. Plain text is fine. Fill each line with your own words, and leave a line empty if you do not know yet.

```text
PROJECT PLAN (one page, private)

1. Title, and one sentence: what it is and for whom

2. User and use: who, doing what, on day one

3. The four questions, one reason each
   a. Useful on day one to one person or a known group, because:
   b. A result I can measure, because:
   c. Depth I can explain in writing, because:
   d. No need for two sides at once, because:

4. Appetite: __ weeks x __ hours a week = __ hours

5. One core flow: step 1, step 2, step 3
   Hours guessed: the flow __, tests and README __, total __
   (the total must be less than the appetite)

6. Out of scope (at least two items), each with a guess in hours:

7. The number to move
   What I count:
   Baseline, and where it comes from:
   How I will measure after:
   What else might change:

8. First real user milestone: who, what they do, by which date

9. AI-use log (one line per session):
   date | what I asked | what I used | what I checked | one thing it got wrong

10. What I will show: a demo account or sign-in, a short demo,
    a README, a case study later

11. Risks (two), and what I will do about each:
```

Each part has a reason, and you have met them all. Part 3 is the idea filter, and every answer needs a reason that names a person, a number or a topic. Part 4 is the time box, and you must multiply the two numbers. Parts 5 and 6 keep the scope inside the box. Add your guesses in hours. What is in scope must add up to less than your appetite, and each item you cut should push the total over it. Part 7 is a claim card written before the result exists: the baseline needs a source, or a plan to get one first. Part 8 needs a date, not a hope. Part 9 is the log from chapter 4.2, with one row as a sample. Part 10 is a note to your later self about what a stranger will see. Module 5 teaches the details, so write only a line for each. Part 11 asks for two honest worries.

## A worked example: Areej's plan

Here is how Areej fills the page, step by step.

For part 1 she writes: "Raqim Stock, a small stock and invoice tool for the owner of a print shop." For part 2 she writes what the owner does. The owner records paper and ink stock and each sale. A warning appears when a paper type falls below its reorder level. On day one the owner is the user.

For part 3 she uses the four answers from chapter 4.3. It is useful on day one, because the owner is the real user. It is measurable, because she can count stockouts per month. A stockout is a paper type that runs out before it is reordered. It has depth to write about, because reorder levels and stock updates must not double count when two sales happen together. It needs no second side, because there is one kind of user. Two other print shops she knows could use it later. That is a small known group, not a marketplace.

For part 4 she writes 6 weeks times about 5 hours a week, which is about 30 hours. For part 5 she writes one flow: record a sale, stock goes down, a warning appears below the reorder level. She guesses 12 hours for recording stock and sales and keeping the invoice export. The low-stock warning sits inside the flow, and she guesses 6 hours for it. The flow is 18 hours. Tests, a README and demo data add 5 hours, so the plan holds 23 hours. That leaves 7 hours spare, because guesses are often too low.

For part 6 she lists supplier ordering, multiple shops and a mobile app, with a guess for each: 10, 14 and 20 hours. Any one of them would push the plan past 30 hours. For example, 23 plus 10 is 33. She does not call these bad ideas. They do not fit the box.

For part 7 she counts stockouts per month. The baseline is 5 stockouts in August and September 2025, from the owner's notebook. After launch, the tool logs each stockout. She compares the two months after launch with the baseline. She also writes down what else changed, such as the season or a new supplier. She does not write a target such as "cut stockouts by half", because before launch she has no evidence for it.

For part 8 she writes that the owner records one real week of sales in the tool by the end of week 3. She adds the calendar date when she starts.

For part 9 she pastes the five column headings. She adds a first line as an example of what she will log, not something already done. The tool may suggest a stock update without a transaction. She will test two sales at once. If the count goes wrong, she will wrap the update in a transaction.

For part 10 she plans a demo account filled with a fake shop and fake stock. She also plans a 90-second demo that starts with the low-stock warning, a README in reader order, and a case study after the build.

For part 11 she writes two risks. The first is that the owner forgets to record some sales, so the stock numbers drift. Her answer is to show the owner on the first day and to compare the tool's count with the shelf at the end of week 3. The second is that two months is a short time, so a change in stockouts could be luck or the season. Her answer is to report what she saw and what else changed, and never to call it proof.

Notice what her page does not have. It has no feature list longer than one flow, no promise of speed, and no number without a source. That is why a reader can ask her any question about it and get a real answer.

## Use AI on your plan, with limits

The rule from chapter 2.5 holds here. An AI tool may restructure and challenge your words, but it may not add a user, a number or a result. This chapter has its own use for it, which is arguing against your plan.

Before you paste anything, remove names, the names of local businesses and any private numbers. Use roles such as "a print shop owner", and paste only numbers that the person agreed you may share. Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys or other people's details.

Then paste your plan and ask the tool to challenge it. Here is a prompt you can copy.

```text
Here is my project plan. Challenge it against four questions: is it useful to
one named person or small group on day one, can I measure a result, do I have
depth to write about, and does it need two sides at once? List the weakest
parts, list two risks I did not write, and suggest cuts that fit my time box.
Do not choose my user, do not invent a baseline or a number, and do not
promise how fast I will finish.
```

The tool may not choose your user, because only you know who you can reach. It may not invent a baseline, because a number you cannot source is the first thing a reader will question. And it may not promise a speed, because the studies in chapter 4.2 show that nobody can. Log the session in part 9: what you asked, what you used, what you checked, and one thing it got wrong.

Then do the edit pass from chapter 2.5 on what it gave you. Remove any number that you did not give it. Keep only the cuts and risks that you can explain in your own words.

## If you have no idea yet, or only a phone

Chapter 2.5 covers the phone path. The plain text template works the same way here: write in your notes app, and dictate with the microphone if typing is slow. You need no laptop and no repository.

If you have no idea yet, use route two above. Ask three people you know what task they repeat every week, and pick the one where you can count something. If you cannot do this now, adapt Areej's plan to a problem you know. A plan modelled on her shape, for your own context, is accepted.

## The open source version

If you prefer a first open source contribution, write this page instead. It is optional.

```text
OPEN SOURCE PLAN (one page, private)

1. Project, and why I think it is maintained (recent activity):
2. The issue, and why it suits a first change:
3. What the contributing guide asks for:
4. My first small step (a comment on the issue saying what I plan):
5. The change: a docs fix or a test:
6. How I will know it helped (merged, a maintainer reply):
7. Time box: __ weeks x __ hours a week = __ hours
8. AI-use log, and what I checked by hand:
```

Parts 1 to 5 follow the five steps in chapter 4.3. Parts 6 to 8 add the number, the time box and the AI-use log. Part 6 is your number: a merged change is a result a reader can see. Keep the change small. A fix that a maintainer can merge in one read is better than a large change that waits.

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is an idea that needs two sides. Sireen's first plan said: "Build a ride app for my whole city with a live map, payments, ratings and chat. I will work on it when I can. The goal is to make getting a ride easier." It has no user she can name, no hours, no number and no first date. It also needs drivers and riders to arrive together. If your filter answer for part 3d is "no", shrink the idea to one side you know, as she did with the school bus schedule.

The second mistake is a scope that is bigger than the hours. Add up your guesses in hours and compare the total with part 4. The third is a number with no baseline, or a baseline with no source. If nobody has counted, write that the baseline comes first. The fourth is a promised result, such as "this will cut mistakes by 80 percent". Write a plan to count it instead. The fifth is a first user such as "small businesses". Name a person, or a group with a size. The sixth is a milestone with no date. The seventh is using AI to fill gaps. If the tool writes a baseline or a user for you, delete it and go and find the real one.

## Check your page

Your plan is ready when all of these are true.

- It passes all four questions, each with a reason, or it is an open source plan.
- The appetite is a number of weeks times hours, and the scope fits it.
- It names one user, one core flow and at least two items that are out of scope.
- The hours guessed for what is in scope add up to less than the appetite.
- The number has a baseline with a source, or a plan to get the baseline first.
- It has a first real user milestone with a date.
- The AI-use log template is on the page.

## Keep it private, and what comes next

Keep the plan in private notes. It is a working document, and it may contain details about a real person that you should not publish. In module 5 you use part 10 to decide what to show, and part 8 to decide who sees the project first. Later, the number in part 7 becomes a claim card if you build it. In module 9 the plan is one of the eight parts of your kit, and a mentor may ask you how you would have found the baseline.
