## People say yes more than you expect

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-more-yes" />

Asking feels riskier than it is. Researchers found that people underestimated how often others say yes to a direct, small request, by as much as 50 percent in some studies. The asker in the picture expects a no and is surprised. This is a reason to send the message. It is not a promise of a reply.

<GoDeeper title="What the studies found">

A team led by Flynn ran six studies, some in real settings. People asked strangers for small, direct help. The askers expected fewer yes answers than they got, and in some studies they were wrong by as much as 50 percent. The researchers explain it simply. Askers think about the cost of helping. They forget how uncomfortable it is to say no to someone's face.

A team led by Brooks studied asking for advice. People who asked were rated as more competent than people who did not ask. The effect was stronger when the task was hard, when the advisor was asked personally, and when the advisor was an expert. So ask about the hard part, after you have tried it yourself.

Keep the limits in view. These were mostly short experiments with small requests. They were not about job offers, and they were not about LinkedIn messages. We found no study that compares job seekers' messages to founders. The result is a good reason to stop waiting until you feel ready. It does not say how often a particular person will reply.

</GoDeeper>

## Start with people you once knew

<MermaidDiagram title="Do you know this person?" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Do you know this person, even a little?"] --> Y["Yes"]
  A --> N["No, a stranger"]
  Y --> Y2["Message them with one specific question"]
  N --> N2["Comment on their post first"]
  N2 --> N3["Ask only after they have seen your name"]
`} legend="Two branches. The stranger branch has one more step before any ask." />

Your first messages go to people you once knew well: teachers, past team leads, clients, classmates. Then write to people you know a little. Strangers come last. A large LinkedIn study found that moderately weak ties helped job moves most. It does not show that messaging strangers works.

<GoDeeper title="What the research says about ties">

A study of executive MBA students found that advice from old contacts compared well with advice from current ones. The students had not spoken to these people for a while, but the advice was still useful, and it was trusted. For you, this means a former teacher or team lead is a better first message than a stranger.

A team led by Rajkumar ran randomised experiments (people were split into groups by chance) on LinkedIn's People You May Know suggestions. They covered more than 20 million people over five years. Ties that were moderately weak helped job moves most. The effect was an inverted U: it rose, then fell, so the very weakest ties helped less. The effect was larger in more digital industries. This is useful for choosing whom to know.

It is not a study of messages. It tested connection suggestions, not what people wrote. So it does not prove that a note to a stranger works. LinkedIn's own page also tells members to invite people they know and trust.

</GoDeeper>

## Free accounts: connections and few notes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-free-account" />

On a free account you can message people you are connected to. For people you are not connected to, you have a few personalised notes each month. LinkedIn's pages say three on one page and five on another, so plan for three and count them. Count what your own screen shows.

<GoDeeper title="Three or five? Trust your screen">

LinkedIn's help page on personalised invitations says free members can add a note to up to three connection requests a month, with a limit of about 200 characters. The help page on invitation limits says five. Both pages were open when we read them, and they disagree (checked 2026-10). The page that was updated more recently says three, so the course plans for three. If your screen shows a different number, follow your screen. When you type a note, read the counter in the box. If it differs from the 200 above, follow the counter.

A free account cannot send InMail, which is a private message to a person you are not connected to and is a Premium feature. It can message only connections. Invitations sent from the People You May Know list in the mobile app cannot carry a note. If you want to add a note, start from the person's profile and check that your screen offers it.

Every member, free or paid, has an invitation limit. Reaching it usually stops you sending invitations for about a week, and withdrawing the waiting invitations does not lift it. Guides that quote a weekly number are guessing, because LinkedIn's page gives none. Keep a short list in your notes: the date, the person and how many notes are left.

</GoDeeper>

## Spend a small note budget

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-spend-the-notes" />

You have three notes this month. Sort eight contacts: use a note, connect only, or comment first. Comment first is the first step for a stranger. The note, if one is left, comes with the request that follows. Never spend a note on a sales message to someone you do not know.

<GoDeeper title="How Areej spends her three notes">

Areej plans for three notes a month and writes each one down. The first goes to her former teaching assistant, whom she has not spoken to for a while. The second goes to an engineer two connections away who commented on a post about PostgreSQL. The third she keeps for a founder, after she has left a useful comment on the founder's post and the founder has seen her name.

Her classmates and her teammate she contacts plainly, because she already talks to them and a note would be wasted. This is the course's method, built on LinkedIn's facts. It is not a LinkedIn rule. LinkedIn's policies add one firm rule: do not use invitations for promotional messages to people you do not know. Amjad broke it by selling his services in forty notes.

</GoDeeper>

## A message a busy person can answer

<MermaidDiagram title="Can a busy person answer this?" chart={`flowchart TD
  M["A busy person opens your message"] --> Q["Is there one clear question?"]
  Q -->|no| X["They close it"]
  Q -->|yes| E["Is saying no easy and polite?"]
  E -->|no| X
  E -->|yes| A["They answer in a minute"]
`} legend="Two questions decide it. A message that fails either one is closed." />

A short message has four parts: who you are in one line, why you chose this person, one specific question, and an easy way to say no. A template with only the first name changed is still the same message sent to many people. A real message could not be sent to anyone else.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's three drafts">

Each draft has a short note for the invitation, and a longer message to send after the person accepts.

**To her former teaching assistant.** Note: "Hello [Name], you were my teaching assistant in my database course. I fixed a slow report query at an internship and have one question about query plans. May I send it?" Message: "Thank you for accepting. At my internship I read a query plan, saw a scan of the whole table and added a composite index. My question: once the scan is gone, what do you check next in a plan? If you are busy, no reply is needed. Thank you either way."

**To an engineer two connections away.** Note: "Hello [Name], I liked your comment on the PostgreSQL index post. I added an index to fix a slow report and have one question about checking the cost on writes. May I ask?" Message: "Thank you for connecting. I ran a query plan on a copy of our staging database. Staging is a private copy of the app used for testing. My report query's execution time fell from 3,840 ms to 46 ms. I did not measure the cost on writes. How would you measure it on a small table before a release? A short answer is plenty."

**To a founder, after a comment.** Note, used only if she has a note left: "Hello [Name], I commented on your post about early backend work. I am a student developer who measures before and after. May I ask you one question?" Message: "Thank you for accepting. I am a final-year student and a full-stack developer. My case study shows how a slow report went from 4.6 s to 0.41 s, and what my lead did and what I did. My question: when you look at a junior developer's work, what do you check first? If now is not a good time, that is fine."

Names stay in brackets, so she can fill them in. Each draft has one question and an easy way to say no. None of them sells anything.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What the evidence says about short and personal">

We found no controlled study of job seekers' messages to founders. What exists is indirect. LinkedIn's own recruiter data says that shorter private messages, and ones sent to one person at a time, got more replies. That is a company claim about recruiters writing to candidates, which is the other direction, and it shows no method. A trial in another setting found that personalised invitation emails got about one and a half times the responses of generic ones, among 344 people invited to a health study. One early paper used an AI tool to rewrite requests for referrals and scored them with a model, not with real readers. The gains were uneven.

Advice about the best day, the ideal length, or a follow-up after exactly three days has no source we could find. Treat these as your own experiments. Pick a time you can keep, keep the message short, and write down what you tried and what happened. Course 7, "Professional & Business Communication", covers etiquette for messages.

</GoDeeper>

## Grow slowly, measure eight weeks

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-eight-week-log" />

No one has a proof of what makes a LinkedIn audience grow. LinkedIn says to share regularly and gives no number. So Areej counts for eight weeks: posts, comments, replies and profile views. The picture shows the counting, not a result. Your log is your own evidence about your own audience.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's rhythm and how to log honestly">

Areej plans one post a week and five useful comments a week. Week 1 is the index story. Week 2 is the cache test. Week 3 is a short post about what she got wrong, which is the stale cache. Week 4 is her published article. In weeks 5 to 8 she keeps the same rhythm with new work.

Every Sunday she writes four numbers: posts, comments, replies to her posts or comments, and profile views as her screen shows them. What a free account can see may differ, so she counts only what she sees. She also writes one line about anything that changed, such as a holiday or a post of hers that someone shared. She does not round numbers. After week 8 she compares weeks 1 to 4 with weeks 5 to 8.

If a number does not rise, that is not a failure. It is an answer about her own audience. Nothing here is promised. The only wrong log is one where the numbers were changed.

</GoDeeper>
