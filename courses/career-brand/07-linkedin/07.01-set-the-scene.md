## Forty identical notes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-forty-notes" />

Amjad is a freelance developer. He builds sites with WordPress and Laravel. His headline says "Passionate developer | Lifelong learner". He pays for one month of Premium, which raised the number of personalised invitations he could send (checked 2026-10; your screen may differ). He sends the same note to 40 founders he does not know. Nobody replies, and LinkedIn restricts his invitations for about a week.

<GoDeeper title="What went wrong with the notes">

Think about the founder who opens Amjad's note. It says nothing about the founder, and it says the same words that 39 other people received. It reads like an advertisement, because that is what it is: Amjad wants to sell his services to people he has never spoken to.

LinkedIn's Professional Community Policies say that invitations must not be used to send promotional messages to people you do not know. LinkedIn's help page on invitation limits says that every member has a limit. Reaching it usually stops you sending invitations for about a week. Withdrawing the invitations that are still waiting does not lift the limit. The same page tells members to invite people they know and trust (all checked 2026-10; if your screen differs, follow your screen).

Premium did not change this. It raised the number of personalised invitations Amjad could write. It did not change the rule about who they may go to, and it did not make the note personal. A note is personal when it could not be sent to anyone else.

Later in this module you will spend your own few notes well. That starts in chapter 7.4. For now, notice only that the length of Amjad's list was the problem, not the cure.

</GoDeeper>

## A group that likes everything

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-like-group" />

Next, Amjad joins a group. The members agree to like each other's posts. His posts now get likes, but from the same few people each time. The founders he wanted to reach do not reply. The likes were real taps and they meant nothing, because a founder cannot learn anything about his work from them.

<GoDeeper title="Why a like group breaks the rules">

LinkedIn's Professional Community Policies say that members must not agree with others ahead of time to like or re-share each other's content. LinkedIn does not use the word "pod" on the pages we read. But this is what a pod is: a group that has agreed to like, comment on or share each other's posts. The policies also say members must not artificially raise engagement with their content (checked 2026-10).

There is a second problem, and it has nothing to do with rules. The likes tell Amjad nothing. If the same six people like every post, he cannot learn which post helped a reader. He has paid for attention in a way that gives him no information.

There is a slower way, and it is the only one this course teaches. You post real work. You write comments that add one useful thing. You write to a few people with a reason. You count what happens for eight weeks. Nobody can promise you a result from it. But it does not put your account at risk, and what comes back is real.

</GoDeeper>
