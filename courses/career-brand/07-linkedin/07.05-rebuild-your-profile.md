## Your turn

In this chapter you make four things. First, you rebuild your LinkedIn profile: headline, About, Featured, Experience, skills, profile address and an Open to Work decision. Second, you write three outreach drafts. Third, you write five comment drafts. Fourth, you plan four weeks of posting and set up a log for eight weeks. All four draw on work you already did: your career arc, your claim ledger, your case study and your resume.

Plan for about 65 minutes, in two or three sittings. Most people need the longest time for the About section and for the outreach drafts. You can write everything in a notes app or a document first and type it into LinkedIn later. Do the work on your own material. If you have no material yet, or you prefer not to use it, use Areej's. She is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative. Say which one you used.

## Why each field matters

Every field on your profile has a different reader and a different job. The headline is read by someone who has seen only your name. About is read by someone who wants to know whether to look further. Featured is where that person checks your proof. Experience shows that your work happened somewhere real. A reader meets them in that order. You will write them in a different order, because it is easier to summarise text that already exists.

Write in this order. Start with About, because it is your longest piece and your ledger feeds it directly. Then write the Experience entries, which reuse your resume bullets. Then set up Featured. Write the headline last. It is a summary of the About section, and if you write it first it fills up with adjectives. Choose your profile address once and then leave it, because your resume and your posts will link to it.

Remember Amjad from chapter 7.1. He had a headline made of moods, forty identical notes and a group that agreed to like his posts. His problem was not a lack of effort. It was that nothing on his profile or in his messages gave a founder a reason to reply. Everything in this chapter is the opposite: one reason, from your own work, with a number that you can source.

### How Areej builds the profile

Areej opens her ledger and her resume first. She starts with About. She copies the two numbers she wants, with their windows and sources, and writes the first paragraph around them. Then she runs the empty sentence edit from chapter 6.4. She reads each sentence and asks whether a stranger could check it or ask about it. She finds one phrase, "passionate about performance", that fails, and she deletes it. What is left is shorter and every sentence survives.

Next she writes the three Experience entries. These are the same bullets as on her resume, and she copies them exactly, so that her resume and her profile say the same thing. For the internship she writes "a parcel delivery company", because her lead said the chart could be public only without the company name. She attaches skills to each entry, and only the ones the entry shows. Then she sets up Featured with the two links, and opens each one on her phone as a stranger would.

Last, she writes her headline from the About section, and reads the counter in the editor. She then asks a classmate, a different one than at the start of the course, to look at her profile on a phone for ten seconds. The classmate remembers three things: "Node.js and PostgreSQL", "makes slow things fast", and "a report that went from 4.6 seconds to under half a second". Her best proof is on the first screen, in the headline and the first lines of About. At the start of the course it was not. The classmate asks one question: "What does p95 mean?" So her one change is to add a few plain words after the first "p95". The words are: 95 out of 100 requests finished within that time. She makes the change and keeps the result in her notes for the gate.

## The second thing to make: three outreach drafts

An outreach draft has two versions. The note is the short text you may attach to a connection request. The message is the longer text you send after the person accepts. Write both, even if you will not spend a note on every person. Copy this card once for each draft.

```
Who: (a role, not a full name)
Tie type: once knew / know a little / stranger, comment first
Why this person: (one reason, taken from something they wrote or did)
The one question:
Note version: (read the counter on your screen; the help page says about 200 characters)
Message version: (sent after they accept)
Easy way to say no:
Channel: LinkedIn note / message after accepting / email or GitHub
Notes left this month before this one:
```

Write three drafts. The first goes to a person you once knew, such as a former teacher, team lead, client or classmate. The second goes to a person you know a little, a moderately weak tie. An example is someone two connections away who wrote about something you also work on. The third goes to a founder or a hiring person. You reach them first through a useful comment on their post. You send the message after they accept your request.

Each draft asks one specific question. Choose a question the person can answer in a minute, and one that you have already tried to answer yourself. "Can I pick your brain?" is not a question. "When you read a query plan, what do you check after the scan is gone?" is one. Areej's three drafts are in chapter 7.4. Notice their shape: one line about who she is, one reason for this person, one question, and a sentence that makes it easy to say no. None of them sells anything.

Count your notes. Plan for three a month, as chapter 7.4 explained, and write the number left on every card. If your screen shows a different number, follow your screen. If a person is not a connection and you have no note left, comment on their post first, send a plain request, and write your message after they accept. You can also reach people by email or GitHub, if their profile offers a way. Do not use any tool that sends invitations or messages for you.

## The third thing to make: five comment drafts

A comment draft is the comment itself, plus one line that says what it adds. Copy this card.

```
Post: (who wrote it, and what it said, in one line)
What my comment adds: (a number, a small true example, or a question)
The comment:
```

Find five real posts from people whose work you read. If you do not have an account, or you cannot find five today, use these five made-up posts.

> Post 1: "I added an index on a status column and the report got faster. Next I want to know what it costs on writes."
>
> Post 2: "My load test passed on my laptop and failed on staging. The laptop had 100 rows and staging had millions."
>
> Post 3: "Our Saturday sign-up form had double bookings until we changed how seats are counted."
>
> Post 4: "We are hiring our first backend developer. What would you want to see in their work?"
>
> Post 5: "I just finished a cloud computing course. Thank you to everyone who helped."

Write each comment in your own words, from your own work. Areej writes hers from hers. For post 1, she writes the comment from chapter 7.3, with the query plan offer. For post 2, she says that her own load test used a seeded database of 10,000 events, and that she would now test against a copy of production-sized data. For post 3, she writes that her club had 9 double-booked seats in the term before its sign-up form, and 1 after. She adds that the club also cut sessions from three a week to two, so she would not give the form all the credit. Then she asks whether anything else changed for them. For post 4, she writes that she would want a before and after number with its window and source, and the part that was theirs. She asks whether that is the kind of evidence the founder trusts. For post 5, she asks which part of the course the writer will try first on a real project.

Each comment adds one thing: an example, a number with its caveat, or a question. None of them says "Great post". None of them sells anything. You write every comment yourself, and no tool posts one for you.

## How to use AI on your own material

An AI tool can help you restructure what you already wrote, and it can find gaps. It cannot supply facts. Paste your career arc and your resume into the tool, with your name, phone, email, address and employer names removed. Ask it for an About draft in two paragraphs and for three headline options. Use a request like this one.

```
Here is my career arc and my resume. Use only the facts and numbers in them. Do not add any number, employer or skill. Write an About section in two short paragraphs: what I do and for whom, two proofs with their windows and sources, one human line, and what I am looking for. Then give three headline options. After the text, list any sentence that is not in my material.
```

Then do three things yourself. Run the empty sentence edit on every sentence. Check each number against your ledger, row by row, and do every sum yourself. Read the whole text aloud and change anything you would not say. If a sentence claims experience that you do not have, delete it. LinkedIn says you are responsible for what generated text says, and its policies forbid misleading statements about your experience or achievements.

Never use any tool that posts, comments, connects or messages for you. LinkedIn's rules do not allow it, and your account could be restricted. Write each comment and each message yourself. A tool may help you check spelling or shorten a draft. The decision about what to send stays with you.

## If you have no account, no laptop or no work yet

If you do not have a LinkedIn profile, write everything in a document. Course 7, "Professional & Business Communication", shows how to create an account later. Your document is complete work for this chapter. Everything here can be done on a phone in a notes app, in short sittings, when the power and the connection allow.

If you have little work yet, use what you have: a study project, a volunteer task, a measured improvement to your own project. Use the "not measured, here is how I would" line from your ledger as a complete Experience bullet. An honest gap is better than an invented number. If you prefer not to use your own material, use Areej's. You can mix the two, if you say which part is which.

Nothing in this chapter needs Premium. If a screen offers you a paid step, skip it.

## Common mistakes and how to spot them

The first mistake is a number with no row. Read your profile and, for each number, name its ledger row. If you cannot, remove the number. The second is a headline of moods. If the first three words are an adjective, rewrite it. The third is a Featured link that fails. Open each link on a phone, signed out if you can, and look at what a stranger sees.

The fourth is a note that sells. Reread each note and ask: would this read as an advertisement to someone who does not know me? If yes, rewrite it as a question or save the note. The fifth is a comment that adds nothing. Cover the post and read your comment alone. If it could sit under any post, it adds nothing. The sixth is the quiet break of a rule, such as joining a group that agrees to like each other's posts, or trying a tool that sends requests for you. Do neither.

## Acceptance criteria

Check your work against this list before you go on.

- [ ] My headline says my role and what I do in its first words, and I wrote it last.
- [ ] My About has two proofs from my ledger, each with its window and its source.
- [ ] Every number on my profile is in my ledger, and I named the row.
- [ ] Featured holds my case study link, or I wrote a dated plan to add it.
- [ ] I have three outreach drafts, each with one specific question and a note version that fits the counter on my screen.
- [ ] I have five comment drafts, and each adds one thing.
- [ ] I have a four-week plan and an eight-week log, and no step uses automation or Premium.
