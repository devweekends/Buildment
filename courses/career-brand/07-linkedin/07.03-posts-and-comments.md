Amjad wanted his posts to reach founders, and he tried shortcuts. In this chapter you learn what LinkedIn itself says about how posts travel, and how to write a post and a comment that are worth reading. Amjad and Areej are fictional fellows, and Areej's numbers are illustrative.

## Each feed is built for one viewer

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-different-feeds" />

LinkedIn builds a different feed for every person, from what is on their profile and what they have read and engaged with. In the picture, the same five posts reach two people in a different order. LinkedIn's engineers published how this works in March 2026. Nobody outside LinkedIn can tell you an exact recipe.

<GoDeeper title="What LinkedIn published">

LinkedIn Engineering describes a feed built from two kinds of signal. One is what members chose to share, such as industry, experience and skills. The other is what each member has read, liked, commented on or scrolled past. It says a model reads more than a thousand of a member's past actions to rank posts, and that it uses professional signals and engagement patterns, never demographic attributes. It also says the feed includes suggested posts from outside a member's network (checked 2026-10).

LinkedIn's Help Center adds that your content is shown to a subset of your connections and followers, depending on how strong the connection is and on their settings.

What does this mean for you? There is no single feed to beat. Two readers of the same post see it in different places, for different reasons. The text of your post and the headline on your profile are among the things the system reads, so say plainly what the post is about. Beyond that, nobody outside LinkedIn knows the exact recipe, and anyone who sells you one is guessing.

</GoDeeper>

## Folklore, and what LinkedIn says

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-folklore-cards" />

Guides say "the first hour decides", "links are punished" and "use exactly three hashtags". None has a first-party source. LinkedIn's own help page is plain: write what your audience relates to, avoid being overly promotional, share regularly, and react and comment on other people's posts. Treat any exact number from a guide as a guess.

<GoDeeper title="Where the folklore may come from">

We cannot say where each claim began. One possible source is old advice. A LinkedIn newsroom post from 2019 said that no content format is favoured, and it suggested no more than three hashtags. That post is seven years old. LinkedIn's 2026 engineering post describes a different system, one that reads the text of a post. So an old suggestion can live on as a rule long after the system it described has changed.

Use three questions on any claim about reach. Who measured it? Is there a page from LinkedIn that says it? Does it ask me to break a rule? A claim that fails the first two is a guess. A claim that fails the third is not worth trying. LinkedIn's own advice passes all three, because it is general, written down and within the rules, even though it gives no numbers.

</GoDeeper>

## A post about real work

<MermaidDiagram title="Four parts of a short post" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["The problem"] --> B["One number, with its source"]
  B --> C["A screenshot"]
  C --> D["What I would change"]
`} legend="A short post about real work. The last box is the part most posts leave out." />

A short post about real work has four parts: the problem, one number with its source, a screenshot, and what you would change. That is enough for a reader to judge it. Module 8 teaches the long version. Here you write the short one, and you write it yourself.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's week 1 post">

Areej's first post tells the index story. She writes it with names removed, as her lead asked.

"The daily orders report at a parcel delivery company's back office was slow. For 95 out of 100 requests it took 4.6 seconds or less (p95, 14 days, team dashboard). The query plan showed a scan over the whole table. I added an index on two columns, and my lead reviewed it and scheduled the release. After: 0.41 seconds (14 days). A front-end release in the same period did not touch this endpoint. What I would change: add a slow-query alert, so the next slow query is found sooner. [The redacted chart.]"

Every number is in her ledger, with its window and its source. She names her part and her lead's part. She says what else changed. She also says what she would do differently, which gives a reader a reason to reply.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Rules for posts, and AI help">

LinkedIn's help page on posts states a length limit. Do not rely on a number from this course or from a guide: your editor shows how much room is left. The same page says LinkedIn may limit the visibility of posts if it detects excessive posting that looks inauthentic, or the use of an automation tool.

If an AI tool helps you draft a post, you read it, you fix it, and you are responsible for it. LinkedIn's User Agreement says so. Course 13, "Learn and Build with AI", shows how to say when AI helped. Never let a tool post for you.

</GoDeeper>

## A comment that adds one thing

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-comment-edit" />

A comment adds one useful thing: a number, a small true example, or a question that shows you read the post. Tap the parts of this draft that add nothing. Each tap says why and shows what Areej would write. The question stays, because it already adds something.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's comment, and how to write yours">

Areej reads a post about adding an index to a PostgreSQL table. Her finished comment is: "On the index point: a composite index cut a report's p95 from 4.6 s to 0.41 s for me (14 days before and after). Did you test on data of the same size? Happy to share the query plan if useful." Before she shares a plan, she removes real table and column names.

Five habits make a comment worth reading. Add one thing the post did not say. Keep it short enough to read on a phone. Keep it true. Write it yourself. Disagree politely and specifically when you disagree. LinkedIn's help page says you can build your reputation by reacting and commenting on other members' posts, and its policies ask members to respond authentically.

This is the course's method, with LinkedIn's advice behind it. We found no study of what makes a comment useful. One study does suggest that people underestimate how good a genuine compliment feels to the person who receives it (Zhao and Epley). So a short, true, specific compliment is welcome. "Great post!" is not specific.

</GoDeeper>

## What the rules forbid

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-rules-forbid" />

LinkedIn's rules do not allow bots or tools that connect, message, comment, like or post for you. They do not allow agreeing in advance to like each other's posts, or fake accounts. Accounts can be restricted or closed. The honest way is slower, and it is the only one this course teaches.

<GoDeeper title="What the pages say">

LinkedIn's User Agreement, in the version effective 3 November 2025, says you agree not to use bots or other unauthorised automated methods. That covers adding contacts, sending messages, and commenting on, liking or sharing posts. It also bars scripts or plug-ins that scrape profiles, any second or false account, and manipulating the algorithms. LinkedIn's page on prohibited software says it does not allow crawlers, bots or browser extensions that automate activity, and that accounts may be restricted or shut down.

The Professional Community Policies say members must not agree with others ahead of time to like or re-share each other's content. That is what a pod does, although the pages do not use the word. The policies also say not to use invitations for promotional messages to people you do not know (all checked 2026-10).

LinkedIn does not say how often accounts are restricted. "Everyone does it" is not a reason, because you cannot know it. The risk falls on your account, which holds the proof you are building.

</GoDeeper>
