## The headline travels with your name

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-headline-travels" />

The headline is the line under your name. LinkedIn shows it on your profile and beside your name in search results, so a reader may meet nothing else. The first words must say what you do and who you do it for. The picture shows one line in two places.

<GoDeeper title="What LinkedIn says about the headline">

LinkedIn's help page says the headline is the line under your name, that it shows in search results, and that it can differ from your job title. By default it is built from your current position. You may rewrite it to promote an area of expertise. The page states no length limit (checked 2026-10).

Some guides give an exact character count for the headline and for About. We could not find such a number on LinkedIn's own pages, so we do not teach one. Do this instead: type your headline in the editor and read the counter on your screen. If the counter says you are over, cut the end, not the start. On a small screen the end of a long line may not show, so the first words must carry the meaning.

A headline is not a place for a mood or a hope. "Passionate" and "open to opportunities" tell a stranger nothing about your work. If you want to say that you are looking for work, LinkedIn has a separate tool for it, which beat 6 covers.

</GoDeeper>

## Write a headline a stranger understands

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-headline-parts" />

Build Amjad's new headline. Tap a chip, then the part it fills: role, stack, or proof habit. Four of the chips fit no part, and the card says why. Notice that no number is invented for him. His headline promises to show the numbers, so he must be able to.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's headline, part by part">

Areej's headline is: "Full-stack developer (Node.js, PostgreSQL). I make slow pages fast and measure it." It has the same three parts as Amjad's. The role comes first, then the tools in brackets, then one sentence about what she does and how she proves it.

The last part comes from her career arc. Her one-line difference is "A full-stack developer who measures before and after, and writes it down." The headline shortens that to "measure it". It holds no number, because a number in a headline has no room for its window or its source. The numbers go in About, where she can give both.

Write the headline last. It is a summary of your About section, and you cannot summarise what you have not written yet. If you write it first, you will fill it with adjectives to fill the space.

Amjad's old headline had two phrases that a stranger cannot use and one that LinkedIn already covers with a feature. "Passionate" and "lifelong learner" describe a mood. "Open to opportunities" is what the Open to Work setting is for.

</GoDeeper>

## About: mission, motivation, skills

<MermaidDiagram title="Four parts of an About section" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Paragraph 1: what I do, and for whom"] --> B["Two proofs with numbers"]
  B --> C["Paragraph 2: one human line"]
  C --> D["What I am looking for"]
`} legend="Two short paragraphs. The proofs come early, the human line comes second, and the last sentence says what you want." />

LinkedIn says About should cover your mission, motivation and skills, in one or two paragraphs. It gives no word target. Follow the picture. Say what you do and for whom. Give two proofs from your ledger. Add one line that only you could write. End with what you are looking for.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's About, written out">

Areej writes two short paragraphs.

Paragraph 1: "I am a full-stack developer (Node.js, PostgreSQL, React) and I make slow things fast. In a six-month internship at a parcel delivery company's back office, I found a slow orders report and added an index. Its p95 response time fell from 4.6 s to 0.41 s (14 days before and after, team dashboard). My lead reviewed the change and scheduled the release. On a four-person university project, I added a 30-second cache. It cut an events endpoint's p95 from 820 ms to 95 ms under the same load test (a laptop, not production)."

Paragraph 2: "Since I was 12 I have kept a notebook of football match statistics. It taught me that an average can hide the one bad match, so I look at the slow tail and not only the average. I am looking for remote work with a small international team that wants someone who measures before and after, and writes it down."

Check what she did. Both numbers come from her ledger (the report p95 row and the load test row). Each has its window and its source. Her lead's part is named. The caveat about the laptop is in the sentence, not hidden. The football line appears once. She keeps "p95" because engineering readers know it. In chapter 7.5 a viewer asks what it means, and she adds a few plain words after it.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Run the empty sentence edit on your About">

In chapter 6.4 you removed every word that a reader cannot check, and looked at what was left. Do the same to your first About draft. Read each sentence and ask: could a stranger check this, or ask me about it? "I am a results-driven professional" fails. "I cut a report from 4.6 s to 0.41 s" passes, because a reader can ask where the number comes from.

If an AI tool helped you draft About, you read it, you fix it, and you are responsible for it. LinkedIn's User Agreement says this about generated content. Its policies also forbid misleading information about your qualifications, experience or achievements. So every number in About must be a number in your ledger.

</GoDeeper>

## Featured is where your proof goes

<MermaidDiagram title="Areej's Featured section" chart={`flowchart TD
  F["Featured"] --> A["The case study link"]
  F --> B["The Campus Board repository link"]
  F --> C["Later: her post"]
`} legend="Three items. The first two exist already. The post is added after she publishes it." />

Featured is a small shelf under About where you place your best proof. Areej puts the case study link first, then the repository for her study project, and later her post. Adding a link, a document or your own post is not described as paid on LinkedIn's page (checked 2026-10). Pinning items from your own profile sections is described as Premium, so we do not use it.

<GoDeeper title="What the free path allows">

LinkedIn's help page lists what you may feature without paying. It names posts you created or shared, articles you published on LinkedIn, and links to outside sites such as a blog or portfolio. It also names uploaded images, documents, presentations and videos. To feature a post, you must have published one first. You need at least two items before you can change their order. A separate page says that pinning items from your own profile sections is available only on some paid plans (checked 2026-10; if your screen differs, follow your screen).

Two cautions. First, a removed item cannot be restored, so keep your own copy of each link and file in your notes. Second, a link in Featured is only as good as the page behind it. Open each one on a phone, as a stranger would, and check that it loads. Module 5 showed how to make a page that survives.

If you have not published a post yet, add the case study link and the repository now. Then write a dated plan in your notes, such as "post one in week 1, add it to Featured in week 1". Featured with two good items is better than five weak ones.

</GoDeeper>

## Experience: accurate, real, with proof

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m7-experience-entry" />

Each Experience entry names a real tie, carries a bullet from your ledger, and has skills attached. Areej reuses the first bullet from her resume, from the same ledger row. In public she writes "a parcel delivery company", because her lead allowed the chart only without the company name.

<GoDeeper title="Accurate names, real ties">

LinkedIn's help page asks you to name organisations accurately and to list real professional ties: employment, contract work, an official internship, volunteer work or board service. It says informal ties, such as being a customer or a user, should not be listed. Areej's entries are an internship, freelance work for a print shop, and volunteer work for a coding club. All three are real ties.

LinkedIn's policies forbid misleading information about your qualifications, work experience or achievements. So there are two rules at once. Be accurate about the organisation, and be careful about what you may share about it. Areej asked her lead before she used the dashboard chart. The answer was yes, with the company name, table names and customer data removed. So her bullets say "a parcel delivery company". If LinkedIn asks you for an organisation name, ask your own lead whether the name may be public. Never type a different name to hide a real one.

You can attach skills to each position, and LinkedIn says a profile can hold up to 100 skills (checked 2026-10). Attach only the skills that the entry shows. Areej attaches PostgreSQL and Node.js to the internship, not React. LinkedIn also supports a profile in a second language. Whether you add one in Arabic is your choice.

</GoDeeper>

## Open to Work and your profile address

<MermaidDiagram title="Who sees Open to Work" chart={`flowchart TD
  O["Open to Work"] --> A["All members: adds the frame, includes people at your current company"]
  O --> B["Recruiters only: privacy at your company is not guaranteed"]
  O --> C["Only you"]
`} legend="Three audiences. The first is the widest. The second is narrower but is not a promise of privacy." />

Open to Work has three audiences. "All members" adds a frame to your photo and includes people at your current company. "Recruiters only" is narrower, but LinkedIn cannot promise privacy from recruiters at your own company. "Only you" tailors job suggestions without showing anyone. If you work somewhere now, choose with care.

<GoDeeper title="Your profile address and your photo">

Your profile address is the link people can type or tap. Choose a short, plain one with your name. LinkedIn's help page says you can change it only a few times in six months. The page we read said five (checked 2026-10; follow your screen). After you change it, the old address stays linked for a while, and later someone else can take it. The address on your resume must keep working, so choose it once and then leave it.

LinkedIn also says that profiles with a photo get up to twice the views. That is LinkedIn's own claim, with no method shown, so treat it as a company claim. A clear, recent photo is a low-cost step, but it is not a proof of anything about your work.

</GoDeeper>
