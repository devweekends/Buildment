## Why you build this now

Moath had good work and a founder who never saw it. His README asked for four steps before it showed anything. His demo was asleep, and then it asked for an account. Nothing was wrong with his code. The path to it was wrong. In this chapter you build the path, so that a stranger can reach your best work in seconds.

You make three things. The first is one case study, written from a row of your claim ledger. The second is a showcase plan for one project. It says how the project starts, how a stranger gets in and how safe the demo is. It also says what the short demo shows, and what still opens when the demo is asleep. The third is a stranger test, in which someone who has not seen your work opens your link on a phone and you record what happens.

Each one feeds a later module. In module 6, your resume links to the case study, and its result becomes a bullet. In module 8, the case study becomes a page on your website. In module 9, a mentor checks that every link opens and that no private detail is visible. So build these as things other people will open, and keep the private notes private.

Plan about 75 minutes. Most fellows need two sittings. Write the case study first, because the showcase plan and the test both use it. Do not wait until the project is finished. A plan and a case study from work you have already done are enough.

## Part 1: the case study

Choose one ledger row with a real before and after. If you may show the work, use your own. If the work belongs to an employer or a client, ask first, and use the version with names removed. If you cannot use your own work, use Areej's, as described below.

Start with the numbers. Write the numbers before and the numbers after first, with the same metric, the same length of window and the same source for both. Everything else in the case study hangs on them. Then write the problem, who felt it, what you did and what the team did, what else changed, what you would do differently, and where to see it. Copy this outline into your private notes and fill it in.

```text
Title: [the result in one line, names removed]

1. The problem, and who felt it:
2. Numbers before (metric, value, window, source, ledger row):
3. What I did, and what the team did:
   I:
   The team:
4. Numbers after (same metric, same window length, same source, ledger row):
5. What else changed in that time:
6. What I would do differently:
7. Where to see it (images, chart, links, names removed):
Permission: who I asked, when, and what they allowed:
```

Here is how Areej fills it. She is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative. Her title is "From 4.6 seconds to 0.41: fixing a slow report with one index". She writes the numbers first, from her ledger rows R1 and R2. Before: the p95 response time of the report was 4.6 seconds over 14 days, from the team dashboard, and the query plan's execution time was 3,840 milliseconds. After: 0.41 seconds over 14 days, from the same dashboard, and 46 milliseconds in the plan.

Next she writes the problem: operations staff open the daily orders report many times a day, and it was slow. Then she writes the split. She found the query, read its plan, chose an index on two columns and wrote the change to the database. Her lead reviewed it, scheduled it for a quiet hour and released it. Then she says what else changed. A front-end release on 20 May did not touch the report. Request counts were 6,300 before and 6,100 after, so traffic was about 3 percent lower. Last, she writes what she would do differently: add a slow-query alert, so the next slow query is found sooner. Her "where to see it" section holds the redacted chart and the two plans as images, with a line saying they are shared with permission.

Notice what she did not do. She added no number that her ledger did not hold. She did not write "we". She did not hide the 20 May release. And she named her company only as "a parcel delivery company", because her lead allowed the chart with the names removed. Her private notes still name the company. The public page does not.

## Part 2: the showcase plan

The showcase plan covers one project, either the plan you made in module 4 or a project you already have. It has five parts, and each part answers a question a stranger would have. Copy the table into your notes and fill the middle column.

| Part | My plan | Done or planned |
|---|---|---|
| Deployable unit and one-command start | | |
| Access path (demo account, or Sign in with Google with name, email and profile only) | | |
| Demo account checklist (below) | | |
| Demo storyboard, 60 to 120 seconds | | |
| Base layer (case study page, screenshot, recording with its size) | | |
| README outline in reader order | | |

The demo account checklist is the part that makes a demo safe for a stranger. Tick a box only when it is true. If a box cannot be ticked yet, write the gap and a plan next to it.

```text
[ ] Fake data only, with no real person's information
[ ] Low rights: cannot reach real email, SMS, payments or paid services
[ ] Spending and rate limits on any paid service it can reach
[ ] A scheduled reset
[ ] No real keys in the repository, only an example settings file
[ ] A visible "this is a demo" line
```

Areej fills the table for Raqim Stock like this. The deployable unit is a container with a one-command start and a README. Her access path is a seeded demo account that can do everything, with Sign in with Google kept for later, asking only for name, email and profile. Her checklist has all six boxes ticked. She has a fake shop with fake stock, and no real email or payments. She has rate limits and a nightly reset. The repository holds only an example settings file, and every page has a demo line. Her storyboard is 90 seconds. It opens on the low-stock warning, then a sale, then the hard part with two sales at once, then the invoice, then a closing card. Her base layer is the case study page and a screenshot, which still work if the demo is asleep. Her README opens with one line, a screenshot and the demo link, then the one command, then how it will be measured, and then what is not done.

Notice that her plan says "planned" where the work is not done. Raqim Stock is not launched. A showcase plan that is honest about what is planned is stronger than one that pretends.

## Part 3: the stranger test

The stranger test finds what you cannot see yourself. You know your project too well to see it fresh. A stranger does not.

Run it like this. Choose someone who has not seen your work, such as a classmate or a relative. Do not choose your mentor. Use their phone, or yours with a clean browser, on an ordinary data connection. Give them only the link. Say nothing else, and give no hints. Start a timer when they tap the link. Stop it at the first useful screen, which is the first screen where they could say what the project does and see a result. Then ask them to say, in their own words, what the project is. Last, ask for two questions they would ask you if you were in the room. Write down everything, including the time, in this record.

```text
Date:
Who (role only, for example "a classmate"):
Device and connection:
The link they opened:
Time to the first useful screen:
What they understood, in their words:
The two questions they asked:
What I will change:
```

Areej ran her test on the case study page, on a classmate's phone. The time to the first useful screen was 8 seconds. The two questions were: "Was this production or a test copy?" and "What did your lead do?" Both questions came from gaps in her page. She had not said that the plan figures came from a copy of a staging database and the p95 figures came from the live endpoint. She also had not made the split of the work obvious enough. She fixed both in the page, and she keeps the answers for the gate.

Be fair to the test. If the link asks for a login, that is a result, so record it and do not fix it in the moment. Do not explain, do not defend, and do not say "it is better on a laptop". If the stranger asks nothing, ask what they would want to know next. If you have no one to test with, ask a classmate by message to open the link and reply with the time and their two questions.

## Use AI on your own material

An AI tool can help you place your material in the outline. It cannot know what you did, and it must not make something up. Paste one of your own ledger rows, with the names replaced by roles. Ask the tool to put the row in the case study outline, and to list the questions a stranger would ask. Tell it plainly not to add any number, result, tool or credit that is not in the row, and to write "missing" where the row has a gap.

When the draft comes back, check every sentence against your ledger. If a sentence has a number that is not in the ledger, delete it. If it gives you credit the ledger does not, or removes your lead's part, fix it. Then do the empty-sentence edit: cut every sentence a reader could not check, such as "a great and powerful solution". The questions it lists are useful for the gate, because your stranger will ask some of them.

Never paste your full name, phone, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles, such as "a parcel delivery company" or "my lead".

## If you have no work to show, or no laptop

Use Areej's case study. Copy her outline into your notes, read her numbers, and write her case study out in your own words as practice, labelled "Areej's case study, a fictional example". Then make your own showcase plan for the project you planned in module 4. Run the stranger test on the case study.

If you have no laptop, do all of it on your phone in a notes app. A link that opens without a login is enough for the test. A repository README or a shared page will do. If you cannot make a link, hand a stranger your phone with the page already open, and record the time as before. If you cannot deploy anything, the no-hosting path from chapter 5.3 counts: a case study page, screenshots and a short recording.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is opening with history, such as why you chose the project. A reader needs the problem and the result first. The second is a number that is not in the ledger, often a round one added at the last minute. The third is "we", which hides your part, or the opposite, which hides your lead's. A fourth is mixing sources: a plan from a test copy presented as a live result.

Watch for private details in images. A company name can sit in a browser tab, a file name or a corner of a chart. A key can sit in a screenshot of a settings page. Also watch the test itself. Running it on your own phone with the page already stored, or answering the stranger's questions before they finish asking, will give you a time that is too good.

## Acceptance criteria

You are done when you can tick all of these.

- The case study has every section, and every number appears in your ledger.
- Your part and the team's are split, and permission and removed names are respected.
- The showcase plan covers all five parts, and the demo checklist is complete or each gap is named.
- The stranger test is recorded with a time and two questions.
- No real key, secret or customer data appears anywhere.

## Where to keep it, and what comes next

Keep the outline, the plan, the checklist and the test record in your private notes. Keep the permission message too. The case study page itself may be public once it is clean. Module 6 puts its link and its one-line result on your resume. Module 8 turns it into a page on your website.
