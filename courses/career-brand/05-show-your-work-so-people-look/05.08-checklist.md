## Your task: report your stranger test and defend your case study

This task checks what the module is about. Can a stranger reach your work and understand it? And can you answer the two questions every reader has: "how was that measured?" and "what was your part?" The stranger test shows how easy your work is to reach. The case study shows whether you can explain what you did. The safety list shows whether you can be trusted to put something in front of strangers.

Write about 130 words. Say how long the stranger took to reach the first useful screen, and on what device. Say what they understood, in their words. Give the two questions they asked. Paste the link to your case study, or its text with the names removed, and name the ledger row that each number comes from. Then list which of the six demo controls your showcase plan has, and name any gap. A named gap with a plan is a good answer. A hidden gap is not.

Here is a strong answer from a fictional fellow. Fadwa is a developer who wrote a case study about a script that made a weekly stock report faster for a shop. She wrote: "A classmate opened the case study on her phone, with no help from me. She reached the chart in 12 seconds. She said I made a weekly report quicker and timed it. Her two questions were whether anyone else changed the spreadsheet in those weeks, and why I used a median. The report took 45 minutes before and 6 minutes after, median of five weeks each, from my own timing sheet, ledger row L2. I wrote the script. The shop owner chose the columns. My demo has fake data, low rights, a reset, no keys and a demo line. The gap is a rate limit. I will add it before I share the demo."

Look at the shape of her answer. It has a time and a device. It says what was understood and gives two real questions. It has a number with its source and ledger row, and it splits her part from the owner's. It names a gap with a plan. A mentor can check every part of it.

Here is a weak answer. "My stranger test went well. They liked it. My case study has all the sections and everything in it is true. My demo is safe and I have no gaps." It gives no time, no device and no questions. "Everything is true" cannot be checked, and "no gaps" is rarely honest. Asked "how was that measured?" or "what was your part?", the writer has nothing to say. A mentor cannot help with a gap that was not named.

If you prefer to use Areej's case, use her stranger test on the case study page. She is a fictional fellow. She used a classmate's phone and reached the first useful screen in 8 seconds. The two questions were "was this production or a test copy?" and "what did your lead do?". The facts for her answers are in chapters 5.5 and 5.6. Her request to the AI interviewer was refused in this drill, so she took the written option. Her case is the worked example for that option.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer part by part. Each part is Met, Partly or Not yet.

**Part 1: Case study complete.**

- **Met:** The link or text shows the problem, the numbers before and after with the same source and window, your part and the team's, what else changed, what you would do differently and where to see it. Every number names a ledger row.
- **Partly:** One section is missing, or one number has no ledger row.
- **Not yet:** There is no case study.

**Part 2: Stranger test.**

- **Met:** A time, what the stranger understood and the two questions are all stated.
- **Partly:** Two of the three are stated.
- **Not yet:** The test was not run.

**Part 3: Safety.**

- **Met:** All six demo controls are listed as present, or each missing one is named with a plan. No key, secret, customer data or employer name is visible in the case study or in your answer.
- **Partly:** One gap is not named.
- **Not yet:** A key, a secret, private data or an employer name is visible.

**Part 4: Defence (drill or written).**

- **Met:** The drill is finished, or both stranger questions and the probe are answered.
- **Partly:** One of the three is answered.
- **Not yet:** It is missing.

**Pass rule:** every part is at least Partly, and "Safety" is Met. If a real key or private data is visible, your mentor will ask you to remove it. A key that was ever visible must be treated as stolen and replaced. Your mentor will not copy it into the reply.

Your mentor then replies with one to three questions. They might ask: "Is the number from production or from a test copy?" Or: "What did your teammate or lead do that the case study does not say?" Or: "If the demo is asleep tomorrow, what will a reader still see?" You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks you to walk through your case study. It follows up on how each number was measured and whether it came from production or a test copy. It asks what your part was, and what a reader sees if the demo is asleep. It ends with feedback: your strengths, what to do better, named flags and next steps, including one thing to measure or build next. The five flags are vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, and jargon without explanation. Paste the text of your case study, about 150 to 300 words, with names removed. Or paste Areej's case study from chapter 5.5.

Answer honestly. "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers. The drill is practice, not a hiring decision, and its feedback can be wrong. AI tools tend to agree with you and to like long answers, so check every point against your own work. The drill gives no score. Read the level word that starts each note: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. If your request is refused, as Areej's was, use the written option. Do not use a VPN or any other workaround.

Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead, such as "a parcel delivery company" or "my team lead". If you do not have a key or credit, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "Your case study, questioned"
opening: "Walk me through your case study. What was the problem, and how do you know your change fixed it?"
focus: [case-study, best-technical]
context: project
maxTurns: 8
whyDepth: 3
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a case study that states the problem and who felt it, numbers before and after from the same source and window, the learner's part apart from the team's, what else changed, what they would do differently and where a stranger can see it. Probe each number: 'How did you measure it? Over what window? Where can I see it?'. Ask whether the numbers came from production or a test copy, what a reader sees if the demo is asleep, and whether names were removed with permission. If the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'; on a technical choice ask 'What was the alternative, and why not that?'. Accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would measure next; never push the learner to guess or invent. If pasted text looks like a key or private data, say to remove it and stop that thread. Judge features, not length, and quote the learner's words. Begin each focus-area note with a level word: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Next steps: one thing to measure next or one small thing to build next around the learner's own needs, never a named project idea. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person."
```
