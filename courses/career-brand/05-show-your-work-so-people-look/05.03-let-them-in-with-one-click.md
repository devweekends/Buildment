Moath's founder met a sign-up form before the app. This chapter is about the way in. You will compare a sign-up path with one button. You will learn the smallest request Sign in with Google can make. And you will learn what makes a demo login safe for a stranger. Areej and the people in the pictures are fictional.

## Every extra step is a door to leave

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m5-extra-steps" />

Watch the same reader take both paths. A sign-up path has four steps: sign up, confirm your email, fill in a profile, then the app. Each step before the app is a door to leave by. One button has none. The app behind both paths is the same. Only the number of steps changed.

<GoDeeper title="Two ways in, and which to pick">

This course has one rule here: let people in with one click. There are two ways to do it.

The first is Sign in with Google. A reader taps one button and arrives with their own Google account. The second is a demo account. A reader taps "Try the demo", or uses a login printed on the page, and arrives in an app full of fake data.

For a showcase, a demo account is usually the better first choice. It needs nothing from Google, it works for a reader who does not want to share any account, and you control everything inside it. Sign in with Google suits an app that will have real users later. Areej plans the demo account first for Raqim Stock, and keeps Google sign-in for later.

Do not quote a number for how many visitors a sign-up wall costs. Shoppers do say forced account creation is one reason they leave a checkout. That is a claim by a company about shopping carts, and we found no study of it for demos. The safe sentence is the one in the picture: every extra step is a door to leave by.

</GoDeeper>

## Sign in with Google, smallest request

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m5-google-consent" />

Watch what Google sends back. Sign in with Google lets a person enter your app with their Google account. If your app asks only for their name, email address and profile picture, the consent screen shows no warning and needs no list of test users. Ask for the smallest request. A bigger request brings the limits back (checked 2026-10).

<GoDeeper title="What Google says (checked 2026-10)">

This is a snapshot of Google's documentation. Check before you rely on it, and trust your own screen.

Sign in with Google returns an ID token, which tells your app who signed in. Signing in is a separate step from asking for Google data later. New web apps should enable FedCM, a newer way for the browser to handle sign-in. Behind it is the OAuth 2.0 standard, where your app never sees the person's Google password. The browser goes to Google, a short-lived code comes back, and your app trades the code for tokens.

While an app is in testing mode, you can list up to 100 test users, and their permission expires after 7 days. The exception is an app that asks only for name, email and profile. That app needs no test list, shows no warning and has no 7-day expiry. Once an app is in production, any Google user can sign in. Asking for sensitive or restricted data needs verification by Google. An unverified app that needs it is capped at 100 new users over the life of the project.

So the idea that Sign in with Google needs a long approval is a myth, when you ask only for name, email and profile.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Will it work from where you are?">

We have not confirmed that every developer can set up Sign in with Google from every place. Google's own pages do not list countries for it, and we could not verify whether a basic sign-in needs a billing account.

So test it early, before you plan around it. Try the setup on your phone or laptop in a quiet hour. If the Google console blocks you, ask your mentor. If it still does not work, use a demo account. It gives the reader the same instant way in.

Never use a VPN or another trick to get past a block. It can break the service's rules, and it puts your account at risk.

</GoDeeper>

## A demo login that can do everything

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m5-demo-login" />

Watch the stranger tap once. A "Try the demo" button opens the full app on a fake shop with fake stock. The stranger can try every core feature. Nothing is hidden from them. The safety comes from the data and the rights around the account, not from hiding features. A demo login is a door that strangers will push on.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's demo account plan for Raqim Stock">

Areej's Raqim Stock is a stock and invoice tool for a print shop. Her plan for the demo account has these parts.

The demo opens on a fake shop with fake paper and ink stock. The account can do everything the owner can do inside the app. It cannot send real email or take real payments, because nothing in the demo is connected to those services. The data resets every night, so a stranger who breaks it cannot break it for long. Rate limits stop anyone from filling the demo with junk. The repository holds no real keys, only a file of example settings. A line on every page says "this is a demo".

Separately, she keeps Sign in with Google for later, asking only for name, email and profile.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Why 'a shared password in the README is fine' is half true">

It is true only when the account is a throttled, seeded, low-rights demo with no real data behind it. If any of those is missing, it is not fine.

Think of the password as public from the start. Anyone can read it, so a secret password is not the protection. The protection is that the worst a stranger can do with the account is small. That is course reasoning built on OWASP's guidance, and the next beat shows the controls one by one.

The visible "this is a demo" line has a job too. It tells a visitor that the data is fake. It stops a well-meaning reader from typing their real details into a page they think is a product.

</GoDeeper>

## Remove a control, see the risk

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m5-remove-a-control" />

Each control stops one kind of harm and no other. Sort the eight things a stranger could do into the control that stops each one. Fake data, low rights, limits with a reset, and keys kept out of the repository. Leave one out, and that harm stays open. Add a visible "this is a demo" line as well.

<GoDeeper title="The checklist, and what OWASP says (checked 2026-10)">

The checklist is this course's method, built from two OWASP guides: the Authentication Cheat Sheet and the Secrets Management Cheat Sheet.

The data is seeded and fake, with no real person's information. The account has low rights, so it cannot reach real email, SMS, payments or paid services. Any paid service has spending and rate limits. The data resets on a schedule. No real key is in the repository, only an example settings file. The page says plainly that it is a demo.

From the authentication guide: slow down repeated wrong guesses, and tie any lockout to the account, not to the address the guesses come from. Allow long passphrases. Check passwords against lists of leaked passwords. Multi-factor sign-in is the best single defence against password attacks. And do not give internal service accounts a login on the front end.

A shared demo login cannot use multi-factor sign-in, because strangers have no second factor. That is one more reason the safety must come from what the account can reach. If your app also has real accounts, turn multi-factor sign-in on for those.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Keys: the one control you cannot undo">

OWASP's secrets guide says to keep secrets out of source code and repositories. It says to rotate and revoke a secret that may be exposed, and to give each job the least access it needs. It also says to be careful with environment variables, because they can leak into logs and dumps. Restarting an app does not revoke a stolen secret.

So the rule is the one to remember: never put a real key in your code. If a key was ever pushed, assume it is stolen and replace it.

Areej learned this on Campus Board, a four-person study project. A teammate had pushed an email-service key in an early commit. Deleting the file would not remove it from the history, so she revoked the key and made a new one. She moved the secrets to environment variables, added a `.env.example` file with placeholder values, and turned on the code host's push protection. Push protection blocks known kinds of secrets before a push (checked 2026-10). It helps before a push. It does not repair a key that is already out.

Her teammate agreed and reviewed the change. Notice that the fix has a proof a reader can check without seeing the key: the revocation and the setting.

</GoDeeper>

## When you cannot deploy at all

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m5-no-hosting" />

Some fellows cannot deploy: no laptop to install tools, or a host that will not load. Watch the phone open the page. A static case study page with screenshots, and a short recording with captions, still counts as a showcase. Nothing there can be asleep or ask for a login. A live demo is a bonus.

<GoDeeper title="The no-hosting path">

A reader needs something that opens on a phone, with no account and no wait. You can build that without a server.

Write the case study as a page: the problem, the numbers before and after, what you did, and images. Any link that opens without a login can hold it, such as a repository README with pictures. Take the screenshots from your phone. Record the screen with your phone's recorder, keep the recording short, and add captions.

In your showcase plan, write "not deployed" honestly, and show the base layer instead. A reader who sees an honest "not deployed" and a clear case study learns more than one who finds a dead link. Chapter 5.4 covers the recording, and chapter 5.5 covers the case study.

</GoDeeper>
