The case study is the base layer of your showcase. It still opens when the demo is asleep. This chapter builds one from a row of the claim ledger you made in module 3.

## A postmortem, but for a success

<MermaidDiagram title="An incident report beside a case study" chart={`flowchart TD
  I1["Incident report: summary and impact"] --> C1["Case study: the problem, and who felt it"]
  I2["Incident report: root cause and resolution"] --> C2["Case study: what I did, and what the team did"]
  I3["Incident report: lessons"] --> C3["Case study: numbers after, and what I would change"]
`} legend="Each field of an incident report has a partner in a case study. Both say what was wrong, what it cost, what changed and how you know." />

After an outage, engineers write an incident report: what happened, what it cost, why, and what to learn. A case study is a postmortem for a success. It says what was wrong, what it cost, what you changed and how you know it worked. Google's engineers write incident reports in this shape, and so can you.

<GoDeeper title="Where the shape comes from">

Google's Site Reliability Engineering book includes an example postmortem. Its fields are status, summary, impact, root causes, trigger, resolution, detection, action items, lessons learned, timeline and supporting information. The book also describes a blameless style, which looks for the contributing causes of a problem and does not blame people.

We use this only as an analogy. We found no standard for how a portfolio case study should be built. The order in this chapter is the course's own method, adapted from the postmortem idea. It starts with the problem and who felt it, then the numbers before, with source and window. Next come what you did and what the team did, then the numbers after, with the same source and window. It ends with what else changed, what you would do differently, and where to see the work.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Areej's case study, title and sections">

Areej's case study is about the slow orders report she fixed during her internship. Her public title is "From 4.6 seconds to 0.41: fixing a slow report with one index".

The problem section says that operations staff open a daily orders report many times a day, and that it was slow. The numbers before give the p95 response time of 4.6 seconds over 14 days, from the team dashboard, and the query plan's 3,840 milliseconds. The p95 is the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within. The next section says what she did and what the team did. The numbers after give 0.41 seconds over 14 days, and 46 milliseconds in the plan. Then come what else changed, what she would do differently, and where to see it: the chart and the two plans, as images.

You will build a case study like this in chapter 5.6.

</GoDeeper>

## Build it from a ledger row

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m5-case-from-row" />

A case study is built from ledger rows. Tap a snippet, then the section it fills, or drag it. Six sections take six snippets. Three snippets fit nowhere: a boast, a vague percentage and a "we". No new number appears. The case study only arranges numbers the ledger already holds.

<GoDeeper title="The seven parts, in order">

Start with the problem and who felt it. A reader needs a person and a pain before a number. Then give the numbers before, with their source and the window they cover. Then say what you did and decided, and what the team did. Then give the numbers after, from the same source and over the same window. Then say what else changed in that time. Then say what you would do differently. Last, say where to see it: the images, the chart and any link.

Notice the order of the numbers. The numbers before and after sit on either side of what you did, so a reader sees the change and then the cause. Write the two sets of numbers first. The rest of the page hangs on them.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Areej's two ledger rows">

Row R1 is a query plan pair. Areej ran the plan on her laptop, on a copy of a staging database. Staging is a private copy of the app, used for testing. Her copy held the same amount of data as the real one. Before, the plan was a scan of the whole table, with an execution time of 3,840 milliseconds. After, it was an index scan, with an execution time of 46 milliseconds. She quotes only the actual time figures, because the cost units in a plan are not milliseconds.

Row R2 is the report's p95 response time from the team's dashboard, which her lead owns. Over the 14 days before (1 to 14 May 2025) it was 4.6 seconds. Over the 14 days after (16 to 29 May 2025) it was 0.41 seconds. The request counts in the two windows were 6,300 and 6,100, so traffic was about 3 percent lower after, and does not explain the change. A front-end release on 20 May did not touch the report.

Keep the sources apart in the page. The plan pair comes from a test copy. The p95 figures come from the live endpoint. A reader will ask which is which.

</GoDeeper>

## Your part and the team's, in writing

<MermaidDiagram title="Two ways to answer 'what was your part?'" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q["The reader asks: what was your part?"] --> W["'We fixed it'"]
  Q --> V["Verbs for each person"]
  W --> D["The reader cannot tell who did what"]
  V --> T["Each verb can be checked against the ledger"]
`} legend="A blurred 'we' leaves the question open. Verbs for each person close it." />

A reader will ask, "What did your lead do?" Answer it before anyone asks. One section splits the work. Areej found the query, read the plan, chose the index and wrote the migration. Her lead reviewed it, scheduled it and deployed it. Say both plainly. Naming the team's part makes your own part easier to believe.

<GoDeeper title="How to write the split">

Use a verb for each thing. "I found the query in the slow query log. I read its plan. I chose an index on two columns. I wrote the change to the database." Then: "My lead reviewed the change, picked a quiet hour and released it."

Do not write "we fixed it". Do not write "I fixed everything" either. Both fail the question "what was your part?". A plain split passes it, and a mentor can check each verb against the ledger row.

If you worked alone, say so, and say who else had a say. Areej wrote the invoice script on her own, and the shop owner chose the invoice fields. That sentence is honest and short.

In Areej's stranger test, a stranger asked "what did your lead do?". The case study should have answered it already.

</GoDeeper>

## What you would do differently

<MermaidDiagram title="Two paths from the same slow query" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["A query becomes slow"] --> N["Today: someone reads the slow query log"]
  S --> Y["With an alert: the alert fires"]
  N --> L["Found when someone happens to look"]
  Y --> E["Found sooner"]
`} legend="The change is the second path. It is tied to how the first path worked." />

A case study ends with a change you would make. Areej found the slow query by looking in the slow query log. Next time she would add a slow-query alert, so the next one is found sooner. A change like this shows that you iterate. It is one sentence, and it is tied to what happened.

<GoDeeper title="Why this section matters">

A company that publishes its hiring process says its interview looks for proactivity, directness, communication, awareness of impact and iteration. Iteration means you improve a thing after you learn from it. A "what I would do differently" section is the cheapest way to show it.

Choose a change that follows from the story. "I would work harder" does not. "I would add an alert", "I would test on a copy of production data" or "I would measure the customers' side" do. If you have an honest gap, such as a result you never measured, this is the place to say how you would measure it.

Keep the change small and real. One clear sentence is stronger than three vague ones.

</GoDeeper>

## Permission and removed names

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m5-redacted-chart" />

Watch what is kept and what is removed. Ask first. Areej asked her lead before using the dashboard chart. The answer was yes, with the company name and the real table and column names removed, and no customer data. In public she writes "a parcel delivery company". The numbers stay. A line under the chart says it is shared with permission.

<GoDeeper title="How to ask, and what to check">

Ask in writing, so you have the answer. Keep it short: "May I show this p95 chart in my portfolio, with the company name and the table names removed and no customer data? I can send you the page first."

Before you share the image, check what else shows. Look at the browser tab, the address bar, the file name and any small labels in the chart. A name hidden in a corner is still a name.

If the answer is no, or you hear nothing, do not use it. Show a measurement you made yourself instead, as chapter 3.5 describes, or write the case study about a project you own. Your private notes, such as your career arc and ledger, may name the employer. Anything public may not, unless you have permission. The same rule applies to text you paste into the AI drill in the gate.

</GoDeeper>

## It still works when the demo sleeps

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m5-demo-asleep" />

Watch the phone try the demo first. It is asleep. The case study page opens at once, with the before and after plans and the chart. That is why it is the base layer. It also becomes a page on your website in module 8 and a resume link in module 6.

<GoDeeper title="Make the page work on a phone">

Put the title and one line with the result at the top, for example the before and after numbers. Put the chart next. Then the sections in the order above. Keep the images small enough to load on weak data, and give each image a short text description.

A static page can still be moved or deleted. Keep a copy of the images and the text in your own files. Open the link on your phone before you put it on a resume. In chapter 5.6 a stranger will do the same, and the time it takes to reach the first useful screen is your result.

</GoDeeper>
