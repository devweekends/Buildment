## Your task: compare your old resume and your new one

This task shows that your new resume is built on proof. In chapter 1.6 you recorded a baseline. Now you write down what changed, what is still weak, and where each strong line comes from. An honest "this line is still weak" is worth more than a claim that everything is fixed.

Write about 150 words. First, say the biggest change between your old resume and your new one. Second, name one flag or weak line that remains, and say what you will do about it. Third, for your three strongest bullets, name the ledger row each one comes from. Fourth, say how you ran the paste test and what order came out, and say that you opened each link on a phone.

A model answer comes from Khalood, a fictional data analyst. She wrote: "The biggest change is the top of the page. My old resume began with an objective paragraph and a block of 25 tools. The new one begins with my report bullet. One flag remains. The drill marked vague ownership on my third bullet, where I wrote that we moved the reports to a new tool. I will name my part, which was writing the migration script, and the team's part, which was choosing the tool. My three strongest bullets come from rows L1, L2 and L4. I pasted the PDF into my notes app and the order stayed the same: my name first and each bullet under its job. I opened all three links on my phone in a private window. One said 'portfolio', so I relabelled it 'Case study: monthly report from 6 hours to 2'."

Notice what a mentor can check. The change is specific and tied to the top of the page. The remaining flag is named with the exact bullet, and the fix names her part and the team's part. Each strong bullet has a row. The paste test and the phone check say what was done and what came out. She did not say the resume was perfect.

Compare it with a weak answer. "My new resume is much better. It is cleaner and uses strong action words. All the numbers are true and the links work. I will keep improving it." It names no change a mentor could look for, no remaining flag, no ledger row and no paste test result. A mentor cannot tell which numbers were checked or against what. It fails the same question a founder would ask: "how do you know?".

If you prefer to use Areej's case, here are her facts. Her old resume had two pages, an objective paragraph an AI tool wrote, a block of 22 technologies and three weak bullets. At the baseline in chapter 1.6, the drill marked vague ownership on "Worked on backend performance" and unsupported number on "Improved speed by 90%". Her new resume has one page. The objective paragraph and the technology block are gone, and the report bullet leads. Her three strongest bullets are the report bullet (rows R1 and R2) and the invoice bullet (row R3). The third is the leaked-key bullet (row R6), which has no number and names three checkable acts. The paste test kept the order. Her links are "Case study: a slow report from 4.6 s to 0.41 s", "Campus Board load test (GitHub)" and her LinkedIn profile, and she opened each on a phone. For a backend posting at a small logistics software startup she moved Campus Board above Volunteer. She changed the summary to start "Backend-leaning full-stack developer", and she copied none of the posting's words. At the new drill, the unsupported number flag is gone and the vague ownership flag is gone. One flag is new: jargon without explanation, on "composite index" (an index on two columns), which she keeps for engineering readers and explains in her case study.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer part by part. Each part is Met, Partly or Not yet.

**Part 1: Ledger mapping.**

- **Met:** Every resume line that carries a number or a result names a ledger row. Education and dates count as plain facts.
- **Partly:** One or two lines have no row.
- **Not yet:** Three or more lines have no row, or a number has no row at all.

**Part 2: Bullets.**

- **Met:** Your three strongest bullets each show what you owned and the change, with its before, after and window. When there is no number, they show a checkable action. Each shows your share. None begins "responsible for".
- **Partly:** Some bullets miss one of those parts.
- **Not yet:** The bullets are duties and adjectives.

**Part 3: File and links.**

- **Met:** You passed the paste test and say what order came out. Every link is labelled and was opened on a phone.
- **Partly:** One problem, such as one unlabelled link or one link not tested.
- **Not yet:** The file cannot be read as text, or a link is broken.

**Part 4: Tailoring.**

- **Met:** The tailored copy changes at least two of: the first bullets, the project order and the summary line. Each change names its ledger row, and no words are copied from the posting.
- **Partly:** There are changes, but no ledger row is named for them.
- **Not yet:** Posting keywords were copied, or nothing changed.

**Part 5: Comparison.**

- **Met:** You name the module 1 flags and today's flags, and say which are gone and which remain. Or, if you did not do the drill, you defend three lines in writing.
- **Partly:** You give one side of the comparison only.
- **Not yet:** The comparison is missing.

**Pass rule:** every part is at least Partly, and Ledger mapping must be Met.

Your mentor then replies with one to three questions, usually about your top bullets. They might ask what your part was in one of them, or where a number comes from. You answer in writing or in a voice note. Your answer to it finishes the gate, so check for it once you have submitted.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks about the first line of your new resume. It follows up with questions like "how did you measure it?" and "what was your part?", and it asks how you chose the order of your lines. It uses the same probes as the baseline drill in chapter 1.6, so you can compare the flags line for line. It ends with feedback: your strengths, what to do better, named flags and next steps, including one thing to measure or build next. The flags it names are the five flags from gate 1.6. Paste your new resume, without the tailoring if you like.

Answer honestly. "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers. A technical term kept for engineering readers is fine if you can explain it. The drill is practice, not a hiring decision, and its feedback can be wrong. AI tools tend to agree with you and to like long answers, so check every point against your own words. The drill gives no score. Read the level word that starts each note: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. Copy the flags into your learning log and compare them with the ones from module 1. For practising aloud, course 14, "The Founder Interview", is the place to start.

Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead, such as "a parcel delivery company" or "my team lead". If you do not have a key or credit, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "Your new resume, line by line"
opening: "Pick the first line of your resume. What exactly did you do, and how do you know it worked?"
focus: [resume, ownership, impact]
context: resume
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 4
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows resume lines that each name what the learner owned, the change with a before, an after and a window, how it was measured and their own share, with no empty phrases. Use the same probes as the first resume interview, so flags can be compared: after any number ask 'How did you measure it? Over what window? Where can I see it?'; if the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'; on a technical choice ask 'What was the alternative?'. Ask how the order of the lines was chosen for a reader. Accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would measure next; never push the learner to guess or invent. A technical term kept for engineering readers is fine if the learner can explain it. Judge features, not length, and quote the learner's words. Begin each focus-area note with a level word: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Next steps: one thing to measure next or one small thing to build next around the learner's own needs, never a named project idea. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person."
```
