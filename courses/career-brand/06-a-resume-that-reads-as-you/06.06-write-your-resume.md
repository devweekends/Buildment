## Your turn

You now have the pieces. You know how to order a resume by strength of proof (chapter 6.2). You know how to turn a ledger row into a bullet (6.3) and how to use AI without letting it add anything (6.4). You also know how to add links and tailor to one posting (6.5). In this chapter you put them together and write your own resume.

The resume comes late in the course for a reason. A resume written first is a list of things you hope are true. A resume written from your claim ledger is a list of things you can show. Every number in it has a row behind it, and every link goes to something from module 5. That is why a reader can question any line and you can answer.

What you write here feeds later work. Module 7 rebuilds your LinkedIn profile from this resume and the ledger. Module 9 asks you to defend any line of it. The gate for this module runs the AI interviewer on your new resume and compares its flags with the baseline from module 1, so keep your old resume file. Plan for about 80 minutes. Most fellows need two sittings, and that is fine.

If you do not have your own ledger rows yet, use Areej's. Her numbers are illustrative. You may do the whole chapter with her material and say that you did. A resume built from Areej's rows teaches the same skills, and you can swap in your own rows as you earn them.

## What you produce

You produce three things. The first is a one-page resume in a file that passes the paste test. The second is a mapping table that shows, for each line, the ledger row behind it. The third is a tailoring record for one copy of the resume that you fit to a real posting. Keep the table and the record in your private notes, not on the resume and not in public.

Work along the second path. The ledger comes first, then the three strongest bullets, then the outline, then the file, then the tailored copy. An AI tool is optional on that path, and it only touches the structure, never the facts.

## Template one: the resume outline

Copy this into your own notes and fill it in. It is the order from chapter 6.2.

```text
NAME
Links line: [label 1: address] | [label 2: address] | [LinkedIn: address]

SUMMARY (two lines, from my "what makes me different" line)

EXPERIENCE
[Role, organisation described by what it is, dates]
- [strongest bullet first]
- [second bullet]
[Next role, same pattern]

PROJECTS
[Project name, link label]
- [bullet]

VOLUNTEER
[Role, dates]
- [bullet]

SKILLS (one line)

EDUCATION (one line)
```

Fill the summary last. Start from the "what makes me different" line in your career arc and keep it to two lines. If a phrase in it cannot be backed by a ledger row, cut the phrase. Describe an organisation by what it is, for example "a parcel delivery company's back office", if you did not get permission to use its name. Areej asked her lead before she used her chart in public, and you should ask too.

## Template two: the mapping table

This table is how you prove to yourself, and later to a mentor, that nothing on the page is made up.

| Resume line (first words) | Ledger row | Number or result | Source | My share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (the bullet's first words) | (row ID) | (the before and after, or the act) | (where it lives) | (what I did, what the team did) |

Every line that carries a number or a result needs a row. A plain fact, such as a degree or a date, can be marked "fact". A line with no row and no fact is a line to cut.

## Template three: the tailoring record

For the tailored copy you write three lines, and nothing else.

```text
Posting: [title, kind of organisation, the work it asks for in plain words]
Change 1: [what moves first] | row: [ID] | words I did not copy: [...]
Change 2: [project order] | row: [ID] | words I did not copy: [...]
Change 3: [summary line] | row: [ID] | words I did not copy: [...]
```

If you do not have a real posting you might apply to, use one of these two. Both are made up and come from no real company. Posting A: a backend developer for a small logistics software startup that builds route and delivery tools for local couriers. The work is keeping a PostgreSQL database fast as orders grow, writing Node.js services, and talking to customers' operations teams. Posting B: a frontend developer for a web agency that builds sites for small businesses. The work is building pages in React, working with designers, and fixing layout problems on phones.

## Worked example: Areej writes her resume

Areej starts with the three strongest bullets, as chapter 6.3 taught. She does not write the summary first. She opens her ledger and picks the rows with the clearest before and after.

The first is the slow orders report, from her internship, which sits in two rows: the query plan and the team dashboard. She writes the bullet from the card: "Cut the p95 of a daily orders report from 4.6 s to 0.41 s (14 days before and after) by finding a full-table scan and adding a composite index; my lead reviewed and scheduled the release." The p95 is the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within. A full-table scan means the database reads every row, and a composite index lets it find rows without reading them all. She checks the bullet against the rows. The 4.6 s and 0.41 s come from the dashboard, and the windows are 14 days each. Her lead's part is in the sentence. She leaves out the company name and the table names, because her lead said she could use the chart only with those removed.

The second is the invoice script for a print shop. She writes: "Saved a print shop about 3 hours a week of manual invoicing, net of upkeep, with a Node.js invoice script (median 7 to 1 minute per invoice, timed two weeks before and after)." She checks the arithmetic in her row again. About 31 invoices a week, 6 minutes saved each, gives 186 minutes. She subtracts about 7 minutes of weekly upkeep and gets 179 minutes, about 3 hours. The word "net" in the bullet tells the reader she did that subtraction.

The third has no number. She writes: "Revoked and replaced a leaked email-service key, moved secrets to environment variables and turned on push protection for a four-person team repository." Each act is checkable, and she invents nothing.

Next she adds the remaining lines. Campus Board gets a bullet from its row: "Cut an events endpoint's p95 from 820 ms to 95 ms under a constant 50 requests per second (same k6 script, data and laptop before and after) by adding a 30-second cache; a teammate wrote the endpoint." The k6 tool is a load tester. The bullet says who wrote the endpoint, so her share is clear.

For the youth club she writes two bullets. The first is about the sign-up form and the migration: 412 rows across three spreadsheets, 57 duplicates merged and 355 unique sign-ups imported. She checks the sum, because 412 minus 57 is 355. The second reads: "Double-booked seats fell from 9 to 1 a term (counted from the organisers' chat); the club also cut its sessions from three a week to two." Her row records that cut, so she says it and does not claim the whole drop.

Then she fills the mapping table, with a row for every line that carries a number or a result.

| Resume line (first words) | Ledger row | Number or result | Source | My share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cut the p95 of a daily orders report | R1 and R2 | 4.6 s to 0.41 s, 14 days before and after | Query plan on a copy; team dashboard | I found the scan and chose the index; my lead reviewed and scheduled it |
| Saved a print shop about 3 hours a week | R3 | Median 7 to 1 minute per invoice; 179 minutes a week net | Timing notes; upkeep estimate | All of it; the owner chose the invoice fields |
| Cut an events endpoint's p95 | R7 | 820 ms to 95 ms at 50 requests per second | Same k6 script, data and laptop | I wrote the cache and the test; a teammate wrote the endpoint |
| Revoked and replaced a leaked key | R6 | Three acts, no number | Provider console; repository settings | I noticed and fixed it; my teammate agreed and reviewed |
| Built a sign-up form and moved 412 rows | R4 | 412 rows, 57 duplicates, 355 imported | Row counts per session before and after | The form and the migration; the organisers chose the fields and tested it |
| Double-booked seats fell from 9 to 1 | R5 | 9 to 1 in a term | Counted from the organisers' chat | Mine: the form and the migration. Not mine: the club cut sessions to two a week, which may explain part of the drop |
| Education | fact | None | Degree programme | Not applicable |

Then she orders the page. Name and links, a two-line summary, Experience with the internship first and the report bullet first inside it, then freelance, then Projects, Volunteer, Skills in one line and Education. She writes the summary last, from her own line: a full-stack developer who measures before and after, and writes it down.

She saves the file as a PDF, selects all, and pastes it into her notes. The name comes first and the bullets stay under their jobs. She opens each link on her phone: the case study, the Campus Board load test and her LinkedIn profile. All three open. She keeps a one-line note for each: the label, the date and the result.

For the tailored copy she uses Posting A. She makes the three changes from chapter 6.5. The report bullet is already first, so she moves Campus Board above Volunteer and changes the summary to start "Backend-leaning full-stack developer". She copies none of the posting's words, and she records the row behind each change. One flag remains: "composite index" is jargon. She keeps it, because the posting's readers are engineers, and she makes sure her case study explains it in one sentence.

## How to use AI on your own material

Chapter 2.5 set the rule for AI tools. They may restructure what you wrote, and they never add numbers, ownership or experience. Remove names and contact details first, and use roles such as "a parcel delivery company" or "my team lead". For this chapter, paste your career arc and three to five ledger rows. Ask for each row as one bullet in the 6.3 shape, with MISSING for any part that is not there, and ask for an order suggestion for a posting.

Then check every bullet against its row, and run the empty sentence edit from chapter 6.4. If a bullet came back smoother but says less than your own, use your own.

Keep one line in your private notes for each use: the date, what you asked, what you used, what you checked, and one thing it got wrong. It might read: "Asked for bullets from three rows. Used the order and the verbs. Checked every number against the rows. It wrote hours a day where my row says a week, and I corrected it." Do not put this line on the resume. It belongs in your notes. Course 13, "Learn and Build with AI", explains when and how to say that AI helped in public work.

## If you have no work yet, or no laptop

Take the path from chapter 2.5. For this chapter, use Areej's rows for the lines you cannot write yet, and say in your notes which lines are hers. Write your own lines for whatever you have, even if it is one study project, and let the gap show in the mapping table. A short honest resume is a good starting point, and you will add rows as you earn them.

On a phone alone, write the outline in your notes app, use a word processor that runs in a web browser, and export a PDF. Run the paste test by pasting the PDF text into your notes. If you have no resume draft at all, start from Areej's outline and replace each of her lines, one at a time, with your own.

## Common mistakes and how to spot them

The most common mistake is writing the summary first. It fills with adjectives because you have no facts in front of you. If your summary has an adjective in it, replace it with a fact from a row.

The second is a duty that survived. Search your file for "responsible for" and "worked on". Each one is a place where a result should be. Ask what changed because you did it.

The third is a number that is not in the ledger. This often comes from an AI tool or from rounding up. Check every figure against its row, character by character. A figure that appears in the resume and not in the ledger is a defect, even if it feels true.

The fourth is hiding the team. If a bullet says "I" for something the team did, add the team's part in a few words. If a bullet says "we" for everything, say what you did.

The fifth is a broken link or an unlabelled one. A label that says "portfolio" tells the reader nothing, and a link that fails is worse than no link. Test every link on your phone on the day you send the file.

The sixth is tailoring by copying. If the posting's phrases appear in your resume and your ledger has no row for them, take them out. Tailoring changes what is first, not what is claimed.

## Acceptance criteria

Check each item before you move on.

- Every line maps to a ledger row, or is a plain fact such as a degree or a date.
- No empty sentence survives the edit, and no line begins "responsible for".
- The file is a standard format and passes the paste test.
- There are two or three labelled links, and each was opened on a phone.
- The tailored copy changes the order and the summary, copies no keywords, and has a row behind each change.
- No number appears that is not in the ledger.

## Where to keep it

Keep the resume file, the mapping table, the tailoring record and your AI-use lines in your private notes. The resume is the only one of these that you send out. The mapping table and the record are for you and your mentor. You will need all of them again in modules 7 and 9.

If you get stuck, write the three strongest bullets first. Everything else is ordering. If you are stuck on the summary, start from the "what makes me different" line in your arc. The companion course, The Founder Interview, is the place to practise defending a line aloud before the gate.
