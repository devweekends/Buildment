Areej's resume is ordered and her bullets are written. Two jobs are left: give the reader a place to check her claims, and fit the resume to one posting. By the end of this chapter you will know how to do both without adding a single word you cannot back up.

## Two or three links, each labelled

<MermaidDiagram title="Two labels for the same link" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Label: my portfolio"] --> B["The reader must guess what is behind it"]
  C["Label: Case study, a slow report from 4.6 s to 0.41 s"] --> D["The reader knows what they get and opens it first"]
`} legend="A vague label makes the reader guess. A label that names the result tells them which link to open first." />

Each label says what the reader gets: "Case study: a slow report from 4.6 s to 0.41 s", "Campus Board load test (GitHub)" and her LinkedIn profile. Two or three links are enough. Write each address out as plain text too, so it survives a paste and a printout.

<GoDeeper title="Which links to choose, and in what order">

Choose links that you control and that will still open in a year. The best first link is the one that holds your strongest proof. For Areej that is the case study from module 5, because it has the numbers, the plans and the redacted chart. The second is the code or the test for another claim. The third is your profile, which a reader may want to check.

No study measures whether links help. The rule is a course habit: stable links, labelled by what the reader gets. A tracking-software company's help pages also tell employers who want portfolios or large files to ask for a link, so a link is a normal part of an application.

Do not link to a demo that sleeps or needs a sign-up as your main link. Module 5 showed why. The case study is the base layer: it works when the demo is asleep.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="How to write the links line">

Use one line, with the label first and the address written out next to it. A label with no address cannot be typed from a printed page. An address with no label gives the reader no reason to open it.

For example: "Case study: a slow report from 4.6 s to 0.41 s" and then its address. Keep each label under about ten words, and make it say a result or a thing the reader will see, not "my portfolio" or "click here".

</GoDeeper>

## Test every link on a phone

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-test-links" />

A reader will open each link on a phone, so do the same before you send. The case study opens. The old demo link says "not found", because a free plan ended. A link that worked last month can fail today. Test them all on the day you send.

<GoDeeper title="How to test your links">

Open each link on your phone, in a private window, so that you are not signed in. A reader is not signed in to your account, and a page that works only for you is broken for them.

Write one line for each link in your private notes: the label, the date, the device and the result. If a link fails, fix the page or take the link out. A missing link is better than a broken one.

Also check the address of your LinkedIn profile. LinkedIn's help pages say a custom address can be changed up to five times within six months (checked 2026-10). They also say an old address you unlink can be claimed by someone else after a short time. Pick one address and keep it, or the link on your resume may point to a stranger.

</GoDeeper>

## Tailoring changes what comes first

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-tailoring-reorder" />

A small logistics software startup posts a backend role that asks for PostgreSQL performance work. Areej changes three things and adds nothing. Her report bullet moves first. Campus Board moves above Volunteer. Her summary starts "Backend-leaning full-stack developer". She copies none of the posting's words. Same facts, new order.

<GoDeeper title="Why this counts as real tailoring">

One software company that publishes how it hires says its reviewers look at whether an application was tailored, and whether the candidate tried its product or read its public handbook. That is one company, so it is an example, not a rule.

Read it this way. Real tailoring changes which evidence comes first, and it shows you looked at their work. It is not the same as sounding like the posting.

Areej did not claim anything new. She has no production uptime numbers of her own, which is a gap in her arc, and the posting does not ask for them. She also does not hint that she has them. Her first bullet is true, and it is what the posting asked for.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="How Areej read the posting">

She read the posting twice and wrote down, in plain words, what it asked for: PostgreSQL performance, a small team, and logistics software. For each one she asked which ledger row answers it.

PostgreSQL performance has two rows: the report query and the cache test. A small team has the four-person Campus Board repository and her internship team. Logistics matches her internship, which was in the back office of a parcel delivery company. She says that plainly and claims nothing more.

That gave her the three changes. Each is backed by a row, and she wrote the rows next to the changes in her tailoring record.

</GoDeeper>

## Pick a posting, watch the order change

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-pick-a-posting" />

This is Areej's untailored resume as a first screen. The posting asks for database speed work, but the first screen holds her summary, the invoice script, the key fix and the club form. Move the report bullet and the load-test bullet up. The p95 in them is the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within.

<GoDeeper title="A different posting, a different order">

Nothing is deleted in the picture. Every block is a true line from her ledger, and each one is a good first line for some other posting. The key fix would lead for a role about security. The invoice script would lead for a role about tools for small businesses.

A frontend role would pull up different rows. If Areej had a row about a screen she built and measured, that row would go first. If her rows only describe the database behind the club form, then the honest result is that this posting is a weaker match for her. She would say less, not more.

Your tailoring record for chapter 6.6 has three lines: the change, the ledger row that backs it, and the words you did not copy.

</GoDeeper>

## Copied words carry nothing

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-copied-words" />

A resume with the posting's own words pasted in gives the reader no proof. A resume with the same facts in a new order gets read on. Tailoring means changing what goes first, not copying their words. If you cannot back a line with proof, take it out.

<GoDeeper title="Why a polished match no longer proves much">

Two working papers, which are studies shared before journal review, looked at cover letters. One found that the link between how well a letter matched a posting and callbacks fell by about half once an AI writing tool was offered. A callback is a reply from the employer asking for an interview. Both found that the extra signal from a customised letter weakened, and that employers turned to other signals. When anyone can match a posting's words in a minute, matching them says very little.

A resume company has also published a study of tailored cover letters, with large claims. It is a company's own claim, so do not quote it. Keyword stuffing and hidden white text are dishonest. They are never part of this course.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="A last test before you send">

Go through the tailored resume line by line. For each line, ask three questions. Which ledger row backs it? Could I explain it aloud for two minutes? Is this word here because I did the work, or only because it was in the posting?

If the answer to the third question is "only because it was in the posting", delete the word. If a whole line fails, delete the line. A shorter resume that is all true is a stronger resume.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

The idea that real tailoring shows you looked at the employer's work comes from one software company's public hiring handbook. The finding that matched letters lost their signal comes from two working papers, by Galdin and Silbert, and by Cui, Dias and Ye. The tracking-software company's help pages and LinkedIn's help pages were checked 2026-10. The three-change method is this course's own.

</GoDeeper>
