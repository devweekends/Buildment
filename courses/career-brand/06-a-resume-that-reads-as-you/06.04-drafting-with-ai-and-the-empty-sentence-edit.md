An AI tool can save you an hour of layout work. It can also add a number you never measured. This chapter sets the rule for using it on a resume, and the edit pass that keeps your resume full of facts.

## Your material in, structure out

<MermaidDiagram title="What the AI tool may and may not do" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Your arc and ledger rows"] --> B["AI tool: restructure only"]
  B --> C{"Is every fact in my inputs?"}
  C -->|no| D["Delete it"]
  C -->|yes| E["A draft you edit yourself"]
`} legend="Your own material goes in. The tool returns a draft. The diamond is your check: a fact that is not in your inputs is deleted, and what is left is yours to edit." />

AI may help you with structure. You give it your own material: your career arc and the ledger rows for this resume. It returns a draft. Then you check every fact in the draft against your inputs. A number, a tool, a duty or a result that is not in your inputs is deleted. You edit what is left yourself.

<GoDeeper title="The rule, and why it holds">

The rule from module 2 is: restructure, never add. A tool may reorder, shorten and tidy your own words. It may not generate numbers, ownership or judgment, because it does not know what you did. The check comes after the tool, because the tool cannot tell a true fact from a smooth guess. It will often write something that sounds right. A sentence that sounds right and was never measured is exactly what the first reader question, "how was that measured?", breaks.

You stay responsible for every word you send. LinkedIn's user agreement says the same about content its members generate with AI tools: the member is responsible for it (checked 2026-10). The idea holds for any tool and any platform.

Before you paste anything, remove your name, phone number, email, address and any employer or client name. Use roles such as "a parcel delivery company" or "my team lead". Never paste keys or private company data. Course 13, "Learn and Build with AI", explains how to say when AI helped.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="A prompt you can use">

Paste three ledger rows and ask: "Write each row as one resume bullet in this shape: a strong verb and what I owned, the change with before and after, the window in a short bracket, how I did it, and my share. Use only facts in the rows. If a part is missing, write MISSING. Do not add numbers, tools or duties."

Then do the check yourself. Read each bullet next to its row. Put a mark on every fact. If a fact has no row behind it, delete it. If the tool wrote MISSING, go back to your ledger and find the part, or accept that the bullet has a gap.

</GoDeeper>

## What an empty sentence looks like

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-old-bullet-marked" />

Here is Areej's old bullet, questioned one phrase at a time. It reads: "Responsible for leveraging cutting-edge technologies to drive seamless performance improvements across robust backend systems." The reader asks about "leveraging", "seamless" and "robust". Each comes back empty. Almost nothing a reader can check is left. The bullet was not false. It held no fact to ask about.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's old bullet, phrase by phrase">

"Responsible for" is a duty, not a result. "Leveraging cutting-edge technologies": which technologies, and what did they do? "Drive seamless performance improvements": which performance, improved from what to what? "Across robust backend systems": which systems, and robust by what measure?

What is left after you remove every phrase a reader cannot check is the word "backend". That is all. The edit pass does not repair the sentence. It shows that there was no fact in it.

So Areej does not polish it. She replaces it with the first bullet from her ledger: "Cut the p95 of a daily orders report from 4.6 s to 0.41 s (14 days before and after) by finding a full-table scan and adding a composite index; my lead reviewed and scheduled the release." Here a p95 is the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within. A full-table scan means the database reads every row, and a composite index lets it find rows without reading them all.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Habits that make text empty">

A volunteer-written guide to signs of AI writing lists habits that make a text empty. We use it only for orientation, because it is a secondary source. The habits are these. Making something sound more important than it is. "Experts say" with no expert named. A trailing phrase ending in "-ing" that adds no fact. "Not just this but also that". Lists of three. Promotional adjectives. Heavy bold type and emoji.

People write all of these too. They are habits of empty text, not proof that a machine wrote it. Check your own draft for them: big claims with no number, "experts say" with no expert, and three adjectives where one fact would do.

</GoDeeper>

## Tap the empty phrases

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-empty-phrases" />

Waed's AI-written summary and duty line hold phrases a reader cannot check. Tap each one you find. Each tapped phrase says why it is empty and offers a fact she can back up. The counter shows how many facts a reader can check. It starts at 0 and rises as each empty phrase is replaced by something she can prove. Deleting words alone never raises it.

<GoDeeper title="The edit pass, in four steps">

The pass is about information, not about "sounding human".

1. Mark every phrase a reader cannot check: adjectives, big claims, duties, and anything with no number, name or place.
2. For each mark, ask: "what is the fact behind this?".
3. If a ledger row holds the fact, use it, with its window and your share.
4. If no row holds it, delete the phrase. Do not fill the gap with a new adjective.

The result for Waed is plain: "Operations coordinator on an NGO's logistics team who built a shared tracker: missed confirmations fell from 90 to 18 over three months (team tracker); a daily check also began. Source: the team's tracker."

</GoDeeper>

## Three adjectives or one fact

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-adjectives-or-fact" />

Compare two lines. "Dynamic, results-driven, passionate" gives a reader nothing to ask. "Missed confirmations fell from 90 to 18 in three months" gives a reader a question: how was that measured? A good line invites the next question. Adjectives never do.

<GoDeeper title="What to do when you have no fact yet">

If your summary is full of adjectives, the fix is not a better adjective. The fix is a fact. Go to your ledger and pick the row you are proudest of. Write one plain sentence from it.

If you truly have no row for what you want to claim, say less. A short, plain summary is better than a long empty one. Module 1 made the same point: when writing is cheap, one fact a reader can check does more than any number of smooth words.

Module 3 showed how to build a row. If a summary line needs a fact you have not measured yet, write it down as a task for the ledger and leave the line out for now.

</GoDeeper>

## Never write to sound unlike a model

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-humaniser-no" />

Some tools promise to make text "sound human" or to pass an AI detector. They change style and add no facts. Nobody can reliably tell if a profile was written with AI, and detectors misjudge second-language English. Ask instead: does this line say something only I could say?

<GoDeeper title="Why there is no banned word list">

Researchers have shown that some words became far more common in scientific papers from 2023. That is a population-level finding, which means it describes many papers taken together and not one writer. It does not make one word proof that one person used a tool. Word lists also date as models change.

In one study, widely used detectors wrongly labelled many essays by non-native English writers as written by AI. People do poorly at telling the two apart as well. So a resume tuned to pass a detector, or to avoid certain words, solves a problem that nobody can reliably measure.

A line is yours when it holds a fact that only you could give, with a source: your number, your window, your part.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What about AI screeners that prefer AI text?">

A preprint, which is a paper shared before other experts have checked it, reports that in a simulated screening, AI models chose resumes written by the same model more often. It is a simulation, not the real market. A major tracking-software company says it does not use AI to reject candidates (checked 2026-10).

So do not write to match a model, and do not write to avoid one. A resume tuned to sound like or unlike a model has no information added by the tuning. Write for the person who will ask you about each line.

</GoDeeper>
