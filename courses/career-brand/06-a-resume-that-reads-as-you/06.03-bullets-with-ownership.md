A bullet is where a ledger row meets a reader. Waed's resume says "Responsible for coordinating shipments", and Areej's old one said "Worked on backend performance". This chapter turns a row of your claim ledger into a bullet that can be questioned and still stand.

```predict
prompt: "Which bullet survives the question 'how was that measured?'"
options:
  - id: counted
    label: "Missed delivery confirmations fell from 90 to 18 (team tracker, three months before and after)"
  - id: percent
    label: "Improved delivery tracking by 80 percent across the whole team, with a tracker I built"
correctOptionId: counted
explanation: "Right. The first bullet gives a before, an after, a window and a source, so a reader can ask to see the tracker. The second gives a percentage with no starting count, no window and no source, and 'across the whole team' hides which part was hers."
allowRetry: true
```

## "Responsible for" is a job description

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-duty-vs-bullet" />

A duty says what Waed was supposed to do. A bullet says what changed because she did it. "Responsible for coordinating shipments" leaves the reader's next question open: what changed? Her owned bullet answers it first: missed delivery confirmations fell from 90 to 18, counted from the team tracker over three months before and three after.

<GoDeeper title="Waed's rewrite, part by part">

Her new bullet reads: "Built a shared delivery tracker; missed delivery confirmations fell from 90 to 18 (team tracker, three months before and after); teammates also began a daily check in that period."

Take it apart. "Built a shared delivery tracker" is what she owned. "Fell from 90 to 18" is the change, with its before and its after. "Three months before and after" is the window. "Team tracker" is the source, in a short bracket. "Teammates also began a daily check in that period" names what else changed, and it names the team's part, so her own part is easier to believe. A reader can ask how she counted, and the answer is already on the page.

A duty line is not wrong. It is a fair description of a job. It just does not answer the question a reader asks next, which is "so what happened?". Keep a duty only when it sets the scene for a result, and put the result right after it.

</GoDeeper>

## Build a bullet from a claim card

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-build-a-bullet" />

Tap a chip, then the part of the bullet it fills. A bullet has five parts: what you owned, the change, the window, how, and your share. Areej's old lines fit none of them, and the card refuses each with a reason. Her new line uses a p95, the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within.

<GoDeeper title="The five parts, and where the formula comes from">

A former head of hiring at a large technology company gave a formula that many people repeat. Say what you accomplished, how it was measured, and what you did to get there. It is one practitioner's advice, not a research finding. The course adds two parts from module 3: the baseline with its window, and your share.

So the shape has five parts. Start with a strong verb and the thing you owned. Then give the change, with its before and after. Then the window. Then how you did it, in a few words. Last, your share. In short: "A bullet says what you owned, what changed, how you know, and what part was yours."

Percentages need a base. "90 percent" means nothing until the reader knows 90 percent of what, over which days. A before and an after give the percentage, if you want to add it.

Two terms in Areej's line need a plain word. A full-table scan means the database reads every row. A composite index lets it find rows by two columns without reading them all.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="From claim card to bullet, in Areej's case">

Her claim card from module 3 has more fields than a bullet can hold. It has the metric, the before and the after, the window, the source, what else changed, and her share. The bullet keeps the parts a reader needs first.

The card says: "Cut the p95 of the orders report endpoint from 4.6 s to 0.41 s (14 days before and after, team dashboard) by finding a full-table scan and adding a composite index; I found and fixed it, my lead reviewed and scheduled the release." The bullet trims the endpoint name and keeps the rest. The note about the front-end release on 20 May stays in the card and the case study, where there is room.

Use the same method for every row of your ledger. Write the card first. Then cut it to fit one or two lines, and do not cut the window or your share.

</GoDeeper>

## Before, after and window in few words

<MermaidDiagram title="The same bullet, with and without a bracket" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["No bracket: p95 from 4.6 s to 0.41 s"] --> B["The reader asks: over which days? from where?"]
  C["With a bracket: 14 days before and after"] --> D["The first question is already answered"]
`} legend="Without a bracket the reader has to ask. With a short bracket the first question has an answer on the page." />

Put the window and the source in a short bracket: "(14 days before and after, team dashboard)". Keep it to a few words. The rest lives in two other places. The ledger row says where the numbers are and how to check them again. The case study tells the whole story, including what else changed in those weeks.

<GoDeeper title="Short brackets that work">

Here are brackets from Areej's own material. Her report bullet uses "(14 days before and after)". Her invoice bullet uses "(median 7 to 1 minute per invoice, timed two weeks before and after)". Her cache work could use "(same k6 script, data and laptop before and after)", where k6 is a load-testing tool.

Each one names a window and a way of measuring. None of them names a company, a customer or a table. The real names stay in your private ledger. Areej had to ask her lead before using the dashboard chart in public, and he said yes with the company name and table names removed.

If a bracket will not fit on the line, shorten the bullet, not the bracket. A bullet with a number and no bracket is the "improved speed by 90%" problem again.

</GoDeeper>

## Say your share, without shrinking it

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-found-vs-team" />

You saw this picture in module 2, now applied to a resume line. Areej's report fix had seven steps. She did four: found the query, ran the plan, chose the index and wrote the migration. Her lead did three: reviewed, scheduled and deployed. The bullet says so: "my lead reviewed and scheduled the release". Naming the team's part makes your part easier to believe.

<GoDeeper title="Why a blurred 'we' is a risk, and 'I' for everything is too">

Researchers who study resume deception describe three kinds. Fabrication is inventing something. Embellishment is making something bigger than it was. Omission is leaving something out to mislead. Writing "I built it" when a team built it is embellishment. Leaving out the lead's part so that your own looks bigger is omission.

The opposite mistake is also common. Some fellows write "we" for everything because it feels modest. Then a reader cannot tell what they did. Write "I" for your steps and name the team's steps in plain words. "I found the query and wrote the migration; my lead reviewed and scheduled the release" is believable because it is specific.

A reader will test it. The companion course, The Founder Interview, shows how to defend a line aloud. If your share is written clearly now, the questions get easier.

</GoDeeper>

## No number? Show the change another way

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-no-number-bullet" />

Not every good bullet has a number. Areej found a leaked key in a team repository. Her bullet names three checkable acts: the key was revoked and replaced, secrets moved to environment variables, and push protection was turned on. No number was invented.

<GoDeeper title="Why you should never invent a number">

Nothing we found shows that bullets with numbers win more interviews. The advice is common, but it comes from resume vendors and no study stands behind it. So there is no reason to add a number you do not have.

An invented number breaks the first question a reader asks: "how was that measured?". "Improved security by 100 percent" has no answer. A checkable act has one: the revoked key shows in the provider's console, the setting shows in the repository, and the commit shows in the history.

Areej's full bullet reads: "Revoked and replaced a leaked email-service key, moved secrets to environment variables and turned on push protection for a four-person team repository." If you truly have no measurement, an honest gap is also fine, and the case study can say how you would measure next time.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Show the fix, never the secret">

When your proof involves a key or a password, show what you did about it, and never the secret itself. Chapter 3.3 explains why. A screenshot of a setting turned on is proof. A screenshot of the key is a new leak.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

The three kinds of resume deception come from Henle, Dineen and Duffy, who studied them in surveys (2019). The bullet formula is one former hiring head's advice, so we use it as a practitioner view. The claim that quantified bullets win more interviews has no study behind it that we found. The five-part bullet is this course's own method.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "In this made-up case, Habib, a backend developer on a student team, revoked a leaked key, moved the team's secrets to environment variables and turned on push protection. He has no number for it. Which bullet is honest and still strong?"
options:
  - "Improved the security of the team's backend by 100 percent after a review of how its credentials were handled"
  - "Prevented every future breach of the team's repository by fixing how credentials were stored and then handled"
  - "Responsible for security improvements across the team's repository, including how its credentials were handled"
  - "Revoked and replaced a leaked key, moved secrets to environment variables and turned on push protection"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "The 100 percent has no base, no window and no source. Habib cannot answer 'how was that measured?', because nothing was measured."
  - "No one can show that a breach will never happen. The claim cannot be checked now or later."
  - "This is a duty. It says what Habib was supposed to do, not what he did. A reader has nothing to look up."
  - "Right. It names three acts that a reader can check in the provider's console and the repository settings. It invents no number."
```
