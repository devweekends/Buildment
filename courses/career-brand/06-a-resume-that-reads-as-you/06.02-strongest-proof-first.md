Areej's old resume had two pages and hid her best work. This chapter is about the order of a resume, and the file it travels in. Areej is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative. By the end you will know how to order your own resume, and how to check that the file reads the same way for a program as it does for a person.

## No proven right length

<MermaidDiagram title="How long should a resume be?" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["One page or two?"] --> B["No proven right length"]
  B --> C["Put the strongest proof first"]
  C --> D["Cut what does not help this reader"]
`} legend="The question at the top has no proven answer. The rule below it replaces it: strongest proof first, then cut. The page count is whatever is left." />

People argue about one page or two. The only data we found come from one resume company's own test, and they do not settle it. So use a different rule. Put your strongest proof first. Then cut anything that does not help this reader. The page count is whatever remains.

<GoDeeper title="What the one test says, and does not say">

A resume-writing company ran a simulation with 482 people who recruit or hire. They chose between pairs of resumes, one of one page and one of two pages, with similar content. Most of the resumes they chose had two pages.

Read this narrowly. It is a company's simulation, not a peer-reviewed study. It shows that a second page did not hurt when there was real content to fill it. It does not show that long resumes win.

For most fellows, at early career or mid level, the result of "strongest proof first, cut the rest" is usually one page. That is a course recommendation, not a research finding. Areej's old resume had two pages, and the page count was not the problem. The second page held a paragraph about being passionate and a block of 22 technologies. Her new resume is one page because she cut those and added the work that was missing.

</GoDeeper>

## Order by strength of proof

<MermaidDiagram title="Areej's new resume, top to bottom" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Name and links line"] --> B["Two-line summary"]
  B --> C["Experience: internship, then freelance"]
  C --> D["Projects: Campus Board"]
  D --> E["Volunteer"]
  E --> F["Skills, one line"]
  F --> G["Education"]
`} legend="Each box is a section, in the order a reader meets it. The proof sits in the first four boxes. The skills and education come last." />

Across sections, put first what this reader needs most. Areej's proof is in her internship, so Experience comes right after her name, her links and a two-line summary. Inside a role, put the strongest result a reader can check first. Date order comes second.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's old resume, step by step">

Her old resume began with an objective paragraph, then a block of 22 technologies, then three weak bullets. Her freelance and volunteer work were missing. Campus Board was one line with no link. To rebuild it, she followed five steps.

1. She cut the objective paragraph. It said nothing a reader could check.
2. She wrote a two-line summary from her own line: "A full-stack developer who measures before and after, and writes it down."
3. She put the internship first, and inside it the report bullet first, because it has the clearest before and after.
4. She added the freelance invoice script, Campus Board and the club's sign-up form, each from a row of her ledger.
5. She cut the skills block to one line at the end.

Nothing here needed new work. It needed the same work in a better order.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="If you have little work experience">

Put your strongest proof first, even if it is not a job. A fellow with one internship result and one study project puts Experience first, then Projects, with skills in one line and education last. A fellow with no job yet starts with Projects, or with a showcase from module 5.

Education goes first only when it is the strongest thing you can show for this reader. A degree in progress is a plain fact. It needs one line near the end, not a section at the top.

</GoDeeper>

## What tracking software really does

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-filing-cabinet" />

Applicant tracking software works like a filing cabinet. An employer sets the rules. The software sorts the applications into a list. A person reads the list and decides. The story that robots throw out most resumes has no source. What is true is that some employers use filters, and people set them.

<GoDeeper title="Where the 75 percent comes from, and what is true">

A consultant tried to trace the figure. It came from a 2012 sales pitch by a company that sold resume tools and closed in 2013. No study stood behind it. Many pages still repeat it.

What is true is smaller. A major tracking-software company wrote in 2025 that it never uses AI to rate candidates or reject them, and that its recruiters and hiring teams decide (checked 2026-10). An employer survey by Harvard Business School and Accenture reports that more than 90 percent of employers using recruitment software rely on it for a first cut or a ranking. Some use negative filters, for example one that ranks down a long gap in work.

That survey is about employers that use such software. It does not describe a two-person startup that reads an inbox. Both facts can be true at once: tools sort by rules, and people set the rules and decide. So write for both readers, a program and a person.

</GoDeeper>

## The paste test

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-paste-test" />

Select all, paste into a plain text editor, and read it. If the order and the words survive, a program can read the file too. Sort the eight layout choices into the two buckets. Each wrong try tells you what a paste does to that choice. This is a quick check, not a guarantee.

<GoDeeper title="How to run the paste test">

On a laptop, open your resume file. Select everything, copy it, and paste it into a plain text editor. Read the result from top to bottom. Ask three questions. Is your name first? Do the bullets stay under the right job? Are your links still readable?

On a phone, open the file and try to select a line of text. If you can select it, copy a few lines and paste them into a notes app. If you cannot select anything, the file is an image, and a program cannot read it either. That is your answer.

If the order breaks, the cause is usually the layout: two columns, a text box, or a header image. Change the layout, not the words, and test again.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Where the layout advice comes from">

Most advice to avoid columns, text boxes and graphics comes from resume-service blogs. We did not find it in the help pages of tracking-software makers. So treat it as a cautious habit, and not as a rule from any vendor.

The habit costs you little. A single column with normal headings is easy for a person to read on a phone as well. Different programs read files in different ways, so trust your own paste more than any list (checked 2026-10).

</GoDeeper>

## A plain file a program can read

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m6-plain-file" />

Send a simple file: text you can select, normal headings, and a standard format. One system's help pages (checked 2026-10) say a resume may fail to import if it has too little information, a non-standard file type, or characters the parser cannot read. Write your links out as plain text too.

<GoDeeper title="Which file to send">

The same help pages list the uploads that system accepts: Word files (.doc and .docx), PDF, RTF and plain text, up to 100 MB. They also tell employers who want portfolios or large files to ask for a link, which is why your links belong on the page.

A PDF with selectable text looks the same on every phone. A Word file is fine when a posting asks for one. Follow the posting. Keep the file small and plain. Do not send a screenshot of your resume or a file made from an image.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Areej checks her file">

Areej exports her one-page resume to PDF. She selects all and pastes it into a notes app. The paste reads: her name, her links line, the two-line summary, then the internship bullets in order. The links line shows the label "Case study: a slow report from 4.6 s to 0.41 s" and the address written out next to it.

She then opens the file on her phone and long-presses a line. The text can be selected. She sends this file, and she keeps the paste in her notes as proof that she tested it.

</GoDeeper>
