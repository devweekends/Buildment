Some of your best numbers sit on dashboards that someone else set up. Before you quote one, you need to read it the way a careful reader would. A few words first. A request is one visit to a service, such as opening a page. Response time is how long the service takes to answer. A percentile such as <GlossaryTerm term="p95" definition="The time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within. One request in 20 was slower than this." /> describes the slow end of the line. The picture in the section "p50, p95 and p99 in plain words" shows it.

## Six questions for any number

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-six-questions" />

You meet a number on a dashboard you did not set up. Before you quote it, ask six questions. What does it measure? Which statistic is it? Over what window? Where does the data come from? What else changed? Who owns it? The tile answers some of them. The owner answers the rest. The last question is also a permission question.

<GoDeeper title="The six questions, in full, with Areej's answers">

One: what exactly does it measure, including what counts as an error? Two: which statistic is it, a mean, a median or a percentile, and was a per-server number averaged? Three: over what window, and at what interval was it aggregated? Four: where does the data come from, and how late can it arrive? Five: what else changed in the window? Six: who owns the dashboard, and did they say the number may be used in public? These come from the Google SRE books and the monitoring documentation, and the set of six is this course's method.

Areej's tile reads "report endpoint p95, 14 days". Her answers: it measures the time from request to response at the API. The statistic is p95. The window is 14 days before and 14 days after the fix. The data comes from the team's monitoring, which her lead owns, and she asked him how it is collected rather than guessing. What else changed: a front-end release on 20 May. And the number is public only with the company name and real table names removed, which her lead agreed to.

Notice that two answers came from the tile and four needed a person. If you cannot find out how a number is collected, say so, or leave the number out.

</GoDeeper>

## Averages hide slow requests

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-same-average" />

Two services each answered 20 requests, and both average 120 ms. Service A takes 88 to 150 ms every time. Service B takes 40 to 58 ms, except two requests at 700 and 810 ms. Tap Average, p50 and p99 to see what each one shows. The average hides B's slow users. Then tap Combine A and B.

<GoDeeper title="The numbers behind the picture">

Add up service A's 20 times and you get 2,400 ms, so its average is 120 ms. Service B's 18 fast requests add up to 890 ms. With 700 and 810 ms, the total is 2,400 ms again, so its average is also 120 ms. The same average, with very different users.

The other statistics split them. The middle value, the p50, is 120 ms for A and 50 ms for B, so B even looks better than A. The p95 is 144 ms for A and 700 ms for B. The p99 is 150 ms for A and 810 ms for B. With only 20 requests each, the p99 is simply the slowest request. Real dashboards use thousands. The dashed line in the picture is a target: p99 under 300 ms. A meets it, and B does not.

The Google SRE book makes the same point with a different example. Suppose most requests take about 50 ms, but 5 percent are twenty times slower. Do the sum: 0.95 times 50 is 47.5, and 0.05 times 1,000 is 50, so the average is 97.5 ms. It nearly doubles, so it does move. But it cannot tell you whether every request got a little slower or one in 20 got much slower. Only a percentile can. A second chapter of the book warns that a 100 ms average at 1,000 requests per second can still mean that 1 percent of requests take about 5 seconds. In plain words: averages hide slow requests, so say p95 or p99 and say over what window.

</GoDeeper>

## p50, p95 and p99 in plain words

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-percentile-line" />

Line up 100 requests from fastest to slowest. The p50 is the middle one: half were faster. The p95 is the point where 1 request in 20 was slower. The p99 is the point where 1 in 100 was slower. The markers are places on the line, not averages. The higher the percentile, the closer to the slowest requests.

<GoDeeper title="Which one to quote, and what to leave out">

A percentile is a position in a sorted line. To find the p95 of 20 requests, sort them from fastest to slowest and take the 19th, because 19 is 95 percent of 20. That is how service B's p95 became 700 ms. This is the "nearest rank" method, and real tools may choose the point in slightly different ways.

Which one should you quote? The p50 tells the typical experience. The p95 tells what the slower 1 in 20 users meet. The p99 tells the near-worst cases. Areej quotes the p95 because the team's dashboard chart already shows it, and because her fix was meant to help the slow report. Pick the one your source uses, and say which it is.

Track failed requests and successful ones separately. An error often comes back very fast, so a dashboard that counts failures together with successes can look quicker than it feels. If the tile mixes them, ask.

</GoDeeper>

## The window changes the number

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-window-changes" />

The same requests can look spiky second by second and calm as a one-minute average. Neither view is wrong. If the window is not stated, a reader cannot tell which one they are looking at. So every number needs its window, and every target needs one too.

<GoDeeper title="Windows and targets">

A target is stated over a window too. When you read a dashboard, find the window and the interval it was aggregated over. When you write a claim, give them. "p95 4.6 s over 14 days" is a claim. "p95 4.6 s" alone is half of one.

A short note, which you do not need in order to quote a number: dashboards often call a measurement an SLI and its target an SLO. An SLA is a contract with consequences if it is missed.

</GoDeeper>

## Percentiles do not average

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-percentiles-dont-average" />

Take the same A and B again, as two servers behind one dashboard. Server A has a p99 of 150 ms and Server B has 810 ms. A dashboard that averages them shows 480 ms, a number no request had. Merge the 40 requests first, and the p99 is 810 ms. If you see "average p95 across servers", ask how it was computed.

<GoDeeper title="Why the right way is to merge first">

The average of 150 and 810 is (150 plus 810) divided by 2, which is 480. But no request took 480 ms, and the real slowest request in the merged line took 810 ms. A percentile is a position in a sorted line, and the line of one server says nothing about where another server's requests would sit among them.

The documentation for a widely used monitoring tool calls the average of per-server 95th percentiles statistically nonsensical. The safe way is to merge the requests, or the counts per time range, before you read the percentile. In the picture, the 40 merged requests are sorted and the 40th is 810 ms, which is the p99 because 99 percent of 40 rounds up to 40.

What do you do with this? When a tile says "average p95 across servers", ask: is this the p95 of all requests together, or an average of each server's p95? Do not quote it until you know.

</GoDeeper>

## Uptime percent is downtime minutes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-slo-window" />

Drag the target along the rail and pick a window. At 99.9 percent over 30 days, a service may be bad for 43.2 minutes. Each extra nine cuts that allowance ten times. A 25 minute outage fits inside 99.9 percent and breaks 99.95 percent.

<GoDeeper title="The arithmetic of nines">

Thirty days is 43,200 minutes, because 30 times 24 times 60 is 43,200. Take the share that may be bad. At 99 percent, 1 percent is 432 minutes, which is 7.2 hours. At 99.5 percent, 0.5 percent is 216 minutes, which is 3.6 hours. At 99.9 percent, 0.1 percent is 43.2 minutes. At 99.95 percent it is 21.6 minutes, and at 99.99 percent it is about 4.3 minutes. This is simple arithmetic and assumes the service is fully down while it is bad.

So "99.9 percent means almost never down" is a misconception: it allows about 43 minutes in a 30-day month. The SRE workbook says 100 percent is the wrong target, because change causes outages and each extra nine costs more.

When you read an uptime number on someone's dashboard, ask which window it covers. When you write one, write the minutes as well.

</GoDeeper>
