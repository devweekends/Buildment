## Why the ledger comes first

Everything you write from now on needs numbers, and every number needs somewhere to come from. In module 5 you write a case study. In module 6 you write a resume. In module 7 you write a profile, and in module 8 a post. If you write those first and look for evidence later, you will be tempted to make the evidence fit the sentence. So you build the evidence first, once, in one private place. That place is your claim ledger.

The ledger is a table with one row per claim. Each row holds the draft line you might use in your kit and the number behind it. It also says where the number lives, how someone can check it again, and what part was yours. Next to it sits a shorter list, the proof-of-work inventory, which lists every piece of work you did and what evidence it left. The inventory shows you where to look. The ledger holds what you found.

Later chapters draw from this table. Your resume bullets will be copied from ledger rows. Your case study will expand one row. In module 9 a mentor picks any number in your kit, and you open the row it came from. If a number is not in the ledger, it does not go in the kit. That rule is strict, and it saves you from the question you cannot answer.

This chapter takes about 80 minutes, and most fellows need two sittings. Chapters 3.2 to 3.5 were the preparation: the claim card, the proof for each kind of work, the six questions and the controlled test. Here you use all of them on your own work, or on Areej's.

## The two templates

Start with the inventory. Open a private note or a spreadsheet, and make one line for each piece of work from the role cards in your career arc. For each line, write the kind of work, the evidence type from chapter 3.3, where the evidence lives, and a status. The status is one of three words: have it, can get it, or not measured. "Have it" means you can open the evidence now. "Can get it" means it exists but you need to ask someone or search for it. "Not measured" means no evidence exists yet.

| ID | Work item | Kind of work | Evidence type | Where it lives | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W1 | (one line about the work) | speed fix, cleanup, security, migration, automation, uptime, users, cost | plan pair, dashboard, pull request, counts, timing sheet, bill | (a file, a link, a person to ask) | have it, can get it, not measured |

Then make the ledger. Copy this block into your notes once for every claim you want to keep. A vertical block is easier on a phone than a very wide table. If you prefer a spreadsheet, make each line a column.

```text
ID:
Draft kit line:
Metric (what was measured):
Before:
After:
Window (dates, or how long):
Source (where it lives, and how to re-check it):
My share (what I did, what others did):
What else changed:
Permission (public-ok, redacted, private):
Status (ready, needs source, honest gap):
```

The permission column needs a word of explanation. It has three values. "Public-ok" means you may use the number in public as it stands. "Redacted" means you may use it once names and customer data are removed, and you say how. "Private" means you keep it out of public material. If you asked someone for permission, as you learned in chapter 3.3, write what they said. If you did not ask and you are unsure, write "private". Your ledger itself stays private. It may name your employer. Your public lines never do.

## The steps

Work in this order, and do not skip the second step.

1. Copy the results from your role cards into the inventory, one line each. Do not worry about numbers yet.
2. For each line, find the evidence. Use chapter 3.3 to decide what to look for. Open the dashboard, the plan, the pull request, the sheet. Set the status honestly.
3. For any number that sits on a dashboard you did not set up, answer the six questions from chapter 3.4, in writing. If you cannot answer one, ask the owner, or mark the row "needs source".
4. Fill the ledger block for your three strongest lines. Write the draft kit line last, after the facts are in.
5. Write at least one honest gap, with a concrete way you would measure it.
6. Check your arithmetic. Do every percentage and every subtraction yourself.

If you are stuck, write the gap first. A gap with a plan is a complete row, and it often makes the other rows easier. If you have no laptop, do all of this on a phone in a notes app. Nothing here needs software.

## A worked example: Areej's ledger

She starts from her raw notes and from the dashboard export you saw in chapter 3.5. Her private copy names the company, the shop and the club. Here they are written the way she would write them in public.

First her inventory. She finds seven pieces of work with evidence, and one without. The orders report query has a plan pair. The report endpoint has the team dashboard chart. The invoice script has a timing sheet. The sign-up migration has row counts. The double bookings have a count from a group chat. The leaked key has a repository setting. The cache has a load test script. Only one item has no evidence at all: whether customers received invoices sooner. Seven are "have it". That one is "not measured".

**R1, the query plan pair.** The draft line reads: "Found a full-table scan in a daily report query. Cut its actual time from 3,840 ms to 46 ms with a composite index." The metric is the actual time printed by EXPLAIN ANALYZE. Before is a sequential scan at 3,840 ms. After is an index scan at 46 ms. There is no time window. The same query ran on a laptop copy of a staging database (a private copy of the data, used for testing) with the same data volume. The source is the two saved plans, and anyone can re-check by running EXPLAIN ANALYZE on a copy. Her share is finding the query, reading the plan, choosing the index and writing the migration. Her lead reviewed it. The index is the one difference between the two plans. Permission is "redacted", with real table and column names removed. Status is ready. She quotes only actual times, never cost units.

**R2, the report p95.** The p95 is the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within. The draft line is the claim card wording from chapter 3.2. It says she cut the p95 of the orders report endpoint from 4.6 s to 0.41 s. The windows are 14 days before and after. The fix was finding a full-table scan and adding a composite index. The window is 1 to 14 May and 16 to 29 May 2025. The source is the team dashboard, owned by her lead, and a stranger can re-check by asking him to open those dates. The plan pair in R1 is a second source. What else changed: a front-end release on 20 May that did not touch the endpoint, and requests of 6,300 and 6,100, about 3 percent fewer afterwards. The deploy day, 15 May, sits in neither window. Her lead said yes to public use with the company name, table names and customer data removed, so the permission is "redacted". Status is ready.

**R3, the invoice time.** The line says she saved a print shop about 3 hours a week of manual invoicing, net of upkeep. The metric is the median (middle) minutes per invoice, 7 before and 1 after, from 64 and 61 timed invoices over two weeks each. The sums are 6 minutes saved on about 31 invoices, which is 186 minutes, minus about 7 minutes of weekly upkeep, which leaves 179 minutes, about 3 hours. The source is the timing sheet she kept with the shop's assistant. Her share is all of the script. The owner chose the invoice fields. Permission is "redacted", since the shop is "a print shop" in public. Status is ready.

**R4, the sign-up migration.** The line says she moved a youth coding club's sign-ups from three shared spreadsheets into one database: 412 rows in, 57 duplicates merged, 355 unique sign-ups imported. She checks the sum first: 412 minus 57 is 355. The source is the per-session row counts she made before and after the import. There is no time window, because the move happened once. The organisers chose the fields and tested the form, so her share is the form, the database and the migration. The counts are public-ok. The sheets themselves, which hold teenagers' details, stay private forever. Status is ready.

**R5, the double bookings.** The line says double-booked seats fell from 9 in the term before the form to 1 in the term after, counted from the organisers' group chat. She adds the caveat in the line itself: the club also cut sessions from three a week to two that term, so fewer bookings could clash. She does not claim the whole drop. The counts are public-ok and the chat is private. Status is ready, with the caveat.

**R6, the leaked key.** This row has no number, and that is fine. The claim is three acts: the key was revoked and replaced, secrets moved to environment variables with an example file, and push protection is on. A reader can check each one in the repository and its settings, and Areej never shows the key. She noticed and fixed it. A teammate agreed and reviewed. Public-ok. Ready.

**R7, the load test.** The line says she cut an events endpoint's p95 from 820 ms to 95 ms at a constant 50 requests per second. She used the same k6 script, data and laptop before and after. The window is two minutes per run. The source is the script and its output in the project's repository, and anyone can run it again. She wrote the cache and the test. A teammate wrote the endpoint. She states two caveats: it ran on a laptop, not production, and cached lists can be up to 30 seconds old. If she quoted a percent here, she would work it out as (820 minus 95) divided by 820, which is 0.884, or about 88 percent less time. She quotes the milliseconds. Public-ok. Ready.

**G1, the honest gap.** She never measured whether customers got invoices sooner. The row reads: "I did not measure whether customers got invoices sooner. To find out, I would ask the owner for the order and invoice timestamps for two weeks and compare the median gap with the spreadsheet history." No kit line uses this, and the row is complete.

## One failure, and how to spot it

Areej's old resume says "improved speed by 90%". When she opens the ledger to find that row, there is none. She cannot say of what, over which days, or measured how. She does not search her memory for a source that would fit. She deletes the line and uses the sourced pair in R2 instead. This is the ledger doing its job. A line with no row is a line to remove.

Here are other ways rows go wrong, and how to spot each. A percentage with no before and after: cover the row and ask "of what?". An averaged percentile from a dashboard: look for the words "average p95" and ask how it was computed. A saving that is gross, not net: check whether upkeep was subtracted. A window of "recently": ask for two dates. A team number claimed as yours: check that your share names something you did. A number you remember but cannot find: mark it "needs source". A screenshot with names left in: check the permission column before it goes anywhere. If a row survives all seven checks, it is ready.

## How to use AI on your own material

The rule from chapter 2.5 holds here. An AI tool may restructure your notes, but it may never add a number, a window or a share. This chapter has its own prompt, which asks the tool to find the gaps in one row:

```text
Here are my notes about one piece of work. Place them under these headings: metric, before, after, window, source, my share, what else changed. Do not add any number, date or fact that is not in my notes. For every heading you cannot fill from my notes, write MISSING and ask me one question about it.
```

Paste only notes with no secrets and no client, employer or customer names. If the tool fills a MISSING field with a figure, delete it. A row that looks complete because of an invented figure is exactly what the ledger exists to prevent. Check every filled field against your notes, and do every percentage and subtraction yourself.

Then do a small version of the empty-sentence edit from chapter 2.5. Read the draft kit line, remove every word that a reader cannot check, and see what is left. Chapter 6.4 returns to this edit. Add one line to the row's notes. For example: "I used an AI tool to place my notes under the headings; every number, window and share is mine and I checked each one."

## If you have little work, or no laptop

Chapter 2.5 gave the full path for this. Use Areej's material for the rows you cannot fill, and say that you did. Work on a phone in a notes app if you have no laptop. Nothing in this module needs software, and running k6, Lighthouse or EXPLAIN is optional.

This chapter adds one thing. You still need at least one row of your own, and it can be small. Time a task you do by hand, three times, and write the median (the middle time). That is a baseline, and a baseline with a window and a source is a real row. If you have nothing yet, write a gap row for the work you want to do, with how you would measure it.

## What done looks like

Your ledger is ready when each of these is true:

- At least three complete claim cards, every field filled or marked "not measured", and at least one from your own work.
- At least one honest gap with a concrete "how I would measure".
- Every number has a source and a way to re-check it, and every window is stated.
- The permission column is filled, and nothing marked private appears in any draft public line.
- The arithmetic is correct. Percentages were worked out from the before and after, and every subtraction was checked twice.
- Any row built with AI help carries the one-line note.

## Where to keep it, and what comes next

Keep the inventory and the ledger in a private note, never in a public repository or post. Back it up in a second place you control. The ledger is a working document, so update it when you measure something new. Module 5 uses the row behind your case study. Module 6 draws every resume number from it. Module 7 draws your profile lines. Module 9 asks you to map every number in your kit back to a row. At the gate in chapter 3.8, a mentor will ask you to defend your strongest rows, with the AI practice interviewer or in writing. Do the quiz in chapter 3.7 first, and bring your three best rows.
