Good work leaves a trace, and the trace is your proof. This chapter shows where the trace of each kind of work lives, so that you know where to look in your own history.

## Every kind of work leaves a trace

<MermaidDiagram title="Note the trace while you work" chart={`flowchart TD

  Q -->|"Yes"| A["Open the plan, chart or sheet and quote it"]

  B --> C["A number you cannot back up"]
`} legend="Two paths after the same work. Noting where the trace lives gives you proof you can open. Searching memory later gives you a number with nothing behind it." />

A speed fix leaves query plans and charts. A cleanup leaves a <GlossaryTerm term="pull request" definition="A proposed code change that other people review before it is added." /> and test runs. An automation leaves a timing sheet and counts. These traces usually exist already, so your first job is to know where each one lives.

<GoDeeper title="The list of places to look">

Here are the places proof tends to live. Cloud and database dashboards hold response times, errors and uptime. The history of a code repository holds the code you wrote or cleaned up, with dates and reviewers. Security work leaves settings and revocation records. Performance work leaves query plans, load test output and charts. A migration (moving data from one place or shape to another) leaves row counts and error counts from before and after. An automation leaves a timing sheet, a count of runs, and the hours saved. Work with users leaves database counts or analytics. Work that cuts cost leaves a bill or a cost report.

Areej's four roles show it. Her internship fix left a plan pair and a team dashboard chart. Her freelance invoice script left a timing sheet kept over four weeks. Her volunteer sign-up form left row counts per session before and after the move, and a count of double bookings. Her study project left records in the code host, and a load test script with its output.

The companion course, The Founder Interview, covers finding numbers you never looked at. This chapter asks something different. For each kind of work, where does the proof live, and may you show it?

</GoDeeper>

## Match the work to its proof

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-match-work-to-proof" />

Eight kinds of work, three kinds of proof: charts and plans, code and settings, counts and sheets. Tap the proof each piece of work leaves, or drag the card onto it. A wrong try tells you what that work leaves instead. None of the cards asks for a new number. The proof already exists. The job is to name where.

<GoDeeper title="The right match, with a reason for each">

A slow query fixed leaves a plan pair and a latency chart. A messy module cleaned up leaves the pull request and the test run before and after. A leaked key fixed leaves the revocation and the push protection setting, never the key. A data migration leaves row counts and error counts before and after. An automation leaves a timing sheet. A service kept running leaves the service dashboard for uptime. Users handled come from database counts or analytics. A cloud bill cut comes from the bill or the cost report.

Real work often leaves more than one trace. The cards show the first trace this course asks you to keep. Notice one more point. Some proof needs a stated period before it means anything. A cost figure needs a month. An uptime figure needs a window. You will see why in chapter 3.4.

When a piece of your own work has no trace at all, that is useful to know. It becomes an honest gap in your ledger, with a plan for how you would measure it next time.

</GoDeeper>

## Security proof shows the fix, not the secret

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-leaked-key" />

Areej found an email-service key in an early commit of her team's project. She revoked it and made a new one, moved secrets into environment variables with an example file, and turned on push protection. The proof is those three facts. The key itself is never shown.

<GoDeeper title="Why the file cannot simply be deleted">

A code repository keeps its history. Suppose a teammate pushed a key in an early commit. Deleting the file in a later commit leaves the key in the history, where anyone with access can still read it. Restarting the app does not stop a stolen key from working either. Security guidance says to rotate and revoke: make the old key stop working, and issue a new one. In plain words: never put a real key in your code. If a key was ever pushed, assume it is stolen and replace it.

An environment variable is a setting that lives outside the code, on the machine that runs it, so the code can ask for the key without containing it. An example file, often named `.env.example`, lists the names of the settings with placeholder values, so a teammate knows what to fill in without seeing a real key. Push protection is a feature of the code host. It checks a push for key formats it knows and blocks the push. It does not undo an earlier leak. This was checked in 2026-10, and you should trust the settings page on your own screen.

For your ledger, write the three facts: the key was revoked on a date, secrets now live in the environment, and push protection is on. Show a settings page with no key in it. Never paste the key, even a revoked one, into a case study or a post.

</GoDeeper>

## Time saved is net, not gross

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-time-saved-net" />

Areej's invoice script saves 6 minutes on each invoice: 7 minutes become 1. At about 31 invoices a week, that is 186 minutes gross. Upkeep takes about 7 minutes a week. The net is 179 minutes, about 3 hours a week. The gross number sounds better. The net number is the true one.

<GoDeeper title="The sum, and why to use a timer">

The sum has three steps. First, the saving on one task: 7 minutes before, minus 1 minute after, is 6 minutes. Second, multiply by how often it happens: 6 times 31 is 186 minutes a week. Third, subtract what the automation still costs you: 186 minus 7 minutes of upkeep is 179 minutes. And 179 divided by 60 is 2.98, so about 3 hours a week.

Where did 31 come from? Areej timed 64 invoices in the two weeks before the script, which is 32 a week, and 61 in the two weeks after, which is 30.5 a week. She used 31. The upkeep is about 30 minutes a month fixing the template when the owner changes it, which is about 7 minutes a week.

Her 9 hours of building pay back in about 3 weeks. Nine hours is 540 minutes, and 540 divided by 179 is about 3.0. Time the task as it is done today, and use the median. A timer beats memory, which is easy to bend. Consultants' writing says people recall tasks as quicker than they were, but that is practitioner advice, not research. If a vendor page says automation removes a fixed share of task time, ignore the share and time your own task. This arithmetic is the course's own method, taken from consultants' writing that is not research.

Her claim reads: "Saved the shop about 3 hours a week of manual invoicing, net of upkeep (median time per invoice 7 minutes to 1 minute, timed over two weeks before and two weeks after)."

</GoDeeper>

## Cost comes from the bill

<MermaidDiagram title="Memory or the report" chart={`flowchart TD
  C["A cost claim"] --> M["From memory: about half"]
  C --> R["From the cost report"]
  M --> U["A reader cannot check it"]
  R --> V["Figure, month and report named"]
  V --> W["A reader opens the same report"]
`} legend="Two sources for the same claim. A figure from memory cannot be checked. A figure from the report carries its month and its report, so a reader can open it and see." />

Take the figure from the bill or the cost report, never from memory. Open the report, pick one month, filter by one service or tag, and read the total. Then write the figure, the month and the report you read it from. You do not need a cloud account to learn this rule.

<GoDeeper title="An example report, and a template for the claim">

One example of a cost report is AWS Cost Explorer. When we checked it (2026-10), it showed up to 13 months of history and refreshed at least every 24 hours. Some data arrives late, so the last day or two can still change. Using its web screen was free, and requests to its programming interface carried a small charge. Your provider may differ, so trust your own screen.

Areej has no cost row, so here is a template with no invented numbers. "Cut the monthly cost of [service] from [figure] in [month] to [figure] in [month] (cost report, filtered by [service or tag]); [what I changed], [what else changed]." If you cannot fill every bracket, you do not have the claim yet. Compare two whole months, and remember that months differ in length: February has 28 days and March has 31. Ask yourself what else changed, for example the number of users in each month.

A cost cut often comes with a trade. Say if speed or safety gave something up, because a reader will ask.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask before you show employer data

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-ask-first" />

Areej asks her lead if she may show the <GlossaryTerm term="p95" definition="The time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within. One request in 20 was slower." /> chart in her portfolio. He says yes if the company name, table names and customer data are removed. Asking took one short message. When in doubt, keep the number private.

<GoDeeper title="A note you can adapt">

A dashboard that belongs to your employer is not yours to publish, even when you built the fix behind it. Ask first. Here is a short note that works. "May I show the p95 chart of the report endpoint in my portfolio, with the company name, table names and any customer data removed?" Name the number, say where it will appear, and say what you will remove. A clear request is easy to say yes to.

If the answer is yes with conditions, follow the conditions and write them down in the permission column of your ledger. If the answer is no, or you are not sure, write "private" and keep the number out of public material. A private number is still useful. You can say it in an interview if your lead agrees, and it still proves to you what you did.

Areej's lead said yes. In her private ledger the company appears by name. In public she writes "a parcel delivery company's back office" and uses the redacted chart. Permission is a rule of this course, not legal advice.

</GoDeeper>
