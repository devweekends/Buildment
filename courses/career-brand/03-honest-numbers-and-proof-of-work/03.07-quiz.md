```quiz
type: mcq
question: "In these made-up cases, the people and the numbers are invented. Idris is a backend developer at a retail platform. He changed a query so that the search page loads faster. The team dashboard, which his lead owns, shows a median load time of 9 s for the 14 days before and 2 s for the 14 days after. His lead reviewed the change. A banner release landed in the middle of the window, and traffic stayed flat. Which line has all seven parts of a claim card: what was measured, the before, the after, the window, the source, his share and what else changed?"
options:
  - "Cut the median load time of a retail platform's search page from 9 s to 2 s, measured over the 14 days before and the 14 days after my work, so shoppers found products faster"
  - "Search page load time down 78 percent over the 14 days before and after my work, per the team dashboard, which shows search is now much quicker for every shopper in every region"
  - "We sped up search: median load fell from 9 s to 2 s over 14 days before and after, per the team dashboard, and shoppers noticed the difference straight away that same week"
  - "Cut median search load from 9 s to 2 s (team dashboard, 14 days either side) by changing the query; my lead reviewed it; a banner release landed mid-window, traffic flat"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "It has a metric, a before, an after and a window, but no source and no share. A reader cannot tell where 9 s and 2 s came from, or what Idris did himself."
  - "It has a source and a window, but 'down 78 percent' hides the 9 s and the 2 s it came from. It also gives no share and says nothing about what else changed."
  - "It has everything except his share and what else changed. 'We sped up search' hides what Idris did, and nothing says that a banner release landed in the window."
  - "Right. It names the metric, the before and after, the window, the source, his share (changing the query, reviewed by his lead) and what else changed. Each part is short, so a reader can check any one."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Fidaa is a support engineer. She is shown a tile on a service she does not run: 'average response time 120 ms'. She wants to quote it. A p95 is the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within, and a p99 is the time that 99 of 100 finish within. What is the most useful next question?"
options:
  - "Can she round it down to 100 ms so the number looks cleaner on her resume?"
  - "Which statistic is it, and what do p95 and p99 show for the same window?"
  - "Is 120 ms quicker than the services that other companies run today?"
  - "Who is the most senior person who reads this dashboard each week?"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "Rounding a number in your favour is a form of deception, and it does not tell her what the number means. The first question is about the definition."
  - "Right. An average can hide slow requests. Asking which statistic it is, and what the p95 and p99 show over the same window, tells her whether slow users are hidden behind the 120 ms."
  - "Other companies' services are a benchmark with no definition. She cannot compare 120 ms with anything until she knows what it measures and how."
  - "Seniority is not evidence. Who reads the dashboard does not change what the number means. The statistic and the window do."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Louay is an SRE intern. A dashboard tile reads 'average p95 across 3 servers: 220 ms'. It was built from p95 values of 180, 200 and 280 ms, and 660 divided by 3 is 220. What is wrong with the tile, and what should he ask for?"
options:
  - "Nothing is wrong, because averaging the three servers smooths out the noise in each one of them"
  - "It should show the highest of the three servers, 280 ms, because that is the real p95 figure"
  - "A percentile cannot be averaged; ask for the p95 of all requests together, or for summed buckets"
  - "It should show the median of the three servers, 200 ms, because the median ignores the outlier"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "Averaging does not smooth percentiles. It produces a number that no request had, and it can hide the slow requests of one server."
  - "The highest of three p95 values is not the p95 of all requests either. The overall p95 depends on how many requests each server handled."
  - "Right. A percentile is a position in a sorted line of requests. Merge the requests, or add the bucket counts first, and read the p95 from the result."
  - "The median of three p95 values is another number that no request had. It also ignores how many requests each server served."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Salah is a backend developer. He added a cache to an events endpoint and wants to say that the cache made the endpoint faster. Which test design supports that claim best?"
options:
  - "Run the same load script on the same data and machine at the same arrival rate before and after, with the cache as the only change"
  - "Compare this month's dashboard figures with last month's, since the cache went live between the two months and nothing else was touched"
  - "Run the new version once on his laptop and quote that result, since one clean run is enough to show the gain from the cache"
  - "Compare the endpoint with a friend's similar service that has no cache, since both are web services and both serve the same kind of list"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. With the script, the data, the machine and the arrival rate fixed, only the cache changed. So the cache is the only thing that can explain the difference."
  - "Two months differ in traffic, season and other releases, and 'nothing else was touched' is a guess. The cache cannot be named as the only cause of the difference."
  - "One run is weak proof. A single clean run can differ from the next, so the result may be luck, and there is no before to compare it with."
  - "A friend's service has different data, a different machine and a different load. The gap between the two cannot be traced to the cache."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Tamara is an operations analyst. She wrote a script for a weekly report that she runs 12 times a week. The report took 9 minutes by hand and now takes 2 minutes, including a check of the output. The script needs about 15 minutes of upkeep a week. An AI tool says: '9 x 12 = 108 minutes saved a week.' Which statement about the AI's answer is correct?"
options:
  - "108 is right, because her own upkeep time does not count as time spent on the task"
  - "It is gross; with the check and the upkeep the saving is 69 minutes a week"
  - "84 is right, because upkeep never belongs in a time saving, only the check does"
  - "93 is right, because only the upkeep needs to come off the 108 minutes a week"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "Her upkeep is time she still spends because of the script. It comes off the saving, so 108 overstates it."
  - "Right. 108 is the gross saving. Each run still takes 2 minutes, so the saving per run is 9 minus 2, which is 7, and 7 times 12 is 84. Minus 15 minutes of upkeep leaves 69 minutes a week."
  - "84 is net of the check but not of the upkeep. The upkeep is real time too, and 84 minus 15 is 69."
  - "93 is 108 minus the upkeep, but it forgets that each run still takes 2 minutes for the check. The saving per run is 7 minutes, not 9."
```
