## Your task: your strongest claim card, defended

This task shows that a number in your kit has something real behind it. In module 9 a mentor will pick any number in your kit and ask where it came from. This is the first rehearsal. Nobody is graded on how impressive the number is. A small number with a good card beats a big number with a blank one.

Write about 150 words. Paste your strongest claim card, with all seven parts: what was measured, the before, the after, the window, the source, your share and what else changed. Then say how a stranger could re-check it, meaning what they would open or whom they would ask. Add your honest gap from the ledger in one or two sentences, and how you would measure it. If you did not finish the AI drill below, also defend two more ledger rows in writing, for about 150 more words. For each, give the source, the window, your share and what else changed. Remove employer, client and customer names first.

A strong answer, from a made-up fellow, reads like this. Ghaida built a registration form for her university's debating society. She wrote: "Metric: median minutes from a sign-up to a confirmation message. Before: 12 minutes. After: 3 minutes. Window: I timed 10 sign-ups in the first two weeks of term before the form, and 10 in the first two weeks after. Source: my timing sheet, with the committee's message log as a second source. A stranger could ask for the sheet, or time five new sign-ups the same way. My share: I built the form and the confirmation message. The committee chose the questions. What else changed: two committee members were on duty in the later fortnight, and one before. That may explain part of the gain, so I say it in the card. Gap: I did not measure whether more members came to the first meeting. I would ask the secretary for the attendance lists of the last two terms and compare them."

What makes it work? Every part has a value. The source says what a stranger would open. Her share is separate from the committee's. Her "what else changed" is a fact that works against her, and she wrote it anyway. The gap comes with a plan that another person could follow.

A weak answer sounds like this. "My team made the app 80 percent faster and everyone loved it. I led the work, and the dashboard shows it." It gives no starting number, so 80 percent of what is unknown. It gives no window. "The dashboard" has no owner and no dates, so no stranger could open it. "My team" and "I led" blur the share, and nothing says what else changed. There is no gap. The reader's first question, "how was that measured?", has no answer, and that is the question a founder will ask.

If you prefer to use Areej's case, use her strongest card, the report p95 from the ledger in chapter 3.6. Her gap is the one about whether customers got invoices sooner. For the two extra rows, use her invoice time and her load test. Say that you used her material.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer part by part. Each part is Met, Partly or Not yet. This gate is a full review, so the mentor reads every part.

**Part 1: Claim card complete.**

- **Met:** All seven fields have a value or an honest "not measured".
- **Partly:** One or two fields are missing.
- **Not yet:** Three or more fields are missing, or the answer is a bare number.

**Part 2: Re-checkable source.**

- **Met:** The answer says where the data lives and what a stranger would open or ask for, and it states the window so the look can be repeated.
- **Partly:** The source is named but not how to re-check it, or there is no window.
- **Not yet:** There is no source.

**Part 3: Ownership.**

- **Met:** Your share is stated apart from the team's, with at least one thing you did and one thing someone else did (or "alone").
- **Partly:** The share is blurred.
- **Not yet:** It says "we" only, or it claims the whole change.

**Part 4: Honest gap.**

- **Met:** One gap with a concrete plan: whom to ask, for what, over what period.
- **Partly:** A gap with no plan.
- **Not yet:** No gap.

**Part 5: Defence.**

- **Met:** The drill is finished, or two rows are defended with source, window, share and what else changed for each.
- **Partly:** The drill is started, or one row is defended, or two rows are defended with two or three of the four items.
- **Not yet:** Missing.

**Pass rule:** every part is at least Partly, and "Claim card complete" and "Re-checkable source" are both Met.

Your mentor then replies with one to three questions about your strongest claim. The mentor may ask: "What else changed in that window, and how do you know it did not move the number?" Or: "Where would I click to see the before number?" Or: "What did the team do, and what did you do?" You answer in writing or in a voice note. Your answer to that follow-up completes the gate, so look out for it after you submit. The companion course, The Founder Interview, lets you practise the same defence aloud.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks an opening question about your own strongest claim cards. It then follows up on how each number was measured and what your part was. It ends with feedback: your strengths, what to do better, named flags and next steps, including one thing to measure or build next. The flags are the five from gate 1.6. Paste your three strongest claim cards, with employer, client and customer names removed. Or paste Areej's three: the report p95, the invoice time and the load test.

Answer honestly. "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers. The drill is practice, not a hiring decision, and its feedback can be wrong. AI tools tend to agree with you and to like long answers, so check every point against your own cards. The drill gives no score. Read the level word that starts each note: Strong, Getting there or Not yet.

Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead, such as "a delivery company" or "my team lead". If you do not have a key or credit, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "How was that measured?"
opening: "Take your strongest number from your claim cards. How did you measure it, and over what time?"
focus: [impact, five-whys]
context: project
maxTurns: 8
whyDepth: 4
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows that each claim card has a metric, a before, an after, a window, a source someone else could re-check, the learner's share and what else changed. Probe each number: 'How did you measure it? Over what window? Where can I see it?' Ask which statistic it is (mean, median, p95), whether any percentile was averaged, whether a saving is gross or net of upkeep, and who owns the dashboard. If the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'; on a technical choice ask 'What was the alternative?'. Accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would measure next; never push the learner to guess or invent a number or a reason. An honest gap with a plan to measure scores higher than an unsourced number. Judge features, not length, and quote the learner's words. Begin each focus-area note with a level word: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Next steps: one thing to measure next or one small thing to build next around the learner's own needs, never a named project idea. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person."
```
