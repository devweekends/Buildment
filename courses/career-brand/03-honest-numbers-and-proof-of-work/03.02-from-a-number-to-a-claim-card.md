Tawfiq's 70 percent was true and still not his to claim. This chapter gives you a tool for that: a claim card, with a slot for each part of a number. Tawfiq and Areej are fictional fellows, and their numbers are illustrative.

## "Helped grow the business 50 percent"

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-empty-claim-card" />

Take the sentence "helped grow the business 50 percent". A reader asks five short questions: measured what, before and after, which weeks, from where, and your part. Together they cover six parts of a claim card. Every part comes back empty. The sentence sounds strong, and it gives the reader nothing to check or to ask about.

<GoDeeper title="Embellishing and leaving out are both deception">

Researchers who study resume deception measure three forms. Fabrication is inventing something that did not happen. Embellishment is inflating something that did. Omission is leaving something out in order to mislead. All three count, and the second and third are the ones people forget.

Many fellows think that "rounding up" is a safe middle ground between honest and false. It is not. A rise of 12 percent is not "about 15 percent". A line that leaves out the bigger machines, so that the whole gain looks like yours, is an omission. A simple test helps: if the reader would think differently after learning the missing fact, the line misleads.

This is not a reason to be afraid of numbers. It is a reason to attach the missing facts to them. "The run time fell by 70 percent the week the platform team changed the machines, and my join step went from 50 to 20 minutes" is longer, and it is much safer. A reader who finds out the whole story later will trust the rest of your kit more.

</GoDeeper>

## A percent needs its starting number

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-percent-needs-start" />

"Up 12 percent" is true for a product that grew from 25 users to 28. It is also true for one that grew from 10,000 to 11,200. These are not the same news. A percentage hides the number it started from, so the starting number always travels with it.

<GoDeeper title="How to work out a percent, and one word to watch">

To find a percent change, subtract the before from the after, then divide by the before. For the small product: 28 minus 25 is 3, and 3 divided by 25 is 0.12, which is 12 percent. For the large one: 11,200 minus 10,000 is 1,200, and 1,200 divided by 10,000 is also 0.12. Always divide by the before, never by the after.

A well-known resume formula from a former hiring head at a large technology company asks for three things in a bullet: what you accomplished, measured by what, and by doing what. It also warns that a percentage alone misleads. That is advice from practice, from a post written in 2014, not research. If you took course 14, "The Founder Interview", you met the same idea there: a number needs a before.

Now the word to watch. Tawfiq's run went from 5 hours to 1.5 hours. The time fell by 70 percent. The run became about 3.3 times as fast, because 5 divided by 1.5 is 3.33. Read strictly, "70 percent faster" would mean only 1.7 times as fast. Both readings are in use, so the safest line gives the two times: "from 5 hours to 1.5 hours".

</GoDeeper>

## Fill the claim card

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-claim-card" />

A claim card gives each part of a number its own slot. This one has six: metric, before, after, window, source and your share. Tap a chip, then the slot it fills, or drag it. Some chips fit no slot: a feeling, an AI guess and the team's 70 percent. Read why each one is refused. The finished card reads as one sentence a reader can check.

<GoDeeper title="Five parts, plus two of this course's own">

A good way to say it: a number is a claim with five parts. They are what was measured, the before, the after, the window and the source. If you cannot name the source, do not print the number.

This course adds two more parts. The first is your share: what you did, and what other people did. The second is what else changed in the same window. Together they make seven. The card above holds six slots, and the seventh, what else changed, gets its own picture in the next beat. For Tawfiq, the machines sit in his share chip because that is where the card can hold them.

His finished card says: cut the run time of the join step in the nightly run from 50 to 20 minutes (nightly job logs, the nights before and after my change); I rewrote the join step, and new machines helped the whole run that week. Check each part. The metric is the join step's run time. The before and after are 50 and 20 minutes. The source is the job logs. The share is the rewrite. A real card would also give the dates, or the number of nights, in the window. Here the window is described and not counted, because the story does not give a number.

The 70 percent is not wrong, and it does not vanish. It stays in his private notes as a fact about the team's run. It is just not his claim.

</GoDeeper>

## What else changed in the window

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-what-else-changed" />

Areej's index shipped on 15 May. A front-end release landed on 20 May, inside her window. Her card names that release and gives the request counts: 6,300 before and 6,100 after, about 3 percent fewer. So traffic does not explain the change. Naming what else changed is the seventh part of the card.

<GoDeeper title="Why one before and after cannot prove the cause">

When you compare one period before with one period after, many things can differ besides your change. Traffic can rise or fall. The season can change. Another release can ship. A single before and after cannot separate your change from all of these. This is reasoning from experiment design, and it is why a test you control (chapter 3.5) is stronger.

So you do two honest things. You say what else changed. And you say what you checked. Areej's windows are 14 days each: 1 to 14 May, and 16 to 29 May. The deploy day, 15 May, sits in neither. The team dashboard, which her lead owns, shows the <GlossaryTerm term="p95" definition="The time that 95 of every 100 requests beat. One request in 20 was slower. Chapter 3.4 explains it with a picture." /> of the report endpoint at 4.6 seconds before and 0.41 seconds after. The front-end release on 20 May did not touch that endpoint. The requests were 6,300 and 6,100, which is 200 fewer, and 200 divided by 6,300 is about 3 percent.

Here is her card in one sentence: "Cut the p95 of the orders report endpoint from 4.6 s to 0.41 s (14 days before and after, team dashboard) by finding a full-table scan and adding a composite index; I found and fixed it, my lead reviewed and scheduled the release." A full-table scan means the database read every row of a table to answer one question. An index works like the index of a book: it lets the database jump to the right rows. A composite index covers two columns together.

What Areej does not write is "the index alone caused the whole drop". She writes what she changed, what she measured, and what else happened.

</GoDeeper>

## One row per claim: the ledger

<MermaidDiagram title="The life of a claim" chart={`flowchart TD
  W["Work"] --> E["Evidence"]
  E --> C["Claim card"]
  C --> K["Placed in the kit"]
  K --> Q["Questioned"]
  Q --> D["Defended or corrected"]
`} legend="A claim starts as work and leaves evidence. The evidence becomes a card, the card goes into your resume or profile, and someone questions it. You defend it from the card, or you correct it." />

A claim has a life. Work leaves evidence. The evidence becomes a claim card. The card goes into your resume or profile. Someone questions the line. You then defend it from your card, or you correct it. Keep one row for each claim in one table. That table is your claim ledger, and every number in your kit is drawn from it.

<GoDeeper title="What a ledger row holds">

The ledger is this course's method. Each row holds the draft line from your kit, the number, where it came from, how someone can check it again, your share, and what else changed. Chapter 3.6 gives you the full template, and chapter 3.8 asks you to defend two rows.

The ledger does two jobs. It stops you from printing a number you cannot back up, because a line with no row does not go into the kit. And it saves you work, because when a resume line, a profile line and a blog post all need the same number, they all copy it from one row. If a number is wrong, you correct it in one place.

In module 6 you write your resume from the ledger. In module 9 a mentor picks any number in your kit, and you show the row it came from. Starting the ledger now makes that day calm.

</GoDeeper>

## "Not measured" is a complete answer

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m3-not-measured" />

A reader asks Areej whether customers got her invoices sooner. She never measured that. Her card says so and holds a plan: ask the owner for two weeks of order and invoice times, and compare. A gap with a plan is a finished row.

<GoDeeper title="How to write an honest gap">

Use this shape: "I did not measure [the thing]. To find out, I would ask [whom] for [what], over [which period], and compare [with what]." Areej's line reads: "I did not measure whether customers got invoices sooner. To find out, I would ask the owner for the order and invoice timestamps for two weeks and compare the median gap with the spreadsheet history." The median is the middle value when you sort the numbers.

The plan has to be specific enough that another person could carry it out. "I would look into it" is not a plan. Notice also what the gap does for you. It tells a reader that you know where your evidence stops, and that makes the evidence before that point easier to believe.

There is lab research that points the same way. A combined analysis of 20 studies found that people telling the truth about an event gave more details that others could check than people who were lying. That research is about accounts of events, not resumes, and it needs more study. Use it only as a reason to prefer claims that others can check.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

The three forms of resume deception come from Henle, Dineen and Duffy, who built and tested a measure of it in a paper published in 2019. The resume formula is practitioner advice, not research. The point about a single before and after is reasoning from how experiments are designed. The claim card and the ledger are this course's own methods. The lab research on checkable details is a combined analysis of studies about accounts of events.

</GoDeeper>
