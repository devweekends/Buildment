A resume line is the last step. The first step is to write down everything behind each role, in detail, while you still remember it. Areej is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative. You will use her cards as models for your own.

## One line hides a whole role

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-one-line-opens" />

A resume line is short: "Intern, 2025." Behind it sit three things: what Areej did and what her lead did, the numbers that changed, and the permission to show them. Your career arc keeps all three. You choose later what to show. The resume line is the last step, not the first.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's internship card, field by field">

This is the finished card. It is private, so it holds names that a public page would not.

**Role and dates.** Intern in the back-office team of a parcel delivery company called Darb Parcels, February to July 2025.

**Situation.** Operations staff opened a daily orders report many times a day, and it was slow. The query behind it filtered the orders table, about 2.4 million rows, by status and date, and sorted it by date.

**What I did.** I found the query in the database's slow query log. I ran EXPLAIN ANALYZE, which runs a query and shows the real time each step takes, and saw that the database read the whole table. I chose an index on status and creation date. I wrote the migration, the small script that adds the index.

**What the team did.** My lead reviewed the change and scheduled it for a low-traffic hour. It went live on 15 May 2025.

**Result.** On the team dashboard, the p95 of the report endpoint fell from 4.6 s to 0.41 s, each figure over 14 days. In the query plan, the execution time fell from 3,840 ms to 46 ms. Requests in the two windows were 6,300 and 6,100. A front-end release on 20 May did not touch this endpoint.

**Where the proof lives.** The team dashboard, which my lead owns. Also the before and after plans, which I ran on my laptop copy of a staging database with the same amount of data.

**May I show it?** Yes, with the company name and the real table and column names removed, and no customer data. My lead told me so. In public I write "a parcel delivery company's back office".

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Why keep more than the resume will hold">

A one-page resume has room for three or four lines per role. If you write only those lines, you lose the rest. Later you may need the 20 May detail for an interview, or the permission rule for a case study. Your memory will not hold it. A card does.

There is a second reason. When you write a role in full, you often find a strength you did not see. Many people undersell the work that felt ordinary. Writing the situation, your steps and the result in plain words shows how much happened.

Keep the card private. It may hold a company name, an unpublished number or a person's role. The "may I show it?" field is how you decide, one role at a time, what can leave the page.

</GoDeeper>

## "I did" and "the team did"

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-found-vs-team" />

The report fix had seven steps. Areej did four: she found the query, ran the plan, chose the index and wrote the migration. Her lead did three: reviewed, scheduled and deployed, which means put it live. A sentence about the result should name both. Saying only "I" would hide three of the seven steps.

<GoDeeper title="Why name the team's part">

Researchers who study resume deception describe three kinds: fabrication, which is inventing; embellishment, which is inflating; and omission, which is leaving something out to mislead. So deception is not only a made-up job. Leaving out the team's part so that yours looks bigger is embellishment, even when every word you wrote is true. We keep this at the level of the researchers' summary: all three kinds count.

Naming the team's part also helps you. A reader who sees both parts knows you understand how work gets done. A reader who sees only "I" starts to wonder what is missing. In an interview, "my lead scheduled it" is a sentence you can say without worry, because it is true.

In course 14, "The Founder Interview", you practise saying your part and the team's part aloud under questions. Here you write it down first.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="How to rewrite a 'we' sentence">

Take the sentence: "We cut the report time." Ask one question for each step: who did this step? Write the steps as a list. Areej's list is the seven in the picture. Then write two halves. "I found the query, ran the plan, chose the index and wrote the migration." "My lead reviewed it, scheduled it and deployed it."

You may have done all of it. Then write "alone", and add who decided what. Areej's invoice script for a print shop was all hers. The shop owner chose the invoice fields. That is a complete and honest card.

Avoid "led", "managed" and "owned" unless that is exactly what happened. If you were one of four people, you were one of four. Areej's gaps list says she has not led a team, and her cards do not suggest otherwise.

</GoDeeper>

## Fill the role card

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-role-card" />

Fill Areej's volunteer card. It has six fields: situation, what I did, what the team did, result, proof, and whether she may show it. Tap a chip, then the field it fills, or drag the chip onto the field. A skill word, a boast and a number that credits the wrong cause fit no field. Read the reason when a chip is refused.

<GoDeeper title="The six fields, and why each is there">

Situation is where things stood before you started, so a reader knows what the problem was. What I did and what the team did keep your part clear. Result is what changed, in numbers where you have them. Proof says where those numbers can be checked. May I show it says what can go in public.

You will use the same fields in chapter 2.5. There, a role card also starts with the role and dates, so you can tell the cards apart.

"Where the proof lives" may honestly be "unknown" for now. Write it and move on. Module 3 teaches you how to find the proof and keep it. An "unknown" is a to-do. It is not a failure.

For "may I show it", use one of three answers: yes, yes with names removed, or ask first. If you are not sure, write "ask first". Asking costs a short message. A wrong guess may cost you a job or a friendship.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What the refused chips teach">

"Great at databases" is a skill word. It might be true. It does not belong on a role card, because the card is about what happened in this role. The skill appears in the story of what she did.

"Double bookings fell 9 to 1, thanks to the form" credits the form for the whole drop. But the club also cut its sessions from three a week to two in the same term, so fewer bookings could clash. The honest card says both. That sentence is the hardest to write, and it is the one a reader trusts most.

"I led the whole club" is a boast. Areej was one organiser on a team. The organisers chose the fields and tested the form. Writing "I led" would take their part and give it to her.

</GoDeeper>

## Four kinds of skill, each with an example

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-skills-not-adjectives" />

Skills come in four kinds: hard, soft, leadership and initiative. Each card starts as an adjective and changes into one example from Areej's work. The adjectives were all true, and none could be asked about. Each example can be followed by a question. A skill with no example is only a claim, as you saw in module 1.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's four examples">

Hard skill: PostgreSQL query plans, indexing, Node.js and a load-testing tool called k6. A hard skill is one that can be tested. Soft skill: she explained the new index to her lead in writing, and she worked with a shop owner who is not technical. Leadership: she trained two new organisers at the youth coding club to use the sign-up form. Initiative: she found the slow query without being asked, and she found the leaked key in the Campus Board code.

Leadership here means helping other people do something. It does not need a title. Her gaps list still says she has not led a team. Both are true, and she can say both without a problem.

These four kinds are this course's way to sort your material. They are not a research model. If another list suits you better, use it. What matters is that each skill has an example.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="How to find an example for each kind">

Hard skill: open the cards you have written and underline each tool or method that was needed to get the result. Soft skill: find a time you explained something to someone who did not know it, or worked with a person very different from you. Leadership: find a time you helped someone else get better at something. Initiative: find a time you saw a problem and acted before anyone asked.

If one kind has no example, leave it empty. An empty slot is honest. An invented example is not. In chapter 2.5 the empty ones go on your gaps list.

</GoDeeper>

## Proof lives somewhere you can name

<MermaidDiagram title="Each result points to a place" chart={`flowchart TD

  B["Invoice time, 7 min to 1 min"] --> B2["Timing sheet, two weeks each side"]
  C["Double bookings, 9 to 1"] --> C2["Count from the organisers' group chat"]
`} legend="Each result on the left points to the place where a stranger could look, on the right. If a result had no arrow, it would be a claim." />

Each result points to a place a stranger could look: a team dashboard, a timing sheet, a chat count. If you do not know where your proof lives, write "not measured yet" or "unknown". That is honest and allowed. Module 3 shows how to find proof and keep it, and how to say what else changed.

<GoDeeper title="What counts as a place">

A place is somewhere a person could look, even if only you can open it today. A dashboard owned by your lead is a place. You may need permission to show it, and that is the "may I show it" field. A sheet of timings that you kept is a place. A count from a group chat is a place, because you can show the messages.

Areej's three examples come from three kinds of source. The report time is in a tool her team runs. The invoice time is in a sheet she made with the shop's assistant, over two weeks before the script and two weeks after. The double bookings come from the organisers' group chat, which she counted by hand.

None of these is perfect. A hand count in a chat is weaker than a system log. That is fine. Say how it was counted, and a reader will know how much to trust it.

</GoDeeper>

## Study and volunteer roles count

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-volunteer-full" />

Areej's volunteer card is as full as her internship card: 412 rows, 57 duplicates, 355 imported, and double bookings from 9 to 1. The card also holds a caveat: sessions dropped from three to two a week. The caveat is part of the card. It makes the other numbers believable.

<GoDeeper title="If you have no paid work yet">

Every learner has at least one study or volunteer role. A school or university project counts. So does helping in a family business, running a class group, or organising a club. You do not need a title or a salary. You need a situation, something you did, and a result you can describe.

Start with the role you remember best. Write the situation first. Then write what you did, step by step, and what others did. The numbers may be small. A form that saved an organiser ten minutes a week is a result. A count of mistakes before and after is a result. If you have no number, write what changed and who noticed.

Do not make a small role sound large. A reader who finds a small, honest role believes you. A reader who finds a large role they cannot check does not.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="How Areej counted the club's numbers">

Areej imported sign-ups from three shared spreadsheets edited by three organisers. There were 412 rows in all. She found 57 duplicates and merged them. 412 minus 57 is 355 unique sign-ups. She checked the import by counting rows per session before and after.

She counted double-booked seats from the organisers' group chat: 9 in the term before the form, and 1 in the term after. Then she added what else changed. The club cut its sessions from three a week to two in the second term. That change alone could have reduced clashes. So her card says the number fell from 9 to 1, and that sessions also dropped. She does not claim the whole drop.

</GoDeeper>
