## Why this document comes first

You have spent four chapters looking at how a strength becomes a pillar, how a role becomes a card, and how other people help you find your material. Now you write all of it into one place. This chapter is the longest in the module, and it is the first real piece of your kit. Plan for about 70 minutes. Most fellows will need two sittings, and that is fine.

The order matters. A resume written first fills with duties and adjectives, because the writer has not yet gathered what happened. In module 1 you saw what a reader makes of lines like that. The career arc fixes it by putting the evidence first. It collects what you did, in detail, and says where the proof lives. It is long, plain and private. Nobody reads it but you, your mentor and, if you choose, a tool you control.

Everything later is drawn from it. In module 3 you turn its role cards and pillars into claims with numbers you can source. You keep them in a claim ledger, which is a table that lists each number with its source. In modules 6, 7 and 8 you draw your resume, your LinkedIn profile and your writing from those claims. So a small mistake here travels a long way. A number you cannot source, or a part of the work that was not yours, will reappear in every later piece. Take the time to get it right once.

## The nine parts

The arc has nine parts. Each one exists for a reason, which the numbered list gives in a few words.

1. **Values:** two or three words you would defend, each with one action that shows it. They stop you from writing a value you cannot back.
2. **Pillars:** two or three strengths, each with the strength in one line, two proofs and where they live, and one number or an honest gap.
3. **What makes you different:** one line that a reader can test by opening your work.
4. **Skills by kind:** hard, soft, leadership and initiative, with one example each.
5. **One human detail:** optional, true and small, with the one place where it may appear.
6. **Best-self asks:** the three people you asked, what they said, and what repeats. Or a date by which you will send the asks.
7. **Role cards:** one card for every role, including study and volunteer roles.
8. **Gaps:** things you have not measured, skills you cannot yet show, and the one you will close first.
9. **Privacy marks:** a mark on every item, and one line about any AI use.

The most useful habit is to work role by role first, and fill the other parts from what you find. Pillars and values are easier to write once you can see your cards in front of you. If you are stuck on a pillar, read your best-self replies and write what repeats.

## The template

Copy this outline into a document you own: a notes app on your phone, a plain text file, a word-processor file or a notebook you photograph. Use whatever you can open when you need it. Fill the parts in any order. Where you have nothing yet, write "unknown" or "none yet" and move on. An honest blank is better than a guess.

```text
CAREER ARC (private). Last updated: [date]

1. VALUES (two or three)
- Value: [one or two words]
  Action that shows it: [one thing I did]
  Privacy: private | public-ok | ask first

2. PILLARS (two or three)
Pillar 1: [one plain line naming a strength]
- Proof 1: [what it was] | Lives at: [place] | My part: [...]
- Proof 2: [what it was] | Lives at: [place] | My part: [...]
- Number: [number, window, source] OR Gap: [not measured; how I would]
  Privacy: private | public-ok | ask first

3. WHAT MAKES ME DIFFERENT (one line)
A [role] who [habit], and [how I show it].
  Privacy: ...

4. SKILLS BY KIND (one example each, or "no example yet")
- Hard: ...
- Soft: ...
- Leadership: ...
- Initiative: ...

5. ONE HUMAN DETAIL (optional; write "skipped" if you skip)
Detail: [one true line]   May appear: [one place]

6. BEST-SELF ASKS
- [person's role] | sent on [date] | reply in short: ...
What repeats: ...   (or: Plan to send by [date])

7. ROLE CARDS (one for each role)
Role and dates: ...
Situation: ...
I did: ...
The team did: ...
Result: ...
Where the proof lives: ... (or "unknown")
May I show it in public: yes | yes, names removed | no | ask first

8. GAPS
- Not measured: ...
- Skill not yet shown: ...
- First gap I will close: ...

9. PRIVACY MARKS AND AI USE
Every item above ends with: private | public-ok | ask first
AI use (only if you used a tool): what I asked, what I kept, what I removed.
```

Every item in parts 1 to 8 ends with a privacy mark, and part 9 reminds you of the three marks. "Private" means it never leaves your notes. "Public-ok" means it may appear in public as written. "Ask first" means you need a person's permission before it goes anywhere, such as your lead, the shop owner or the club organisers. When you are unsure, choose "ask first".

## A worked example: Areej's arc

Areej is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative. Here is how she wrote her arc, step by step. She did not start at part 1.

Step 1: she began with the role she remembered best, the internship. She wrote its card using the fields from chapter 2.3. Where she was unsure of a number, she wrote "unknown" and carried on. Step 2: she wrote cards for the other three roles in the same way: the freelance invoice script, the volunteer sign-up form and the Campus Board study project.

Step 3: she read all four cards and asked the same question of each: what is the strength here, and which two cards prove it? Two cards showed the same habit of making something faster and measuring it. That became pillar 1. Two others showed automation for small teams: pillar 2. The leaked key and the permission she asked for before using the company chart became pillar 3. Step 4: she wrote her three values and found one action for each. Step 5: she read her three best-self replies and wrote what repeated. It matched pillar 1 and gave her the habit for her one-line difference.

Step 6: she filled skills, the human detail and the gaps. Her gaps are honest and short: no production uptime numbers of her own, she has not led a team, and her project has no real user yet. Step 7: she marked every item. Here is pillar 1 as she wrote it.

```text
Pillar 1: I make slow things fast, and I measure it.
- Proof 1: orders report, internship | Lives at: team dashboard (my lead owns it);
  before and after query plans on my laptop copy of a staging database
  | My part: found the query, ran the plan, chose the index, wrote the migration
- Proof 2: events endpoint, Campus Board | Lives at: k6 test script in the repository
  | My part: wrote the cache and the test; a teammate wrote the endpoint
- Number: p95 4.6 s to 0.41 s (14 days before and after, dashboard);
  p95 820 ms to 95 ms (same test, data and laptop, 50 requests a second for 2 minutes)
  Privacy: public-ok with names removed (my lead agreed to the redacted chart)
```

Look at three things in this block. Each proof says where it lives, so a stranger would know where to look. Each proof says her part. And the privacy mark names the condition, because her lead allowed the chart only without the company name. Her private marks are on the employer name Darb Parcels, the real table and column names, the print shop's order data and the club's sign-up data. She asks first before using the club's counts, because the organisers share them.

## Optional warm-up: finish two of Areej's cards

If you want practice before you write your own, spend 15 minutes on two of Areej's unfinished cards. Use only the facts in this table, and fill the seven fields of the card for each role. Write "unknown" where the table gives nothing.

| | Freelance, a small print shop | Study project, Campus Board |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | The owner's assistant copied each order into an invoice template by hand | A four-person university team built a campus events board in Node.js and PostgreSQL |
| Her part, first story | Wrote a Node.js script that makes PDF invoices and a monthly summary; 9 hours to build | Found a leaked email-service key in an early commit and revoked it |
| Her part, second story | None | Moved secrets to environment variables, added an example file, turned on push protection |
| Her part, third story | None | Added a 30-second cache to the events list and wrote a load test |
| Others' part | The owner chose the invoice fields | A teammate committed the key and agreed to the fix; a teammate wrote the endpoint |
| Numbers | Median 7 minutes per invoice (64 invoices in two weeks), then 1 minute (61 invoices) | p95 820 ms before, 95 ms after, same test; error rate under 1 percent in both runs |
| Net saving | About 3 hours a week, net of about 30 minutes a month of upkeep | Not applicable |
| Proof | A timing sheet she kept with the assistant | The test script, same laptop, same 10,000 seeded events |

A good freelance card says the saving is net of upkeep, names the timing sheet as the place, and says she did all of it while the owner chose the invoice fields. It also admits a gap: she did not measure whether customers received invoices sooner. A good study card keeps the two stories apart, the key and the cache, or uses two cards. It states that the test ran on a laptop and not in production, and that cached lists can be up to 30 seconds old.

## How to use AI on your own material

An AI tool can help you here, in one narrow way. It can restructure what you wrote. It can sort rough notes into the template, shorten a long paragraph, and ask you questions about empty fields. It may not add anything. That means no new skill, no new role, no new number and no new result. This rule, restructure and never add, is the base for every later AI draft in the course: the resume, the profile, outreach messages and comments.

Do this in four steps. First, write your rough notes yourself. Second, remove anything private that is not yours to share: employer and client names, secrets, keys, customer data and other people's details. Use roles instead, such as "my lead" or "a print shop". Third, paste the notes and ask for help with structure and questions. Fourth, read every line of the result against your memory and your evidence. Keep what you can point to. Delete the rest. A prompt you can copy:

```text
Below are my own rough notes about my work. Sort them into this template. Do not add any skill, role, number, date or result that is not in my notes. If a field is empty, leave it empty and ask me one question about it. Keep my wording where you can.
```

Then do the empty-sentence edit pass. Read each sentence in your arc and ask: what could a stranger check in this? If the answer is nothing, the sentence is empty. Cut it, or replace it with a fact from your notes. "Strong problem solver who delivers results" is empty. "Found a full-table scan in the slow query log and added an index" can be checked.

Here is one way it goes wrong. Areej pasted her notes into a tool and asked it to build her arc. The result looked tidy. It also said she "led a team of five" and had "five years of experience". Neither was in her notes. Her notes showed one of four people on a student team, and a six-month internship. A tool wants to finish a story, so it filled the gaps with things that sound likely. If she had kept those lines, a founder would have asked about the team of five, and she would have had no answer. She deleted both lines and wrote a note under part 9: "AI use: I asked the tool to sort my notes into the template. I kept the structure and my own wording. I removed two lines it added that were not in my notes."

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not paste what is not yours">
Never paste your full name, phone, email, address, ID numbers, passwords or keys into a tool. Never paste a client or employer name, private company data, or another person's details, including what your best-self replies say about them. Use roles instead.
</Callout>

## If you have no work yet, or no laptop

Every learner has at least one study or volunteer role. A school or university project, helping in a family business, a club role and a class group all count. Start with the one you remember best. Write the situation, then your steps, then what others did. Small numbers are fine. A count of mistakes before and after is a result.

If you would rather not describe a role at all, you may complete Areej's arc in full from the facts in chapters 2.2 to 2.4 and this chapter. Say clearly that you did. Your mentor will follow up at the gate with one question, so choose the pillar you understand best.

You do not need a laptop. The template works as plain text on a phone, in a notes app, or on paper that you photograph. Write one part at a time, in short sittings. If typing is slow, say the answer aloud into a voice note, then type it later. The document only has to be something you can open again.

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is writing in resume voice. "Responsible for backend performance" belongs nowhere in a role card. Write what happened: what was slow, what you did, what changed. The second is making every pillar a tool. "Node.js" is not a pillar. A pillar is a habit or strength that two pieces of work prove. The third is "we" everywhere. If you cannot say which steps were yours, ask someone who was there, or write "unknown" and fix it before module 3.

The fourth mistake is a number with no place. If you cannot say where it lives, mark it as a gap. The fifth is forgetting privacy marks, or marking everything "public-ok" to finish faster. When in doubt, choose "ask first". The sixth is letting a tool fill gaps. If a field is empty, it stays empty, and you ask a question or go and measure.

## Acceptance criteria

Your document is ready when:

- [ ] All nine parts are present. Part 5 may say "skipped".
- [ ] Each pillar has two proofs, each with where it lives, and one number or an honest gap.
- [ ] Every role card separates "I did" from "the team did".
- [ ] Every item has a privacy mark.
- [ ] If you used AI, the "AI use" line is there, and every fact is one you can point to.

## Where to keep it, and what comes next

Keep the document in private notes. It is not a public page, and it should not be in a public repository. Back it up where you can find it, because you will return to it in modules 3, 6, 7, 8 and 9. In the gate, chapter 2.7, you will take one pillar from it and show its two proofs, and you will list what stays private.

Module 3 starts from your role cards. It shows you how to find the proof for each result, check the arithmetic, and keep it in a claim ledger. Do not worry that some "where the proof lives" fields say "unknown". Module 3 is where you fill them.
