Your own memory misses things. Other people remember what you did for them. This chapter shows how to ask others, and where to put one human detail.

## Ask three people for a story

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-three-asks" />

Areej asks her lead, the shop owner and a club organiser the same question: "Tell me about a time I did something well." Each replies in different words. One says she found the slow query early. One says she explained the script. One says she taught patiently. Asking is a way to find your material. It is not a test.

<GoDeeper title="How to ask, and who to ask">

Ask three people who have worked with you: a former lead, a client, a teacher or a teammate. Use the same question for each: "Tell me about a time I did something well." Then look for what repeats. Researchers call this a reflected best-self exercise. The idea comes from a theory paper from 2005. It is a way to find your material, not a test, and it does not prove that you will be hired.

Here is a short message you can copy and change: "Hello. I am writing down my work history and I would like your help. Could you tell me about one time I did something well when we worked together? A few lines is enough. You are free to say no." The last sentence matters. It makes saying yes easier.

Asking may feel awkward. In experiments, people underestimated how often others agree to a direct request for help, in a set of experiments from 2008 by as much as 50 percent. Module 7 teaches this fully. For now, one polite message is enough.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What to do with the replies">

Read all three before you decide anything. Underline words or ideas that appear in more than one reply. Ignore compliments with no story, such as "great to work with". A reply with a story gives you material. If a reply is short, you may ask one follow-up: "What did I do, step by step?".

Keep the replies in your private career arc, with the person's role and a date. Do not quote anyone in public without asking them. Do not paste their messages into an AI tool if they contain private details about them or about their company. If you do use a tool, remove names first.

If you do not hear back from one person, that is normal. Two replies are enough to start. Add the third when it arrives, or plan to send the asks by a date, as chapter 2.5 allows.

</GoDeeper>

## What repeats becomes a pillar

<MermaidDiagram title="Looking for what repeats" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["Three replies from three settings"] --> Q{"Does the same idea appear in more than one?"}
  Q -->|"Yes"| T["A theme: finds a problem, fixes it, explains it"]
  Q -->|"No"| F["Ask two more people, or start from the reply you can prove"]
  T --> P["Check the theme against a pillar's numbers"]
`} legend="A theme is an idea that appears in more than one reply. A theme still needs the numbers of a pillar behind it." />

Three different people, three different settings, one theme: Areej finds a problem, fixes it and explains it. No single reply is the answer. The repeated meaning across all three is. That theme fits her first pillar and her one-line difference: she finds the slow thing, fixes it and writes it down.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's replies and what they confirm">

Her internship lead said: "You found the slow query before anyone asked." The shop owner said: "You explained the script so my assistant could use it." A club organiser said: "You trained the new organisers patiently." The words are different. The pattern is the same. She sees a problem, fixes it and then makes sure the next person can use the fix.

Compare Ruqaya in chapter 2.1. Her three replies all said she turned messy research into one clear page. Her theme was about clarity for a reader. Areej's is about finding, fixing and explaining. Your theme will not be the same as either.

The theme does not replace the proof. Her first pillar still needs the report and the cache with their numbers. The replies tell her that the pillar is real to other people. They do not replace the numbers.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="If nothing seems to repeat">

Sometimes three replies seem to share nothing. Check again with simpler words. Write each reply as "I did X, so that Y". Look at the Y. People often describe different actions that all helped someone in the same way.

If there is still no match, ask two more people. Or choose the reply that has a story you can prove with a number or a document, and begin with that one. A pillar built from one reply and two proofs is still a pillar. The asks help you find material. They do not decide which pillars you may have.

</GoDeeper>

## One true, human detail

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-football-notebook" />

Areej has kept a notebook of football match statistics since she was 12. A reader asks what it taught her. Her answer is short: an average can hide the one bad match. The answer leads straight back to her work. A true detail invites a question. It is optional, and nobody is graded on it.

<GoDeeper title="Choose one true detail">

This is a course suggestion, not research. One true detail, such as a sport, films or cartoons, gives a reader something to remember and ask about. A kit full of proof can feel cold. A single human line helps a reader see a person.

Three rules. It must be true. Do not invent a hobby to look interesting, because a reader may ask about it, and you would need to answer. It must be small: one line, not a paragraph. And it must connect, if it can, to your work. Areej's notebook taught her that an average can hide one very bad match. In her work, an average response time can hide the slow requests that real users feel. That link is why the detail fits.

You may skip this part. Nothing in the course grades it. If you have no detail you want to share, write "skipped" in your arc.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="How often to use it">

Areej uses the notebook line once on each page, never as a slogan. It can sit in her LinkedIn About section, and once in the About page of her website. She does not repeat it in her resume, her case study or her messages. A detail used once on a page is a small surprise. The same detail used in five places becomes a slogan.

Choose one place for yours. If you are not sure, write the place in your arc as "About section, to decide". In module 7 you will write that section, and you will see how much room it has.

</GoDeeper>

## Where the detail helps or distracts

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-where-detail-helps" />

Sort eight places where Areej could mention the notebook into helps, neutral or distracts. Tap the bucket or drag the card onto it. The detail is the same in every card. Only the reader's purpose changes, and that decides whether it helps. Read the reason after each wrong try.

<GoDeeper title="A rule of thumb for any detail">

These are course judgments, not research. Where the reader is looking for the person, a true detail helps. An About section is one. A friendly message to someone who already knows you is another. A short example in a comment can also help, if it is on topic and makes your point.

Where the reader is looking for proof, the detail gets in the way. A resume bullet has room for a result. A two-line summary is the most valuable space on the page. A case study is for the problem and the numbers. A first message to a stranger should state your ask. In each of these, a hobby uses room that proof needs.

"Neutral" is a real answer. A photo of a notebook in a featured section does no harm. It adds no proof either. If a place is neutral, you can leave the detail out, and nothing is lost.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Test your own detail">

Take your detail and ask four questions. Is it true? Is it one line? Does it help the reader in this place, or does it use space that proof needs? Could I answer a question about it in one sentence? If any answer is no, leave it out of that place.

You will place your detail in your arc as part 5 of the template. Write where it may appear, for example "About section only". Later modules read that line, so you do not need to decide again.

</GoDeeper>

## Labels a founder cannot check

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-labels-fade" />

A founder tries to check three things. A personality-type badge cannot be checked. Neither can an ikigai circle, a chart of four overlapping questions about work and purpose. A proof card with a real result can. The badge and the circle give a reader nothing to look up. Keep what a founder can check.

<GoDeeper title="Why we skip these labels">

Skip personality-type labels in your kit. We have not checked evidence for them, and a founder cannot verify them. We also have not checked evidence that an ikigai diagram finds your career, so we do not teach it as a method.

There is a fair point on the other side. Exercises that help people name and use their strengths have been studied. A review of 14 studies found small to moderate effects on how happy and satisfied people felt (published 2019). Those results are about wellbeing. They are not about hiring. Naming strengths may help you feel more engaged in your work. It says nothing about how an employer reads a strengths line.

So use the exercise for yourself, if it helps. Do not put the label in your kit.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What to keep in the kit instead">

Keep a proof card: a result, where it lives, and your part. It is the opposite of a label. A label tells a reader what kind of person you say you are. A proof card shows what you did, and lets the reader decide.

If a posting asks for a personality test, do it, because that is their rule. If a friend gives you a result, you may enjoy it. You are never asked to share one in this course. And nothing here asks you to share anything personal or painful. Use only what you choose to use.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

The reflected best-self idea comes from a theory paper by Roberts, Dutton and colleagues (2005). It proposes how people learn who they are at their best, partly from other people's feedback. The advice to ask three people, and to look for what repeats, is the course's own use of that idea. The point about direct requests comes from experiments by Flynn and Lake (2008). The wellbeing review of strengths exercises is by Schutte and Malouff (2019). The advice on one human detail and where it fits is a course suggestion, not research.

</GoDeeper>
