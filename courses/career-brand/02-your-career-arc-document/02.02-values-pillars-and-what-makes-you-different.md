Ruqaya's three colleagues gave her a strength to name. This chapter shows how to name yours so a stranger can check it. Areej is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative. By the end you will see how she turned three strengths into pillars.

## A pillar is a strength with proof

<MermaidDiagram title="One pillar and what it stands on" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["Pillar: I make slow things fast, and I measure it"] --> A["Proof 1: report query, p95 4.6 s to 0.41 s"]
  P --> B["Proof 2: events endpoint cache, p95 820 ms to 95 ms"]
  A --> N["A number a stranger can check"]
  B --> N
`} legend="The pillar at the top stands on two proofs. Both lead to numbers a stranger can check. A strength with no proof under it would have nothing to stand on." />

A pillar is a strength you can prove. Areej's first one is "I make slow things fast, and I measure it." It stands on two proofs: a slow report at her internship and a slow events list in a university project. Each proof has a number a stranger can check. Here p95 is the time that 95 out of every 100 requests finish within.

<GoDeeper title="Why a pillar needs a proof and a number">

This is the course's own method. It is not a researched rule. Researchers who reviewed the work on personal branding found more than 100 papers and many different definitions, so no single tested meaning of "personal brand" or "pillar" exists. We use one simple rule that you can apply today: a strength you cannot show is only a word. For each strength, write the proof and the number.

The proof is the piece of work. The number is the result a stranger could check. In your career arc, each pillar gets four things: the strength in one line, proof 1 and where it lives, proof 2 and where it lives, and one number. If you have no number for a proof, write an honest gap: "not measured yet, and here is how I would". That counts.

Why two proofs and not one? One proof can be luck, or one good week. Two proofs from different places show a habit. A reader who finds the same habit twice believes it more.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Areej's first pillar, step by step">

Proof 1 is her internship at a parcel delivery company. Operations staff opened a daily orders report many times a day, and it was slow. She found the query in the database's slow query log. She saw that the database read the whole table, about 2.4 million rows, to answer it. She proposed an index. An index works like the index of a book: the database finds the rows without reading every page. Her lead reviewed the change and scheduled it. On the team dashboard, the p95 of the report fell from 4.6 s to 0.41 s. Each figure covers 14 days.

Proof 2 is Campus Board, a four-person university project. The events list was slow when many people used it. She added a cache with a 30-second expiry and wrote a load test. A cache is a copy of an answer that is kept for a short time. The test was the same before and after: 50 requests a second for 2 minutes, the same data and the same laptop. The p95 fell from 820 ms to 95 ms.

Both numbers have a place where a stranger could look: a dashboard and a test script. The cache has a cost. A cached list can be up to 30 seconds old, and she says so. Module 3 shows you how to build numbers like these.

</GoDeeper>

## Two or three pillars, not ten

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-ten-or-three" />

A reader is shown ten thin pillars and remembers almost none. Then the reader is shown three solid pillars and remembers them. The three are not bigger claims. Each one stands on a proof. Fewer pillars are easier for a reader to remember and for you to prove. Three is a guide, not a law.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's three pillars">

Areej has three pillars, and each has two proofs. Pillar 1: "I make slow things fast, and I measure it." Its proofs are the report query and the events cache. Pillar 2: "I automate boring work for small teams." Its proofs are an invoice script for a small print shop and a sign-up form for a youth coding club. The script saved about 3 hours a week net of upkeep. The form removed 57 duplicate sign-ups, and double bookings fell from 9 to 1. She adds a caution: the club also cut its sessions from three a week to two. Pillar 3: "I am careful with data and secrets." Its proofs are replacing a leaked key and turning on push protection. The second proof is asking permission before she used a company chart in public.

Notice what she left out. She also knows React, Express and Git. She has not made them pillars, because she has no two proofs for each that are about the tool itself. Tools appear inside the stories.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What to do if you have more than three strengths">

This is a course recommendation, not research. Fewer pillars are easier for a reader to hold in mind and for you to prove. Ten strengths with one weak proof each leave a reader with nothing to remember. Three strengths with two proofs each leave a reader with a clear picture.

List every strength you can think of. Cross out any that have no proof yet. From what remains, keep the two or three that have the strongest two proofs. Move the others to your gaps list, which you meet in chapter 2.5. A gap is not a failure. It is a note about what to build or measure next.

If you have only one strong pillar so far, that is fine. Write one pillar well. A second can come after your next project.

</GoDeeper>

## Claims without proof turn grey

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m2-claims-under-pillars" />

Sort eight of Areej's claims. Tap the bucket for each one, or drag the card onto it. A claim holds a pillar when a proof is attached. Words about her, such as "great communicator", stay grey until an example is attached. Read the reason after each wrong try.

<GoDeeper title="Grey does not mean false">

A grey claim is not a lie. "Great communicator" may be true. It is grey because a reader has nothing to look at. The fix is to attach an example. For Areej, one example is already in her notes: she explained the new index to her lead in writing. With that example, "communicator" can sit inside a pillar as a proof. Without it, the word should wait or go.

Look at the four claims that need a proof first. "Expert in cloud" has nothing behind it in her notes. "Passionate about performance" is a feeling. "Team player" does not say which team or what she did. Each could become a proof if she had a real story for it. If she has no story, the claim goes on her gaps list, and she does not use the word.

The four claims that hold have something a stranger can look at: a number with a window, a team dashboard, a revoked key, a test script.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="A habit for your own claims">

Take every strength you wrote and ask three plain questions. What did I do? Where could a stranger see it? What changed, in a number if I have one? If you can answer all three, the claim holds. If you can answer only the first, you have a story but no proof yet, and module 3 will help you find it. If you can answer none, the claim is only a word.

Do not add a number to a grey claim to make it hold. A number you cannot source breaks the pillar faster than no number. The honest line "not measured yet" always holds, because a reader can ask about it and you can answer.

</GoDeeper>

## Values show in what you did

<MermaidDiagram title="Three values and the action behind each" chart={`flowchart TD
  V1["Honest numbers"] --> A1["Wrote down the 20 May release that week"]
  V2["Finishing what she starts"] --> A2["Revoked the leaked key, then turned on push protection"]
  V3["Teaching what she learns"] --> A3["Trained two new club organisers on the form"]
`} legend="Each value on the left connects to one thing Areej did, on the right. A reader can ask about the action. A reader cannot ask about the word." />

A value is a word until an action stands behind it. Each of Areej's three values connects to one thing she did. A reader cannot test the word "honest". A reader can ask about the 20 May release she wrote down next to her report numbers. Choose two or three values that you can join to an action, and drop the rest.

<GoDeeper title="The action behind each value">

Honest numbers. A front-end release went out on 20 May, inside her 14 days after the fix. It did not touch the report, but a reader would ask. So she wrote the release in her notes beside the numbers, with the traffic counts for both windows. Her lead's dashboard showed 6,300 requests before and 6,100 after, about 3 percent fewer, so traffic did not explain the change.

Finishing what she starts. She found an email-service key, a secret code that lets a program send email, that a teammate had committed to the Campus Board code. Committed means saved into the project history. Deleting the file would not remove the key from that history. So she revoked the key, which makes it stop working, and made a new one. She moved secrets into environment variables, which are settings kept outside the code. She added an example file with placeholder values and turned on push protection, a setting that blocks a push containing a known kind of secret. She did not stop at the first step.

Teaching what she learns. At the youth coding club she trained two new organisers to use the sign-up form.

These are course examples of a method, not research. Pick your values, then look for one action each. If you cannot find an action, the value is not yet yours to claim.

</GoDeeper>

## What makes you different, in one line

<MermaidDiagram title="How the line is built" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["Role: full-stack developer"] --> L["A full-stack developer who measures before and after, and writes it down."]
  H["Habit: measures before and after"] --> L
  S["Proof style: writes it down"] --> L
`} legend="Three parts feed one line: a role, a habit and a way of proving things. The line at the bottom is the one Areej uses." />

Your line has three parts: a role, a habit that runs through your pillars, and a way of proving things. Areej's line joins them: "A full-stack developer who measures before and after, and writes it down." A reader can test this line by opening her work. It has no slogan and no superlative.

<GoDeeper title="Why no slogan, and how to test your line">

Some people think a personal brand is a logo and a slogan. The research does not say that. It is about reputation and identity, and it is not settled. For your kit, a slogan gives a reader nothing to check. A line that names a habit does.

Test your line with one question: could a reader check this by opening my work? "A world-class developer who always delivers" fails, because "world-class" and "always" cannot be tested. "A mobile developer who times every screen before and after, and keeps the timings in a sheet" passes, because the sheet exists or it does not. Words like best, expert and world-class are claims about you. Leave them out.

Build your own line from three blanks: "A [role] who [habit], and [how I show it]." The habit is what repeats across your pillars. In chapter 2.4 you will see where to find it: in what other people say about you.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

The finding that personal branding research has many definitions comes from a systematic review by Gorbatov, Khapova and Lysova (2018). The rule that a pillar needs a proof and a number is this course's own method. The idea of a value that is shown by an action is also course method. Areej's cases are made up for this course.

</GoDeeper>
