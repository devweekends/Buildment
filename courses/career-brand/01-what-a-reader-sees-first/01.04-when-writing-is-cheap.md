Anyone can now make a smooth paragraph in a minute. This chapter is about what that does to how readers judge writing, and what only you can still write.

## Fixing mistakes helped in one study

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-errors-cleaned" />

A resume with errors goes to an error fixer and comes back clean. In one large study, jobseekers who got this kind of help were hired a little more often. The tool corrected mistakes. It wrote no new claims. Clean writing helps. Invented writing is a different matter. So fix your mistakes, and keep every fact your own.

<GoDeeper title="What the study found">

The study was a field experiment with nearly half a million jobseekers on one online platform. The jobseekers who got help with common errors in their resumes were hired about 8 percent more often than the comparison group. For example, if the comparison group made 100 hires, the helped group made about 108. Employers were no less satisfied with the hires.

Three limits matter. It was one platform. The tool suggested fixes to common errors and did not write new text. Two of the authors have ties to the platform. Read it this way: clear writing without errors has been linked to more hires, and the help in that study corrected mistakes. It did not invent claims.

What can you do with this? Run a spell check, and ask a friend to read your resume for errors. If you use an AI tool, ask it to fix mistakes only, and compare the before and after yourself. Check that it changed no number, name or date. Course 13, "Learn and Build with AI", covers how to say when AI helped.

</GoDeeper>

## When everyone writes smoothly, smooth proves less

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-smooth-letters" />

A reader meets a stack of smooth, polished letters that all sound alike. The reader looks elsewhere and checks the one line that holds a real result. When anyone can write a smooth letter in a minute, a smooth letter proves less. What a reader can check proves more. Put that line where the reader looks first.

<GoDeeper title="What two papers found, in direction">

Two economics papers studied the same kind of change. An online platform launched an AI tool that helps write cover letters. Before the tool, a letter that matched the job well was linked to more callbacks. After the tool, that link became weaker, and employers leaned more on other signals, such as work history. A callback is a reply from an employer that asks for an interview. The second paper found that the extra pay employers once gave for customised applications disappeared.

Both papers are preprints, which are early versions that other researchers have not yet reviewed. We read only their summaries, and both used data from one freelancing platform. So we teach the direction and not the numbers. When smooth writing becomes cheap, readers put more weight on things they can check. That means what you built, what the numbers were, and who vouches for it.

A smooth letter is now the minimum. It is not the proof. This does not mean you should write badly. Clear writing still helps, as the first picture showed. It means a clear letter alone will not carry an application. Put the checkable line where the reader will meet it first, as chapter 1.2 showed.

</GoDeeper>

## People cannot reliably spot AI text

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-guess-the-writer" />

A reader guesses whether two profiles were written by a person or by AI. One guess is right and one is wrong. In experiments, people could not reliably tell the two apart. So the useful question is not "will they catch me?". It is "does this say anything only I could say?". The next pictures show why, and how to write it.

<GoDeeper title="What the experiments found">

Researchers ran six experiments with 4,600 people. They asked people to tell AI-written self-descriptions from human ones. The texts were profiles for work, for hosting guests and for dating. People could not do it reliably. They used cues such as first-person pronouns and contractions. Those cues are unreliable, and a writer can change them.

A related experiment found that readers trusted a profile less when it was labelled as AI-written. But this happened only when they saw a mix of human and AI profiles. Surveys by companies that sell resume services give very different shares of hiring managers who dislike AI use. The numbers conflict, and those companies have something to sell. We do not use them.

What does this mean for you? Do not spend effort trying not to be caught. Spend it on making every line something only you could write: a real event, a real number with its source, and your part. A reader may also ask you about it aloud. Then a smooth text does not help, and the facts do.

</GoDeeper>

## Detectors flag second-language English

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-detector-flag" />

A detector scans a careful essay written in a second language and stamps it "AI?". It passes another essay. The tool is wrong. The writer is not. If a screen says your plain English sounds like AI, that is a known weakness of these tools. It is not a verdict on you.

<GoDeeper title="What the study found, and what to do">

Researchers tested several widely used AI detectors. The detectors wrongly labelled more than half of the essays by non-native English speakers as AI-generated. They judged essays by US eighth-grade students correctly. The authors warn against using such detectors in settings where people are judged. We say "more than half" and not an exact figure, because we read only the summary of the paper.

Detectors differ and change, and nobody can promise how a screen will score you. What can you do if a screen or a person says your text looks like AI? Offer your working document, your drafts and the evidence behind each claim. A dashboard export, a commit history or a ledger row shows who did the work. Say it calmly: "I wrote this. I can show my drafts and where each number comes from."

Do not make your English worse to pass a detector. Adding spelling mistakes fixes the wrong thing. Plain, careful English is good writing.

</GoDeeper>

## Only you can write this

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-only-you-can-write" />

This is Areej's old opening, written by an AI tool. Tap each phrase a reader cannot check. Each one says why and offers a fix only she could write. Watch the counter "Facts a reader can check" rise from 0 to 3.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's opening, before and after">

Here is the opening that Areej's AI tool wrote. It reads: "Highly motivated and passionate full-stack developer seeking to leverage cutting-edge technologies in a dynamic environment." Ask it three questions. Which technologies? What did you do with them? What changed? The text cannot answer. Every phrase in it could sit on any developer's resume.

Here is the rewrite from the picture above. It reads: "Final-year software engineering student and full-stack developer who cut a daily report's p95 from 4.6 s to 0.41 s (14 days before and after). I measure before and after, and write it down." A p95 is the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within. A lower p95 means the slow requests got faster. Module 3 teaches it in full.

Now a reader can ask "Show me one time", and Areej has an answer. Only the second text can be followed by a question that has an answer. Notice that the rewrite adds no new claim. Every fact came from her own work, and she can name the source of each one.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Patterns that make text empty">

Some patterns make a sentence empty. A big claim has no number. An "expert" or a "study" is never named. A phrase ending in "-ing" adds a vague extra meaning. "Not just X, but also Y" pads a sentence. Lists of three are used by habit. Adjectives such as "dynamic" or "cutting-edge" promote and do not describe. These patterns are for orientation only. They come from a guide written by volunteers. The guide also warns that lists of AI-sounding words date quickly with each new model, and that one word proves nothing.

So the course gives you no list of banned words. A sentence with the word "pivotal" in it can be honest, and a sentence without it can be empty. Ask the same question every time: can a reader ask about this phrase and get a real answer? If not, replace the phrase with a fact only you can back up. Chapter 6.4 teaches the full edit pass.

AI may help you edit and restructure what you wrote. Numbers, ownership and judgment must be yours. If an AI tool adds a number that you did not give it, delete it.

</GoDeeper>
