```quiz
type: mcq
question: "In this made-up case, Nasser is a data analyst with a two-page resume. It opens with an objective paragraph, then a skills list of 24 tools, then his experience. On page 2 is a dashboard project that cut his team's weekly reporting time, and he kept a timing sheet. One labelled link to the project write-up sits at the bottom. Which arrangement puts his strongest checkable proof on the first phone screen?"
options:
  - "Keep the objective paragraph first, then the skills list and experience, and leave the dashboard project and its link down on page 2"
  - "Open with the skills list cut to his ten best tools, then experience, and leave the dashboard project and its link on page 2"
  - "Put his name, one labelled link to the write-up and a result line first, then experience, with skills cut to one line at the end"
  - "Cut the resume to one line under his name, because readers give a resume only six seconds, and drop the dashboard link"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "An objective paragraph at the top takes the first screen and tells the reader about Nasser, not about what he built. The proof stays on page 2, where a quick reader may never scroll."
  - "Ten tools are still a list of names. A trimmed skills block still fills the first screen, and the proof stays below it."
  - "Right. His name, a link that says what the reader will see and a result line fill the first screen. Experience follows, and the skills shrink to one line at the end."
  - "Nobody has a reliable number for how long a reader looks, so six seconds is not a reason. Dropping the link also removes the one thing a reader could check."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Rola, a mobile developer, drafts four lines for her profile. A founder has five minutes and will not message her first. Which line can the founder check best?"
options:
  - "Cut weekly crashes from 640 to 210 (store console, 4 weeks before and after)"
  - "Improved app speed by 40 percent, and users noticed the change straight after launch"
  - "We shipped a major app release with a great team that cared a lot about quality"
  - "Expert in Kotlin, Swift and Flutter, with six years of experience on mobile apps"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. It gives a before and an after, a window and a named source, so the founder knows what to look at and what to ask to see."
  - "This is a percentage with no starting point, no window and no source. The founder cannot tell 40 percent of what, or how it was measured."
  - "'We' hides who did the work, and 'a great team' is an opinion. There is nothing in the line to look up or test."
  - "A list of tools with years looks like proof, but it only says what she has touched. It names no project or result that a reader can check."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Hind, a QA engineer, hears a classmate say: 'Recruiters give a resume six seconds, so cut yours to one line.' Which reply is honest and still useful?"
options:
  - "Agree with her: the six second figure is proven, so a resume should shrink to one line to get through the first look"
  - "Partly agree: the better figure is 7.4 seconds, so Hind can keep a few more lines but should still cut most of the resume"
  - "Disagree with her: a reader goes through every line of a resume, so the order of the blocks makes no difference at all"
  - "Nobody has a reliable number, but employers say they receive more applications than they can read, so put the strongest proof first"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "The six second figure comes from a company study that did not say how many recruiters took part or which jobs. It is not a proven fact, and cutting to one line would drop the proof."
  - "The 7.4 second figure has the same problem as six seconds: a company study with an unknown sample, about the choice to keep reading. Swapping one unproven number for another fixes nothing."
  - "This goes too far the other way. Employers say they cannot read every application closely, so what comes first on the page still matters."
  - "Right. It drops the false number and keeps the useful habit: the strongest proof and one labelled link come first, and what does not help this reader goes."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Sufian, a backend developer, has a one-page resume. He has one shipped project with a measured result and three course certificates. Where should each one go?"
options:
  - "The three certificates as a block at the top, because they show he finished training, and the project below them"
  - "The project near the top with its result and link, and the three certificates as one small line near the end"
  - "Leave out the project because it is only personal work, and give the three certificates all of the space"
  - "The project and the certificates side by side with equal space, so that the reader can judge both of them"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "A block of certificates at the top makes the weakest evidence the headline. We found no study that shows what a certificate does for a software job."
  - "Right. The project has a result and a link a reader can check, so it leads. The certificates are one small line near the end."
  - "A shipped project with a measured result is the stronger evidence. Calling it personal work and dropping it removes the one thing a reader can check."
  - "Equal space avoids the choice. A reader with little time keeps what comes first, so the project should lead and the certificates should be small."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Maysaa, a frontend developer who writes English as a second language, asks an AI assistant how to avoid AI detectors. It answers: 'Remove words like realm and pivotal, and add a few spelling mistakes.' What is wrong with this advice?"
options:
  - "Nothing, because detectors score word choice, and removing those words lowers the chance of a flag on a text"
  - "Only the spelling mistakes, because swapping the words on the list is sound advice for every kind of writer"
  - "Word lists date, detectors misjudge second-language writing, and specifics only she can write are the fix"
  - "It is wrong because detectors are accurate, so no edit of any kind could change what they report about the text"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "One word proves nothing, and lists of AI-sounding words date as models change. Detectors also often misjudge second-language English, so word swaps do not make a text safe."
  - "The word swap is no better than the spelling mistakes. A word list dates quickly, and one word is weak evidence about who wrote a text."
  - "Right. The advice fixes the wrong thing. Specifics only she could write, with the facts to back them, are what make a text hers."
  - "Detectors are not that accurate: several widely used ones wrongly flagged more than half of essays by non-native English speakers. The better fix is adding specifics only she could write."
```
