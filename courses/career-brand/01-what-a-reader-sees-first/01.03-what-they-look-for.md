A founder with a small team reads your kit to answer two questions. Can this person do real work? Can I believe what they say? This chapter covers what employers who publish their hiring say.

## What hiring teams say they want

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-handbook-cards" />

Two companies publish how they hire. One looks for people who are proactive, direct and clear in writing and speech. It also wants awareness of the impact of your work, and proof that you improved something in steps. The other looks for good code, sound thinking and realistic problems. It warns that extra features nobody asked for can hurt. Both want to see your thinking.

<GoDeeper title="Two lists, one thing in common">

Each list is one company describing its own process. Neither is a tested predictor of who will do the job well. Treat them as clues about what readers hope to see, not as rules. The pattern is still consistent. Hiring teams that publish how they hire describe wanting to see your thinking: what you changed, how you know, and what you would do next.

You can turn each wish into evidence you already have. "Proactive" means you noticed a problem before anyone asked. Areej found a slow query in her internship team's slow query log without being asked. "Aware of the impact" means you know who felt the problem and what changed for them. Operations staff opened her report many times a day. "Evidence of iteration" means you can show a first version, and what you changed after you looked at the result. "Sound thought process" means you can say why you chose one way over another, and what you would try next.

Notice what the lists leave out. They do not ask for a long list of tools or for the most advanced solution. One of them says that simple solutions that met the requirements scored well. If your project has a feature nobody needed, say so, and say why you stopped.

</GoDeeper>

## Shipped work over a list of courses

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-badges-or-project" />

A reader sees two profiles. One is headed by eight course badges. The other is headed by a shipped project with a result. The reader opens the project. Founders who write about hiring say they would rather see something you built than a list of courses. That is their stated practice, not a law.

<GoDeeper title="What shipped means, and what courses are for">

"Shipped" means that real people can open or use the work, even if only a few people do. A project on your own laptop that nobody can open is not shipped. A small page that one real person uses every week is. Modules 4 and 5 show how to plan and show such work.

One software company that hires programmers says it prefers to see real work over credentials or puzzles. An essay on hiring a first engineer treats GitHub as a place to find people to contact, not as a way to judge them. Both are the stated views of their writers. They are not measurements.

None of this says courses are useless. A course is how you learn, and it may help you finish a project. The question is what goes at the top of your resume. Some employers and some roles also require a particular degree or licence, and the posting will say so. Read each posting. This course cannot change a rule that an employer has set. It helps when a reader reaches your work.

</GoDeeper>

## Checkable, or only a claim?

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-checkable-or-claim" />

Sort eight profile lines. A line is checkable when a reader can look something up or test it without asking you. "We" and adjectives are only claims, because the reader must trust you. Tap the bucket for each line, or drag the card onto it. Read the reason after each wrong try.

<GoDeeper title="Three levels of checkable">

Checkable comes in degrees. The strongest is something a stranger can open and try, such as a live demo with a guest login. The next is a result with a named source and a time window, such as "cut report time from 12 to 3 minutes (team dashboard)". A reader may not be able to open that dashboard. But they know what to ask for, and you can show it. The weakest are adjectives and tool lists. "Passionate team player" cannot be looked up or tested.

Checkable does not mean true. A line can be checkable and false. It means a reader could find out. That is why you must be able to back up every checkable line when you are asked. It is also why module 3 teaches you to keep the proof for each number.

Ownership is part of this. "We improved performance" hides who did what. A reader who wants to know your part has nothing to ask about. "I found the cause, and my lead scheduled the release" can be asked about, and your lead can confirm it. The team may have done most of the work. Say so, and say your part.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Bashar's lines, sorted">

Here are two lines from Bashar's profile. "Responsible for backend services" is only a claim. It names a duty, not a change, so a reader has nothing to look up. "Moved the nightly import into a queue; it stopped timing out (job logs)" is checkable. A reader can ask to see the job logs from before and after.

The report-time line in the sorter is an example for this lesson. It is not Areej's, and it is not a real person's result. In module 3 you learn to turn a checkable line into a claim card. A claim card holds the metric, the before, the after, the window, the source, your share and what else changed.

Do not over-correct. A plain job title, a school name and a date are fine. Not every line needs a number. Delete lines that only say you are good, and keep lines that say what you did.

</GoDeeper>

## Where a certificate fits

<MermaidDiagram title="Two orders, two first impressions" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["A reader opens a one-page resume"] --> B["Path 1: certificates first"]
  A --> C["Path 2: shipped project first"]
  B --> D["Sees training, and no result to check"]
  C --> E["Sees a result and a link to open"]
  D --> F["Move certificates to one small line at the end"]
  E --> G["Keep it, and add certificates at the end"]
`} legend="Two paths for the same resume. Path 2 gives a quick reader something to check." />

On a one-page resume, shipped work is the headline. A certificate is one small line near the end. We could not find a study that shows what a certificate does for a software job. So use it as a small line, and let shipped work be the headline.

<GoDeeper title="What we found, and what we did not">

We searched for field experiments on certificates and software jobs. A field experiment is a study where researchers change one thing in real life and watch what happens. We found no published result. The nearest study is about degrees, not certificates. Researchers sent matching applications to employers. A callback is a reply that asks for an interview. A business degree from a for-profit online school got about 22 percent fewer callbacks than the same kind of degree from a non-selective public school. For every 100 callbacks the public degree got, the online degree got about 78. Where a licence was required, the gap was smaller. That suggests the reputation of the issuer mattered to those employers. It does not tell us what a cloud or course certificate does for a software job.

So do not promise yourself that a certificate will help. Write each certificate as one line: its name, the issuer and the year. If a certificate came with a project, link the project instead, because the project is the proof. If a posting asks for a certificate by name, include it.

Here is an order that works on one page. Your name and links come first. Then come your best project with its result, your experience and your education. The certificates come last, as one small line.

</GoDeeper>

## Real tailoring shows you looked

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-generic-or-tailored" />

A reader opens a generic application and skims it. Then the reader opens one that says the writer tried the product and names one change they would make. The reader reads on. One company says its review checks for this. Tailoring shows you looked.

<GoDeeper title="What real tailoring is">

One company that publishes its hiring process says its application review checks whether the candidate tailored the application. It also checks whether the candidate has tried the product or read the company's public handbook. That is one company's practice. We do not know how many others do the same.

Real tailoring takes time, so do it for fewer places. Spend 20 minutes. Open the product or read the public handbook, and note one thing you saw. Then write it plainly, and only if it is true: "I tried the free plan for an hour. The export button was hard to find on my phone. I would move it to the top." A line like this cannot be pasted into every application, because it describes what you did.

Pasting the words of the posting into your resume carries nothing, because the reader sees their own words. Real tailoring changes which of your evidence comes first. Module 6 shows how. You move the most relevant proof up and leave the posting's words alone.

</GoDeeper>

## A good kit cannot remove every bias

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-kit-bias-note" />

Two kits carry the same proof, and a reader replies to only one. A stronger kit helps, but not equally for everyone. This course does not promise that a better kit removes bias against a name, a place or a passport.

<GoDeeper title="What an old study found, and what we do not know">

In a well-known study from 2004, researchers sent made-up resumes to job ads in two US cities. Resumes with names that sound white got about 50 percent more callbacks than the same resumes with names that sound African-American. For every 100 callbacks one group got, the other got about 67. A stronger resume helped the first group much more than it helped the second.

This study is old, and it is about US newspaper ads. It tells us nothing exact about remote hiring today. We found no study that measures what happens to applicants from Palestine. So we make only a narrow claim: resume quality does move callbacks, and a kit cannot fix every kind of bias.

If you do not hear back, that does not prove your work is weak. It also does not prove that it is strong. This course helps with the part you control: what a reader finds when they do look. Your mentor can help you choose where to apply and which roles to try first.

</GoDeeper>

The handbook cards come from two companies' public writing about their own hiring (read 2026-10). One is a handbook page on a talent interview. The other is an article on a take-home test. The founders' views on courses come from the same article and from an essay on hiring a first engineer.
