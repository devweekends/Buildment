## Your task: your ten-second test and your baseline

This task records where your kit stands today. In module 9 you compare your finished kit with this baseline, and you see how far you came. So an honest start is worth more than a good-looking one. A weak result is normal. Nobody is graded on the quality of the resume you have now.

First, report your ten-second test. Say what the viewer remembered, with at least two things, and say the time limit you used. Say what your best proof is, and where it sits: on the first screen, later in the file, or missing. Then choose one change you will make because of what the test showed. Write about 120 words. Use your own resume, or Areej's old resume if you have none.

Second, record your baseline. The best way is the AI drill below. It asks about the lines on your resume, and it ends with flags. Copy the flags into your learning log. If you cannot use the drill, answer two questions for three lines of your resume, in about 150 words. The questions are "How was that measured?" and "What was your part?". "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are full answers when they are true. Never invent a source to look prepared.

Here is a strong answer from a fictional fellow. Rashad is an Android developer. He wrote: "My cousin looked at my resume on her phone for ten seconds, using a timer. She remembered that I am a student and that I know Kotlin. Nothing else. My best proof is a fix that cut the crash reports in our study group's app. It is a bullet on page 2, so it was not on the first screen. I will cut the objective paragraph and put the crash fix and one labelled link under my name." For the baseline, he took the line "Reduced app crashes". He wrote: "I counted crash reports in the store console for 4 weeks before (31) and 4 weeks after (12). I found the bug, and a teammate reviewed the fix." For the line "Improved app speed", he wrote: "I did not measure that. It only felt faster on my phone. I would time the start screen ten times before and after."

What makes Rashad's answer strong? The test result names two things remembered, a time limit, the place of the best proof, and one change tied to the test. The baseline has a number with its source, his own part, and an honest gap. He did not defend a weak line, and he did not make up a number.

Here is a weak answer. "The test showed my resume is good. People said it looks professional. I will make it better." It names nothing that was remembered, no time limit, no best proof and no place where it sits. The change is not tied to the test, so a mentor cannot help with it. A weak baseline looks the same: "I improved speed by 90 percent, and we did it as a team." Asked "how was that measured?", the writer has no answer. The word "we" hides what the writer did. Those are the two questions a founder will ask, and practising them now is the point.

If you prefer to use Areej's old resume, here it is as a phone shows it. It has two pages. At the top is an objective paragraph that an AI tool wrote. Below it is a skills block of 22 technologies. The experience section has three bullets about her internship: "Worked on backend performance", "Improved speed by 90%", and a long line full of big words about technologies. Her freelance and volunteer work are missing. Her study project, Campus Board, is one line with no link. A GitHub link sits at the bottom of page 2.

A classmate looked at the first screen for ten seconds and remembered "a long paragraph about being passionate, then a list of tools". Areej's real work was a slow orders report that she made faster at her internship. She found the slow query, read its query plan, proposed an index and wrote the migration. Her lead reviewed the work and scheduled the release. None of this is on the old resume. The line "Improved speed by 90%" does not say of what, over which days, or how it was measured, and Areej cannot say either. Use these facts to write the ten-second result, to choose one change, and to answer the two questions for her three bullets.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer part by part. Each part is Met, Partly or Not yet. Nothing is failed because the kit is weak.

**Part 1: Test reported.**

- **Met:** You name at least two things the viewer remembered, and the time limit you used, in seconds or as a timer length.
- **Partly:** You name one thing, or you give no time limit.
- **Not yet:** There is no test result.

**Part 2: Best proof located.**

- **Met:** You name your best proof, a specific line or project, and say whether it sat on the first screen, later in the file, or is missing.
- **Partly:** You name the proof but not where it sits, or you say where without naming it.
- **Not yet:** You do neither.

**Part 3: One change.**

- **Met:** You name one specific change, what moves or what is cut, that follows from what the viewer remembered.
- **Partly:** The change is not tied to the test, such as "make it better", or you list several vague changes.
- **Not yet:** There is no change.

**Part 4: Baseline recorded.**

- **Met:** The drill is finished and its flags are visible to your mentor, or all three lines are answered with both "how was that measured?" and "what was your part?". Honest gaps count as answered.
- **Partly:** The drill is started but not finished, or one or two lines are answered, or a line has only one of the two answers.
- **Not yet:** You have neither the drill nor the written lines.

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet. Every part must be at least Partly.

The feedback piece you add at the end of the written task is not graded. It shows your mentor what you learned from the drill, and a disagreement with the feedback is welcome.

Your mentor then replies with one to three questions. They are about the change you chose, for example: "What would you cut to make room for your best proof on the first screen?". If your baseline is missing, a question asks you to add it. You answer in writing or in a voice note. Answer them too: the gate is complete after your reply, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks about three lines on your resume, starting with the one you are proudest of. It follows up with questions like "how did you measure it?" and "what was your part?". It ends with feedback: your strengths, what to do better, named flags and next steps, including one thing to measure or build next. Paste about 100 to 200 words from your resume, with roles in place of names. Or paste Areej's old bullets.

The feedback uses five flags. Each one points at your own words.

1. **Vague ownership.** It stays unclear what you personally did. Example: "We deployed it."
2. **Unsupported number.** A number or result with no source or way of measuring. Example: "It got about 90 percent faster."
3. **Pseudo-story.** It sounds like a story, but no situation, action or outcome in it can be checked. Example: "We faced many challenges, but we overcame them together."
4. **Invented reason risk.** A reason that sounds made up after the fact. Example: "I chose that database because it scales better", when you never compared two.
5. **Jargon without explanation.** A technical term used without saying what it means.

Answer honestly. "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers. The drill is practice, not a hiring decision, and its feedback can be wrong. AI tools tend to agree with you and to like long answers, so check every point against your own words. The drill gives no score. Read the level word that starts each note: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. Copy the flags into your learning log. They are your baseline, and in module 6 you run the drill again on your new resume and compare.

Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead, such as "a delivery company" or "my team lead". If you do not have a key or credit, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "Your resume, line by line"
opening: "Pick three lines from your resume and start with the one you are proudest of. What did you do, and how do you know it worked?"
focus: [resume, impact, ownership]
context: resume
maxTurns: 8
whyDepth: 3
required: false
rubric: "A strong baseline shows, for each resume line the learner picks, what they did, what changed, how it was measured and what part was their own, or an honest statement that it was not measured. This is a baseline, not a test: ask every line the same questions and do not judge the resume. Probes: after any number ask 'How did you measure it? Over what window? Where can I see it?'; if the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'; if a tool or method is named, ask 'What was the alternative?'; if the answer describes habits, ask for one specific time. Accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would measure next; never push the learner to guess or invent a number or a reason. Judge features, not length, and quote the learner's words. Begin each focus-area note with a level word: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Next steps: one thing to measure next or one small thing to build next around the learner's own needs, never a named project idea. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person."
```
