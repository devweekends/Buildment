Bashar's best work was on page 2. This chapter is about the order of your material, and why order matters when a reader has little time. Bashar and Areej are fictional fellows. By the end you will move Bashar's best work onto the first screen, and test your own resume.

## More applications than time to read

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-pile-and-day" />

One reader works through a pile of applications. The day ends and the pile is still tall. Only a few were read closely. The reader is not lazy. There is more than one day can hold. So the first look is quick, and your best proof must be easy to find.

<GoDeeper title="What employers say about volume">

Nobody has a reliable number for how long a reader looks at a resume. What employers do tell us is that they receive far more applications than they can read closely. So the first look is quick, and your best proof must be easy to find.

One software company that publishes how it hires writes that it gets too many applications to review work from all of them. It screens by cover letter and resume first. Then it gives a small work test to about 1 to 3 percent of applicants. That is about 1 to 3 people in every 100 who apply. It is one company, so treat it as an example and not as a rule for every employer.

The useful question is therefore not "how many seconds do I get?". It is "if a reader stopped after the first screen, what would they know about me?". You will answer that for your own resume at the end of this chapter.

</GoDeeper>

## The famous six seconds has no source

<MermaidDiagram title="Where the six seconds comes from" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["'Recruiters look for 6 seconds'"] --> B["Company eye-tracking studies, 2012 and 2018"]
  B --> C["2018 report: number of recruiters not given"]
  B --> D["Jobs and resume lengths not given"]
  C --> E["It measured the choice to keep reading"]
  D --> E
  E --> F["Use it as a habit: best proof first"]
`} legend="The claim at the top traces back to one company's studies. Two things its 2018 report did not say lead to what it really measured. The last box is the habit worth keeping." />

The number often quoted is six seconds. It traces back to a hiring-site company's eye-tracking studies. Its 2018 report did not say how many recruiters took part, or which jobs. It measured how fast a reader decides to keep reading, not how they judge you. Treat it as a habit, not a statistic: put your best proof first.

<GoDeeper title="Where the number comes from">

The claim has a clear starting point. A hiring-site company ran eye-tracking studies in 2012 and 2018. People repeat the results as "recruiters give a resume six seconds", or as 7.4 seconds. A recruiting-industry article looked into it. It reports that the 2018 report does not say how many recruiters were tracked, which jobs they were hiring for, or how long the resumes were. The report also reads more like advice on making a resume easy to scan than like a measurement. The figure is about the time to decide whether to keep reading. It is not the time spent judging your skills.

Here is a way to check any number you meet in career advice. Ask who measured it. Ask how many people, and for which jobs. Ask whether the company that reports it sells something. A number with no answer to these questions is a story, not a fact. Your future reader will use the same habit on your numbers: "how was that measured?".

What survives from the myth is useful. Readers decide early whether to read on, so order matters. You do not need to cut your resume to one line. You need your best proof where a quick reader will meet it.

</GoDeeper>

## Your first screen decides what they see

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-first-look" />

This is Bashar's resume as a phone shows it. The first screen holds 10 lines, and it runs out before his best work. Tap a block, then move it up or down, or drag it. Move his two proof blocks up until the first screen shows both. The proof does not change, only its place.

<GoDeeper title="Why the first screen decides">

A phone shows a small part of a page. Whatever fills that part is what a quick reader keeps, because nothing makes the phone jump to your best work. A reader who scrolls will see more. A reader who stops will not. You cannot know which one you have, so you arrange the page for the one who stops.

Two blocks usually take the first screen without earning it. The first is a long objective paragraph, because it sits at the top and runs to many lines. The second is a skills block, because it lists names and not results. In the picture, Bashar's name takes 2 lines, the objective takes 5 and the skills block takes 5. That is 12 lines, and the first screen holds 10. So the proof never appears.

The habit is simple: best proof first, one link, no search through the file. The link matters because every extra step is a place where a reader stops. A reader should never have to hunt for the thing that makes you worth reading.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Bashar's resume, reordered">

Here is the same resume after the change, step by step. First, his name stays at the top. Second, directly under it goes one line that says what he did. It reads: "Moved the nightly import into a queue; it stopped timing out." Third, beside it goes one labelled link to the code for that import. Fourth, the objective paragraph is cut. It said nothing a reader could check. Fifth, the skills block shrinks to one line and moves to the end. The duty line "Responsible for backend services" goes below the result line, or is deleted, because the result line says the same thing better.

Notice what did not change. No number was added to the import line, because this story gives Bashar none. If he has the job's run time before and after, with its source, he can add it. If he has not, the line is still better than a duty, because it says what changed. You never add a number to make a line sound stronger.

</GoDeeper>

## One labelled link, not thirty repositories

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-one-link" />

Bashar's GitHub has 31 repositories, mostly tutorial copies. A reader opens one, sees a copy of a tutorial and gives up. Then the reader meets one link that says what they will see, and opens it. The proof was there in both paths. Only the second told the reader where to look.

<GoDeeper title="What a good label looks like">

A label tells the reader what they will see and why it is worth opening. Compare three labels for the same link. "My GitHub" tells the reader nothing and sends them to 31 repositories. "Portfolio" does the same. "Code: moving a nightly import into a queue, with a README that explains the problem" tells the reader what is behind the link and what it proves.

Choose one link, to your strongest piece of work. If that work lives in a repository, make sure its README opens with the problem and the result, and not with install steps. A reader who lands there should understand it without scrolling. Module 5 shows how to write that README, and module 6 returns to labelled links.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What research says about public traces">

Researchers who interviewed maintainers of open source projects found that they look up strangers' profiles and activity history. They form a view from what the person did across projects and who they worked with. A second article, on judging developers from their public traces, says the traces can help people who have no degree. It also says the traces are hard to read, and that developers know they are visible and may arrange them.

Put simply: people who judge developers online look at what you actually did, over time, and who you worked with. They can also tell when a profile was arranged to look good, so keep it real. We found no study showing that the count of green squares on a profile helps you get hired. Do not spend time on the squares. Spend it on one clear link.

</GoDeeper>

## The ten-second test

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m1-ten-second-test" />

Ask a partner to look at your resume on a phone for ten seconds. Then ask what they remember, and compare it with your best proof. The ten seconds are a practice limit from this course, not a research finding.

<GoDeeper title="How to run the test">

**With a partner.** Open your resume on your phone. Hand it over and start a timer for ten seconds. When it ends, take the phone back and ask: "What do you remember?". Write down what they say, in their words. Do not explain, and do not argue. Ask for a second thing, and write that too.

**Alone.** Set a timer, look at the first screen for ten seconds, close the file, and write what you remember. This is weaker, because you already know what your resume says. Do it a day after you last edited it. Or send a screenshot of only the first screen to a friend. Crop out your phone number, email and address first. Ask them to reply after ten seconds with what they remember. A voice note works too.

**On LinkedIn.** Open your own profile on your phone and look only at what shows before you scroll. Write what a stranger would remember. If you have no profile yet, write "none yet".

**Then compare.** Write your best proof on one line. Was it among the things remembered? Did it sit on the first screen, later in the file, or is it missing? That answer is your baseline. A weak result is normal. It is a starting point, not a grade. Ten seconds is short enough that a viewer keeps only a few things, and long enough to read a heading and a few lines. That is why the course uses it for practice.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Areej's old resume, as a worked example">

Areej is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative. Her old resume has two pages. At the top is an objective paragraph that an AI tool wrote. Below it is a skills block of 22 technologies. Her best work, the internship project that made a slow report faster, appears only as three vague bullets. One of them says "Improved speed by 90%". She cannot say of what, over which days, or how it was measured. A GitHub link sits at the bottom of page 2.

A classmate looked at it on a phone for ten seconds. The classmate said: "a long paragraph about being passionate, then a list of tools." Her best proof was not among the things remembered.

Her one change: cut the paragraph, put the report fix and one link under her name, and shrink the skills to one line. She did not rewrite everything. She chose one change that follows from what the test showed. You will do the same at the gate in chapter 1.6.

</GoDeeper>
