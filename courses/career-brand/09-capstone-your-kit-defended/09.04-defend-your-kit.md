## Pick any line

Mahir is a fictional front-end developer. His mentor asks him to pick any line of his resume and say where its number came from. He has ten minutes to prepare. He does not reread the whole kit. He opens his number map from the last chapter and picks the line he is proudest of: "Cut the checkout page's load time from 3.2 s to 1.9 s (median of five runs on the same phone, before and after) by compressing images." He opens the ledger row behind it and reads his own source, window and part. Then he answers.

This chapter is the defence. It checks the thing the whole course has been about: for any line of your kit, you can say how it was measured and what your part was. You do not need to be smooth. You need to be able to open the row, say what it shows and say what it does not. Nothing in this chapter asks you to share personal hardship, and nothing needs a paid tool.

There are three ways to defend, and any one of them completes the chapter. Choose the one that suits your key, your connection and your mentor. After the defence you fix one thing the feedback named, and you choose your next step. The fix and the step are what the chapter is for. The defence is how you find them.

| Path | What it is | You need |
|---|---|---|
| A. Full AI interview | A conversation of up to 15 turns about your resume and your best work | Your own key and some credit |
| B. Recorded walkthrough | About five minutes of audio, or video if you prefer | A phone and a quiet spot |
| C. Written defence and a call | Written answers for your two strongest lines, then a short call of about 15 minutes | A way to write, and a phone for the call |

Your mentor decides between B and C, so ask which one fits. If you have a key and credit, A is the most useful practice, because it picks the lines for you. A key that fails is not a problem. If the request is refused or the service does not answer, use B or C. Never use a VPN or change your location to get around a refusal.

## Prepare in ten minutes

Whichever path you take, spend ten minutes before you start. Do these four things.

1. Open your number map from chapter 9.3. Pick three lines you could be asked about: your strongest, one with a shared result, and one that is an honest gap.
2. For each line, open the ledger row. Read the source, the window, your part and what else changed. If any part is missing, write it down. You have just found a fix.
3. Say your project's first real user out loud, in a sentence. Then say what a reader sees if your demo is asleep.
4. Decide what you would measure or build next. A date helps.

Keep the ledger open when you answer. It is not cheating. The whole point of the ledger is that you do not have to remember. A person who opens the row and reads it aloud is doing exactly what a hiring manager hopes to see.

## How to answer "how was that measured?"

The question comes in different words: "Where did that number come from?", "Over what window?", "Can I see it?". Answer in four short parts, in this order.

1. **Source.** Where the number was counted: a dashboard, a log, a timing sheet, a bug tracker.
2. **Window.** The dates or the number of runs, before and after.
3. **My part.** What you did, and what someone else did.
4. **What else changed.** Another change in the same period, and why it does not explain the whole result. Or "I cannot separate them."

Here is Areej answering about her first resume bullet. She is a fictional fellow and her numbers are illustrative.

"The 4.6 seconds and 0.41 seconds are the 95th percentile response time of the orders report endpoint. They come from my lead's team dashboard. The first number covers 14 days before the change, 1 to 14 May 2025, and the second covers 14 days after, 16 to 29 May. I found the slow query, read its plan, chose the index and wrote the migration. My lead reviewed it and scheduled the release. A front-end release went out on 20 May, but it did not touch that endpoint. The request count was about 3 percent lower after the change, so less traffic does not explain the drop. I also have the query plan: 3,840 milliseconds before and 46 after on a copy of the staging data."

Notice what she did. She gave each number its source and its window. She said what was hers and what was her lead's. She named another change and ruled it out with a reason. She did not say "about 90 percent", because that number is not in her ledger. If the interviewer pushes on something she does not know, her honest answer is "I did not measure that." She then adds how she would: for example, for whether customers got invoices sooner, she would ask the owner for two weeks of order and invoice timestamps.

A shorter answer works too. "I did not measure that. I would count rejected rows before and after" is a full answer when it is true. Never invent a source to look prepared. The interviewer flags invented reasons, and a mentor finds them with one more question.

## Path A: the full AI interview

The interviewer reads two things you paste: your resume, and your most important work, which is your case study or your project plan. It asks an opening question, then follows up on how each number was measured, over what window, what your part was, and what else changed. It asks what the alternative was when you made a technical choice. It asks who your first real user is and what a reader sees if your demo is asleep. It ends with feedback: your strengths, what to do better, a note on each topic starting with Strong, Getting there or Not yet, named flags with your own words, and three next steps, including one thing to measure or build next.

Use it as practice. "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers, and the interviewer is told to accept them. It is not a hiring decision, and it can be wrong. It gives no score, so read the level word on each note. Tools like this tend to agree with you and to like long answers, so check every point against your own ledger. A flag you disagree with is a flag you can answer, as chapter 9.2 showed. Your mentor reads the transcript and the feedback with you. A remaining flag is not a failure. An invented reason is the thing to avoid.

Paste about the first page of your resume and the main parts of your case study or plan, with roles in place of names. Never paste your full name, phone, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles, such as "a logistics client" or "my team lead". If you do not have a key or credit, the recorded walkthrough or the written defence below completes this chapter.

```aiinterview
title: "Defend your kit"
opening: "Pick any line on your resume. Tell me where its number came from, and what your part was."
focus: [resume, impact, case-study, best-technical, ownership]
context: resume-and-project
maxTurns: 15
whyDepth: 4
required: false
rubric: "A strong defence lets a reader pick any line of the resume or case study and trace it to a source, a window, the learner's own part and what else changed, and names a first real user for the project and what a reader sees if the demo is asleep. Probe each number: 'How did you measure it? Over what window? Where can I see it?'; if the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'; on a technical choice ask 'What was the alternative?'. Near the end ask 'What would you measure or build next?'. Accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would measure next; never push the learner to guess or invent. An honest gap with a plan beats an unsourced number. Judge features, not length, and quote the learner's words. Begin each focus-area note with a level word: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Next steps: one thing to measure next or one small thing to build next around the learner's own needs, never a named project idea. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person. Flags are feedback, not a pass mark."
```

## Path B: the recorded walkthrough

Speak for about five minutes. Audio only is accepted, and weak data is never marked down. Record it on your phone in a quiet place, in one go or in short pieces. If your connection is weak, record offline and upload when you can, or send the file to your mentor in the way they ask. You do not need a script, but a plan helps. Use the outline below with your kit open.

```text
WALKTHROUGH OUTLINE (about 5 minutes)
0:00  Say the path, and which parts came from Areej, if any.
0:30  Open three resume lines. For each: the number, its ledger row,
      the source, the window. About 30 seconds each.
2:00  Open the case study. Say what I did and what the team did.
3:15  Open the project plan. Name the first real user.
4:00  Answer two of the six defence questions out loud.
5:00  Say one thing I would measure or build next, and by when.
```

Say what you see as you open each part. If you hear yourself say "we", stop and say who did what. If a number has no row, say so. That is a good outcome, because it becomes your fix.

## Path C: the written defence and a short call

Answer the six defence questions in writing for your two strongest lines, as short paragraphs. The questions are: how was it measured, what was your part and the team's, what else changed in that window, why this project and who is the first real user, what a reader sees if the demo is asleep, and what you would build or measure next. Then your mentor calls you for about 15 minutes and asks the follow-ups they choose. Nothing is recorded unless you agree. If a call is hard to arrange because of power or data, tell your mentor, who can send the questions in writing and accept a short voice note.

## Areej's defence

Areej pasted her resume and her case study with contact details removed, and used path A. She picked the report line first. Her answers were close to the answer above. The interviewer drew no unsupported number flag, because every number she gave was in her ledger. It drew one vague ownership flag. When asked about the release, she had said "we deployed it". The note said: "You described the release as a team action, but your part and your lead's part were not separated." Her fix was a line and a ledger row, as in chapter 9.2: "my lead deployed it after my migration passed review". Of the three next steps on her card, she chose the one that closes her honest gap. She would ask the shop owner for two weeks of order and invoice timestamps and compare the median gap with the spreadsheet history, and she gave herself a date.

## Fix one thing

After the defence, fix one thing the feedback named. It may be a line in your resume, a ledger row, or a link. Record it with the line before and after, so your mentor can see the change. Here is Mahir's.

Before, in his profile: "We made checkout faster." After: "I compressed the images on the checkout page and my lead approved the new sizes; load time fell from 3.2 s to 1.9 s (median of five runs on one phone, before and after)." He then changed the ledger row to say the same thing, and added what else changed: his host also changed its caching that month, so part of the gain may not be his. His next step was to time the page on a second phone by the end of the week.

A good fix names its source. It does not add a number you cannot show. It does not delete a number to make a flag go away. And it is small: one line, one row, one link.

## Common mistakes

- **Restating the resume.** Reading your line back is not an answer. Give the source, the window and your part.
- **Deleting the number to remove the flag.** The flag asked for a source. A bare claim with no number is weaker than an honest gap.
- **A fix in the spoken answer only.** The kit still says the old thing. Change the line and the row.
- **A next step with no date.** "Measure more" is a wish. "Ask for timestamps by Sunday" is a step.
- **A made-up reason.** If you did not choose between two options, do not explain why you picked one. Say what you did know.

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] I completed one path: the interview, a recorded walkthrough, or a written defence with a call.
- [ ] I recorded one fix, with the line or row before and after.
- [ ] I chose one next step with a date.
- [ ] Nothing I recorded, pasted or wrote includes contact details, secrets or other people's details.
