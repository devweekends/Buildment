## One evening before the deadline

Sawsan is a fictional data analyst. The night before she sent her kit to her mentor, she did what this chapter asks you to do. She listed every number in her resume and her profile. Then she looked for the ledger row behind each one. Eleven numbers were fine. One was not. Her resume said "cut weekly report time by half", and there was no row. She had never timed the old way of making the report. The number had come from a feeling that the work went faster.

She was lucky, because she found it on a quiet evening and not in front of her mentor. She had two honest choices. She could cut the claim, or she could keep what she knew and say what she did not know. She chose the second. Her line became: "Rebuilt the weekly report so it no longer needs a manual copy step; I have not timed it, and timing starts this week." It is less impressive and much safer. A reader can check the first half, and the second half tells the reader that she knows the limit of her claim. In chapter 9.4, "how was that measured?" is the first question your mentor asks, and an honest gap is a full answer.

This chapter has one job: to make sure that the eight parts of your kit agree with each other, and that you can stand behind every number. It is a checking chapter more than a writing chapter. Most of what you find will be small. A few things will be large. All of them are cheaper to fix now than in the middle of a defence. What you build here feeds chapter 9.4, where you are asked about the lines you check today. It also feeds the gate in chapter 9.6, where your mentor reads your index first.

## What you will do

The work has five steps. Keep the order, because most fixes come from the second step.

1. Collect the eight parts in one kit index.
2. Make a number map: every number in your resume and profile, matched to a ledger row or an honest gap.
3. Run the privacy check on everything public.
4. Run the link check, on a phone, without signing in.
5. Mark each part as public or private, and note any should-have you finished.

The claim ledger sits in the middle of your kit on purpose. Your resume, your LinkedIn profile and your case study take their numbers from it, and your defence goes back to it. A number that appears in a public part and not in the ledger is a missing link. The picture shows the question that finds it. You will ask it once for every number in your resume and profile.

## Step 1: the kit index

Open a private note or document on your phone or laptop. It may be a plain text file, a notes app or a private repository. It must be somewhere only you can open, because it will list where your private parts live. Copy the template below and fill one line for each part. Each line has a link or a file name, a status of public or private, and one sentence on what is in it.

```text
KIT INDEX (private)
Owner: [first name or a nickname, no contact details]
Date: [today]

1. Career arc document        | [file or link] | private | [one sentence]
2. Inventory and claim ledger | [file or link] | private | [one sentence]
3. Project plan               | [file or link] | private | [one sentence]
4. Showcase plan + case study | [plan file]    | private | [one sentence]
                              | [case study]   | public  | [one sentence]
5. Resume + tailored copy     | [files]        | shared with applications | [one sentence]
6. LinkedIn profile           | [profile link] | public  | [one sentence]
7. Post + channel plan        | [post link or file] | public or private | [one sentence]
8. Website plan               | [file]         | private | [one sentence]

Parts I took from Areej: [none, or list, for example "case study"]
Should-haves done: [demo deployed / post published / site live / none]
```

The status words follow one rule. Anything that holds employer details, contact details or your own honest notes stays private. That is your arc, your ledger and your plans. Your resume is sent to people who ask, and it is not posted for everyone, because it holds your phone number and email. Your LinkedIn profile, your case study and your published post are written for strangers, so they are public.

If you used Areej's material for a part, say so in the index. Areej's case study or her post may stand in for yours if you have no work you may show. Your arc, project plan, resume, profile and website plan must still be your own. If one of your parts is not finished, write "not finished" next to it and say what is missing. An honest index is better than a tidy one.

## Step 2: the number map

This is the step that finds most problems. Open your resume and your LinkedIn profile side by side with your claim ledger. Copy the template below. Then read your resume from the top, and every time you meet a number, add a row.

```text
NUMBER MAP
Line (first words) | Where it is     | The number          | Ledger row or gap | Source and window
```

A number is anything a reader could ask "how do you know?" about. Percentages, times, counts, sizes and years of experience all count. So do words that hide a number, such as "doubled", "half", "dozens" and "hundreds". The word "daily" in "a daily orders report" is not a claim and needs no row.

For each number, one of three things must be true. The number is in a ledger row, and the row has a source and a window. Or the line is an honest gap: it says what you did not measure, and maybe how you would. Or the number is a plain fact about the world, such as a date you can show on a certificate or a degree. If none of the three is true, the number has to change. You can find its source, rewrite the line as a gap, or cut it. Do not invent a source to fill a row.

Here is part of Areej's number map. In it, p95 means the time that 95 of every 100 requests finish within.

| Line (first words) | Where | The number | Ledger row or gap | Source and window |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cut the p95 of a daily orders report | Resume | 4.6 s to 0.41 s | R2 | Team dashboard, 14 days before and 14 days after |
| Cut the p95 of a daily orders report | Resume | 14 days | R2 | The window stated in the row |
| Saved a print shop about 3 hours a week | Resume | about 3 hours a week | R3 | Her timing: 6 minutes x 31 invoices = 186 minutes, minus 7 upkeep = 179 |
| Saved a print shop about 3 hours a week | Resume | median 7 to 1 minute | R3 | Timed over two weeks before and two weeks after |
| Revoked and replaced a leaked key | Resume | no number | R6 | Checkable through the revocation and the push protection setting |
| Merged 57 duplicate sign-ups | Resume, Volunteer | 57 | R4 | 412 rows, 57 duplicates, 355 imported |
| Double bookings: 9 to 1 | Resume, Volunteer | 9 to 1 | R5 | Counted from the organisers' chat, with the caveat that sessions fell from three to two a week |
| Campus Board events endpoint | Resume | 820 ms to 95 ms | R7 | Same k6 script, data and laptop before and after |
| Customers got invoices sooner | Gap line in the map | none | G1 | Honest gap: not measured. Next step: two weeks of timestamps |

The whole map has 14 numbers. They map to seven ledger rows and one honest gap, and the rest of the rows look like these. Look at what she did. In every row the source says where the number was counted and over what window. In the row for double bookings, the caveat travels with the number. In the last row there is no number at all, and that is a good row. It shows a reader that she knows where her evidence stops.

When a row is missing, do what Sawsan did. Ask two questions. First: do I have a record that I can find, such as a dashboard, a log, a timing sheet or a message? If yes, build the ledger row now, with its window and its source, and then the line is fine. Second: if I have no record, what do I honestly know? Write that, say it was not measured, and say how you would measure it. Both ways end with a line that is true.

## Step 3: the privacy check

A kit has private parts and public parts, and the danger is the place where they meet. Open every part you marked as public and look for the following. Tick each one only after you looked.

```text
PRIVACY CHECK
[ ] No keys, passwords, tokens or secrets in any public text, screenshot or repository
[ ] No customer data, client names or employer names without written or recorded permission
[ ] The case study uses redacted names, and the permission is recorded in my ledger
[ ] No private company data in any chart (axes, labels, table names)
[ ] No other person's details (names, phones, emails, photos) without their agreement
[ ] My resume is not posted publicly. It is sent to people who ask
[ ] My arc and my ledger are private and not linked from any public page
[ ] My phone number and home address do not appear in any public part
```

Some of these checks need a second look. A chart can carry a table name or a client's name in a small label. A screenshot can show a browser tab, a username or a key in the corner. A public repository keeps its history, so a secret that you deleted last month may still be there. If you find a key in your history, the answer is to revoke it and create a new one, as Areej did, and not to delete a file. If you are unsure whether something may be public, treat it as private and ask the person who owns it.

## Step 4: the link check

Every public link in your kit must open for a stranger, on a phone, without signing in or installing anything. Gather them: the links in your resume, your profile, your case study, your post and your Featured section. Then use a phone that is not signed in to your accounts, or a private browser window, and open each one. Keep a record like this one.

```text
LINK CHECK
Link label                         | Where it is used  | Opened on phone, not signed in? | Loads in a few seconds? | Notes
Case study: a slow report ...      | Resume, LinkedIn  | yes / no                        | yes / no                |
```

For each failed link, fix it before you go on. The usual failures are a sign-in wall, a private repository, a page on a free host that has gone to sleep, and a typo. If your demo sleeps, make sure the case study page and its screenshots open without it, because that is what a reader sees first. If a link is not worth fixing, remove it from the resume and the profile.

## Step 5: public or private, and what you finished

The index already has a status on each part. Now read it once more as a stranger would. Ask what a stranger can open today, and what they can learn about you from it. Then note your should-haves. A deployed demo with a demo account, a published post and a live website are good to have. None is required. Say honestly which you finished.

## Using AI on your own material

The rule from chapter 2.5 holds here: an AI tool may restructure your material, but it never adds numbers, ownership or experience. For this chapter, use it for one job only. Remove your name, phone, email, address and every client or employer name from your resume and profile text. Paste the cleaned text and ask the tool to list every number, every percentage and every word that hides a number, with the sentence it appears in. That saves time, because it is easy to miss a "doubled" in the About section.

Then stop. The tool must not map the numbers or supply a source, an owner or a window, because it does not know where your numbers came from. If you ask it to "fill the ledger row", it writes something that sounds right and is not yours. That is the invented reason that the AI interviewer flags, and a mentor finds it with one question. You do the mapping, from your own records. After you edit a line, run the empty-sentence pass from module 6: ask what a reader could check.

## If you have no work yet, or no laptop

Follow the no-work-yet and no-install paths from chapter 2.5. Everything in this chapter works on a phone with a notes app, with the index and the maps kept as plain text in a private note. Use Areej's case study or post where you have none of your own, and say so in the index. The number map still applies to your own resume and profile. If fewer than eight parts are finished, list what you have, mark the rest "not finished", and ask your mentor which part to finish first.

## Common mistakes

- **A number you remember but cannot source.** It feels true, so it stays. Test it: could you show the source to a stranger in two minutes?
- **A ledger row that exists but does not match the line.** The ledger says 14 days, the resume says "a month". Make them identical.
- **A public part that links to a private one.** The profile links to a document that only you can open. Fix the link or the privacy.
- **A link checked while signed in.** It opened for you because your browser knew you. Check on a phone that is not signed in.
- **A claim that says "we" in one place and "I" in another.** Mark it now. Chapter 9.2 showed why the line and the ledger must agree.

## Acceptance criteria

You are done with this chapter when all of these are true.

- [ ] All eight parts are listed in the kit index, each with a link or a file and a status.
- [ ] Every number in your resume and profile is in the number map, with a ledger row or an honest gap.
- [ ] The privacy check is finished, and anything found is fixed.
- [ ] Every public link opened on a phone without signing in.
- [ ] Each part is marked public or private, and your should-haves are noted.

## Where to keep it, and what comes next

Keep the index, the number map, the privacy check and the link record together in one private place. Do not post them. In chapter 9.4 you will use the number map as your preparation: the lines it covers are the ones the interviewer or your mentor will pick. In chapter 9.6 your mentor reads the index first, then samples three lines from the map.
