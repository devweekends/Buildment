The interview is over and a page of feedback is waiting. What do you do with it? This chapter turns feedback into one change in your kit and one next step. Areej is a fictional fellow, and her numbers are illustrative.

## Strengths first, then fixes

<MermaidDiagram title="The order of a feedback card" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Strengths to build on"] --> B["What to do better, each with a fix"]
  B --> C["Notes per focus area"]
  C --> D["Things to watch: flags with your words"]
  D --> E["Three next steps"]
`} legend="The feedback card from the AI interviewer, from top to bottom. A mentor's feedback can be read in the same order." />

The card has a fixed order. Strengths come first, so you know what to keep. Then come fixes, each with one concrete change. Then a note for each topic, starting with a level word. Then flags, which quote your own words. Last come three next steps. Read it from the top. Do not jump to the flags.

<GoDeeper title="How to read the card, part by part">

**Strengths to build on.** Read these first and copy them into your notes. They tell you what to keep when you edit. A strength is not a compliment to ignore. It is the part of your kit that already works.

**What to do better.** Each item comes with one fix you can do. If an item has no fix you can act on, ask your mentor what it means.

**Notes per focus area.** Each note starts with a level word: Strong, Getting there or Not yet. There is no number score. Read the level words as a map of where to spend your time.

**Things to watch.** These are the flags. Each one quotes your own words, so you can find the exact sentence. The platform keeps a flag only when its quote really appears in your answers.

**Three next steps.** These suggest things to measure or build around your own needs. They never name a specific project idea. You choose one. Beat 4 shows how.

The interviewer is practice, not a hiring decision, and it can be wrong. Your mentor reads the same card with the transcript. Mentor feedback can be read in the same order: what to keep, what to fix, what to watch, what to do next.

</GoDeeper>

## Tap a flag, see your words

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m9-tap-the-flag" />

Part of an early draft of Areej's answer is on the card. Tap each phrase the interviewer flagged. Each flag says why it was raised and gives a fix. Notice that no fix adds a new fact. Each one uses something already in her ledger or her case study.

<GoDeeper title="The five flags and four ways to fix a line">

The AI interviewer uses five named flags. Each points at your own words.

1. **Vague ownership.** It stays unclear what you personally did. Example: "We deployed it."
2. **Unsupported number.** A number or result with no source or way of measuring. Example: "It got about 90 percent faster."
3. **Pseudo-story.** It sounds like a story, but it has no situation, action or outcome that anyone could check. Example: "We faced many challenges, but we overcame them together."
4. **Invented reason risk.** A reason that sounds made up after the fact. Example: "I chose that database because it scales better", when you never compared two.
5. **Jargon without explanation.** A technical term used without saying what it means.

For a flagged line there are four fixes. Choose by asking what is true.

- **Rewrite the line** so it names your part and the team's.
- **Add the source** from your ledger: the number, the window and where it was counted.
- **Write an honest gap** when you never measured it: "I did not measure that. To find out, I would ..."
- **Explain the term**, in the line or in the page where a reader goes next.

A fix is only allowed to use a fact you already have. If you need a new fact, that is a next step, not a fix.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Worked example: Areej's three flags">

The card shows three flags from one early draft answer.

"We deployed it" earns vague ownership. Areej did not deploy alone. Her fix names both parts: "my lead deployed it after my migration passed review". Every word of it is in her ledger.

"It got about 90 percent faster" earns unsupported number. She cannot say of what, over which days, or measured how. The ledger can: the report endpoint's p95 fell from 4.6 s to 0.41 s, measured over 14 days before and 14 days after, from the team dashboard. The fix is the ledger row, not a better-sounding number. If the row had not existed, the right fix would have been a gap.

"Using a composite index" earns jargon. A composite index is one index built on two columns together, here status and creation date. Areej does not need to delete the term. Her case study explains it, so her fix points there.

</GoDeeper>

## One change to the kit

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m9-one-change" />

In her capstone interview, Areej drew one flag: vague ownership on "we deployed it". She did not only fix what she said next. She changed the line in her kit, and the ledger row it came from. Now the kit, the ledger and her answer all say the same thing.

<GoDeeper title="Areej's fix, step by step">

These steps are a pattern to copy for your own kit.

1. **Quote the flag.** She copied the words from the card: "we deployed it".
2. **Search the kit for those words.** The same wording had slipped into the share cell of her ledger row and into a sentence of her case-study draft. She found both with a text search.
3. **Fix the line.** She wrote what each person did: "my lead deployed it after my migration passed review".
4. **Fix the source.** She changed the share cell of the ledger row to the same sentence. If she had only changed the case study, the ledger would still say the old thing, and a mentor opening the ledger would see two stories.
5. **Check every place that quotes the row.** Her resume bullet already said "my lead reviewed and scheduled the release". The three places now agree.

Fix the source of the problem, not only the words of your answer. A spoken answer is gone in a minute. A kit line is what the next reader sees. In chapter 9.4 you will record one fix like this, with the line before and after.

</GoDeeper>

## One thing to build or measure next

<MermaidDiagram title="From three next steps to one" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Three next steps from the feedback"] --> B["Pick the one that closes a ledger gap"]
  B --> C["One action with a date"]
  C --> D["Ask the shop owner for two weeks of timestamps"]
`} legend="Narrowing the card's three next steps to one action with a date. The last box is Areej's." />

The card gives three next steps. You do not need all three. Pick the one that closes a gap in your ledger, or moves the number in your project plan. Then give it a date and a first small action. Areej's gap was that she never measured whether customers got invoices sooner. Her action: ask the shop owner for two weeks of order and invoice timestamps.

<GoDeeper title="How to choose, and what a good next step looks like">

Imagine the card gave Areej three next steps. The first is to measure the honest gap about invoices. The second is to add a slow-query alert, which her case study names as what she would do differently. The third is to have the shop owner record one real week of sales in her project. These three are an illustration, not her real card.

All three are good. She chooses the first, for two reasons. It closes a gap in her ledger, which the capstone rubric reads under Evidence. And it needs one small action she controls: an email to the owner.

A good next step has four parts. It says what you will do. It says what question it answers. It names the person or the source you need. It has a date, such as "ask this Sunday, compare the two weeks by the Sunday after". Areej's full step is to compare the median gap between order and invoice with the spreadsheet history. If it is not possible this month, she writes why, and keeps the gap in the ledger as an honest gap.

A step with no date is a wish. A step that needs a new product, a paid tool or a big rewrite is too large. Choose the smallest step that makes one row stronger.

</GoDeeper>

## When the feedback is wrong

<InteractiveCanvas slug="career-m9-keep-the-term" />

A jargon flag landed on "composite index". Areej decided to keep the term, because her readers are engineers and her case study explains it. She did not ignore the flag. She answered it by putting the explanation where an engineering reader looks. The decision is hers, and it comes with a reason.

<GoDeeper title="When you may disagree with a flag">

A model can be wrong. It may flag a term that every reader of your target posting already knows. It may misread a sentence, or miss that a number is explained in the next line. The platform checks only that the quote appears in your answer. It does not check that the flag is fair.

You may keep what a flag questions if you can give a reason a reader can follow. A good reason names the reader and the place of the explanation: "My readers are backend engineers, and my case study explains the term in two sentences." A weak reason is "I like it" or "the AI is just a tool". Do not reject a flag because fixing it is hard work.

Two checks help. Ask whether a stranger reading the line alone could check it. Ask your mentor, whose review counts alongside any tool. If both agree with the flag, fix the line.

Course 14, "The Founder Interview", chapter 7.2, teaches reading feedback critically in more depth.

</GoDeeper>
