## Learning log

1. **Your strength:** What was the broad version of your strength, and which questions made it narrow? (Or use Hadeel's: what was her broad version, and what did the questions change?)
2. **Your witness:** Who could say something about this strength, and what would you ask them to write? (Or use Hadeel's: who could say it for her?)

## Your task: a strength and a weakness

This task has two short answers, and it fills slot 4 of your story bank. The first answer is a strength that someone can check. Name one skill, tell one recent time with a fact or a number, and say what another person said or saw. If nobody has said anything yet, say that honestly and say whom you will ask. The second answer is a real weakness. Name a habit or a skill gap that matters for the work, give one example with what it cost, and say what you do about it. The plan should be something a person could see.

Slot 4 of the bank holds the weakness story: a time the weakness showed, and what you did. Keep it on the same page or note as your other stories, with the same five lines: the time, your part, the decision, the number or the honest gap, and what you would change. Stories 1 to 3 came from modules 1, 2 and 5. The strength is not a separate slot, but it is worth a line at the top of the page, because it is the first thing you will say when an interviewer asks what you are good at.

Use your own work. If you have no story for this yet, use Hadeel's material and say so. Write each answer in about 60 to 90 words, in short sentences. Choose a skill or a habit from work or study. You do not need to write about health, family or hardship, and you should not.

Then speak. Say both answers aloud once, and time them with a phone. Write down the number of seconds, and write one thing you changed after you heard yourself. Add one pause sentence and one check question that you would use on a video call. A voice note link stays optional, because audio costs data. The spoken test matters here more than in any earlier gate, because a strength or a weakness that reads well can still sound stiff when it is said, and the sentences you cannot say easily are the ones to shorten.

## Hadeel's work, if you use it

Hadeel is the fellow you met in chapter 1.1, and her full resume is in chapter 3.6. Her strength is finding why pages are slow on cheap phones. Her evidence is the booking page for the clinic booking app. It took about 6 seconds to load on the team's cheapest test phone on a slow connection, and she cut it to about 2 seconds. She measured 10 loads before and after and reports the middle value: she puts the ten times in order and takes the middle one. Afterwards the founder asked her to check the clinic dashboard too.

Her weakness is that she waits too long before asking for help. She once spent two days stuck on a time-zone bug in the booking form before she asked the backend developer, who found it in an hour. Her plan: after two hours stuck, she writes down what she tried and asks. She has done this on the bakery work.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your answers part by part. Short answers can be Met. Length is not a criterion.

**Part 1: the strength.**

- **Met:** One skill with an input and an output that someone could check, and a recent example with a fact or number.
- **Partly:** A kind of work, but not yet one skill, or an example without a number.
- **Not yet:** Adjectives only, such as hard-working.

**Part 2: the evidence for it.**

- **Met:** What another person said or saw, or an honest gap with the person you will ask.
- **Partly:** A witness is named, but the claim is larger than the facts.
- **Not yet:** A quote or number you cannot source.

**Part 3: the weakness.**

- **Met:** A real habit or skill that matters, with one example, what it cost and a plan someone could see.
- **Partly:** A real habit with an example, but the plan is only a wish.
- **Not yet:** A strength in disguise, or a weakness that is the whole job.

**Part 4: honesty and speaking.**

- **Met:** An honest limit on the plan, your spoken seconds written down, and one pause sentence and one check question.
- **Partly:** The limit is there, but the spoken time or the check question is missing.
- **Not yet:** A claim that the weakness is already fixed.

**Pass rule:** no part is "Not yet", and at most one part is "Partly".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions, and often one of them is a "why". For example: "Who saw that?" or "What did the late work cost?" You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks about one real strength and then moves to a second topic, so it works best if you have both answers ready. It needs nothing pasted before you start, because it works from what you say. Answer in short sentences, as you would on a call.

Do not paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead: "a client", "my team lead", "a clinic manager". If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "Strengths and weaknesses"
opening: "What is one real strength you bring to a small team? Tell me about one time it showed."
focus: [strengths-weaknesses, storytelling]
context: none
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 3
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a narrow strength with one recent time, a fact or number, and something another person said or saw; and a real weakness that matters for the work, with one example and a plan someone could see. Probes: ask for one specific time when the answer is adjectives or a habit; ask 'What was your part?' when the answer says we; ask 'How do you know?' or 'Who saw that?' after a strength; ask 'What did it cost?' and 'What are you doing about it?' after a weakness. If the weakness is a strength dressed as a weakness, such as perfectionism, ask for a real example. Stop rules: accept 'no one has said that yet', 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would do. Never push the learner to guess. Score features, not length; an honest gap with a plan beats an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words. Flags: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Be kind and direct, about the answer, not the person. Never ask about health, family or hardship. If evidence is thin, suggest how to get it, such as asking a client for a sentence."
```

## Written task

Write one strength with evidence and one real weakness with a plan, yours or Hadeel's. If you did the drill, add the one piece of feedback you will act on and why, or why you disagree with it.

Then add one pause sentence and one check question you would use on a video call, the seconds it took you to say both answers aloud, and one thing you changed after hearing yourself. allowUrl: true

Now I send each problem the same day, in two lines. I have done this for a month, so I cannot yet say how much it helps. Pause sentence: Let me think for a moment.
