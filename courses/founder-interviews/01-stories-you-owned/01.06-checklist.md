## Module 1 checklist

- [ ] I tell a story about one time, not what I usually do or what I am like
- [ ] I use "we" for the team's work and "I" for my part, and I can say where the line is
- [ ] I name the options I had and what mattered at the time I chose
- [ ] I say how I know what happened, or I say plainly that I did not measure it
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 1.2 to 1.4, and finished the quiz in 1.5
- [ ] I submitted my story below

## Learning log

1. **Your first answer:** The last time you told someone about your work, did you describe one time or what you usually do? Give one short example, or use Samer's first answer.
2. **Your part:** In a team project of yours, which part was yours alone? If you prefer not to use your own work, use Hadeel's: what was her part on the booking page?
3. **Your decision:** Which option did you reject, and what mattered to you then? If you cannot remember, what could help you remember, such as old messages or notes?
4. **Your change:** What would you change if you did the work again? Is it something specific, or does it sound like "work harder"?

## Your task: one story you owned

This task brings the whole module together in one piece of writing. You will tell one story about one time, in about 150 to 250 words, the way you would say it to a founder. The goal is not to sound impressive. The goal is to give the founder things he can check and ask about: a day, your part, a choice, a result and a change.

Choose a piece of work you did that has a clear problem. It can be a job, an internship, a freelance gig, a volunteer role or a study project. It can be small. Samer's address check was small, and it was a good story. Then include five things. First, the time and the problem, in one or two sentences. Second, what the team did and what only you did, with "we" and "I" in the right places. Third, the options you had and what mattered then. Fourth, what happened, with a number and its source if you have one, or an honest "I did not measure that" if you do not. Fifth, one thing you would change, and it should be specific.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. A volunteer kept the stock sheet for a charity shop, in a team of three. In October the sheet showed 14 coats the shop did not have. She checked the sheet against the shelves and found that gifts were entered twice. She had three options. She could ask volunteers to enter gifts only once, add a check that flags a repeated item, or count by hand every week. Volunteers change every week, so a rule people must remember mattered less than a check. She chose the check, and the manager agreed. Her first version flagged the wrong rows, because it compared names and not codes, so she changed it on the second day. At the end of the next month the sheet and the shelves differed by 2 coats, counted by hand. She did not measure the hours saved. If she did it again, she would write down the number of mismatches every week from the start.

Read the shape of that answer. It has one time, a clear line between the team and her, three real options, a reason that came from the facts, a number with a source, a mistake and a fix, and an honest gap. Compare it with a weak answer to the same question: "I usually keep the stock sheet accurate. We have a good system, and I fixed some errors." Under the first "why", this answer has no day. Under the second, it has no part for her. It is true, and it gives the founder nothing to hold.

If you prefer not to use your own work, use this provided case. Hadeel is a frontend developer who did a six-month remote internship in a five-person team that built a clinic booking web app used by 6 small clinics. She built the patient booking page from the designer's screens. The backend and the clinic dashboard were built by others. At one clinic she watched three patients struggle with one long form on a phone. She proposed splitting it into three short steps, and the designer agreed. Tell her story as she would. You may choose the options you think she weighed, but say that they are your guess. She did not measure how many more patients finished, so an honest gap is the right answer for the result.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. There is no single right story. A mentor looks for the parts, and for whether each part could survive one more "why?".

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| One time | One day or week, one problem, no "usually" | A time is named, but with some habit words | Only a habit or a description of yourself |
| Your part | "We" and "I" are separated, and your actions use clear verbs | Some "we" is unclear, or one soft verb | Only "we", or a claim wider than your work |
| The decision | At least two options, and what mattered at the time | Options named, but no reason from that time | No options |
| Result and source | A result with its source, or an honest gap with what you would measure | A result with no source | A number that cannot be explained, or no result |
| What you would change | One specific change | A general change | No change, or "work harder" |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Why did you reject the second option?" or "Where does that number come from?". You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill plays a founder. It asks one opening question, follows your answer with "why?" and "how do you know?", and then moves to a second topic. It ends with your strengths and weaknesses, and a fix for each weakness. The practice takes about ten minutes and uses text only.

Answer as you would in a real interview. "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers, and the interviewer is set up to accept them. Its feedback is practice, not a hiring decision, and it can be wrong. Check each point against your own words, and ask your mentor if you disagree.

Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead, such as "a logistics client" or "my team lead". If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "Something you owned"
opening: "Tell me about one piece of work you owned from start to finish. What was the problem, and what was your part?"
focus: [ownership, storytelling]
context: project
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 3
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows one real episode, not a habit: a time, a problem, what the learner did, what happened and how they know. Look for a clear line between team work and the learner's part, the options weighed, what mattered then, and one change. Probes: if the answer describes what they usually do, ask for one specific time. If the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'. After any number ask 'How do you know?' or 'Where does that number come from?'. Ask 'Why this and not another way?' about a choice. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly, and ask what they would do or measure next. Never push the learner to guess a number or invent an example. Scoring: judge features, not length. An honest gap with a plan to measure scores higher than an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words as evidence. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person. If the evidence is thin, suggest how the learner could get it, for example a small project around a need of their own."
```
