## Module 1 checklist

- [ ] I tell a story about one time, not what I usually do or what I am like
- [ ] I use "we" for the team's work and "I" for my part, and I can say where the line is
- [ ] I name the options I had and what mattered at the time I chose
- [ ] I say how I know what happened, or I say plainly that I did not measure it
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 1.2 to 1.4, and finished the quiz in 1.5
- [ ] I submitted my story below

## Learning log

1. **Your first answer:** The last time you told someone about your work, did you describe one time or what you usually do? Give one short example, or use Samer's first answer.
2. **Your part:** In a team project of yours, which part was yours alone? Or use Hadeel's: what was her part on the booking page?
3. **Your decision:** Which option did you reject, and what mattered to you then? If you cannot remember, what could help you remember, such as old messages or notes? Or use Samer's: why did he not block the order?
4. **Your change:** What would you change if you did the work again? Is it something specific, or does it sound like "work harder"? Or use Samer's: what would he change about the address check?

## Your task: story 1 of your story bank

This task brings the whole module together in one piece of writing. You will tell one story about one time, in about 150 to 250 words, the way you would say it to a founder. The goal is not to sound impressive. The goal is to give the founder things he can check and ask about: a day, your part, a choice, a result and a change.

This story is slot 1 of your story bank. Over the course you will fill five slots, one story at a time, and the last gate asks for the whole bank. Keep it simple: one page of paper or one note on your phone for each story, with the date and the words you used. In gate 2 you will add an honest number to this story and write story 2.

Use your own work. If you have no story for this yet, use Hadeel's material, say that you did, and make a plan to find your own story by the end of the course. Choose a piece of work that has a clear problem. It can be a job, an internship, a freelance gig, a volunteer role or a study project. It can be small. Samer's address check was small, and it was a good story. Then include five things. First, the time and the problem, in one or two sentences. Second, what the team did and what only you did, with "we" and "I" in the right places. Third, the options you had and what mattered then. Fourth, what happened, with a number and its source if you have one, or an honest "I did not measure that" if you do not. Fifth, one thing you would change, and it should be specific.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. A volunteer at a community library, in a team of three, noticed that books marked "on loan" were often back on the shelf. She counted on one Saturday: 14 of 60 "on loan" books were on the shelf. She had three options. She could ask borrowers to sign twice, add a return stamp that volunteers press at the shelf, or recount the shelves every Saturday. Volunteers change every week, so a rule people must remember mattered less than a stamp. She chose the stamp, and the librarian agreed. Her first version did not work, because volunteers stamped the wrong page, so on the second Saturday she moved the stamp to the first page of each book. Four weeks later she recounted by hand on one Saturday: 3 of 60 "on loan" books were on the shelf. She did not measure the volunteers' time. If she did it again, she would count on the first Saturday and on every Saturday after, and not just once.

Read the shape of that answer. It has one time, a clear line between the team and her, three real options, a reason that came from the facts, a number with a source, a mistake and a fix, and an honest gap. Compare it with a weak answer to the same question: "I usually keep the loan records right. We have a good system, and I fixed some errors." Under the first "why", this answer has no day. Under the second, it has no part for her. It is true, and it gives the founder nothing to stand on.

If you use the provided case, use Hadeel, whom you met in 1.1. She did a six-month remote internship in a five-person team that built a clinic booking web app used by 6 small clinics. She built the patient booking page from the designer's screens. The backend and the clinic dashboard were built by others. On one visit to a clinic near her home, she watched three patients struggle with one long form on their phones. She proposed splitting it into three short steps, and the designer agreed. Tell her story as she would. You may choose the options you think she weighed, but say that they are your guess. She did not measure how many more patients finished, so an honest gap is the right answer for the result.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer part by part. There is no single right story. A mentor looks for the parts, and for whether each part could still stand at one more "why?".

**Part 1: one time.**

- **Met:** One day or week and one problem, with no "usually" or "whenever".
- **Partly:** A time is named, but habit words are mixed in.
- **Not yet:** Only a habit or a description of yourself.

**Part 2: your part.**

- **Met:** "We" and "I" are separated, and your actions use clear verbs such as built, tested or decided.
- **Partly:** Some "we" is unclear, or one soft verb such as "helped" remains.
- **Not yet:** Only "we", or a claim wider than your work.

**Part 3: the decision and what you would change.**

- **Met:** At least two options, what mattered then, and one specific change.
- **Partly:** Options are named, but the reason or the change is general.
- **Not yet:** No options, or "work harder" as the change.

**Part 4: the result and its source.**

- **Met:** A result with its source, or an honest gap with what you would measure.
- **Partly:** A result with no source.
- **Not yet:** A number that cannot be explained, or no result.

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet, and at most one part is Partly.

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Why did you reject the second option?" or "Where does that number come from?". You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks about one piece of work you owned, then follows your answer with questions about your part and your story. It is the same kind of conversation as the call between Samer and the founder. Paste 50 to 150 words about one story from your bank, with roles instead of names, when the drill asks for your project.

Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "Something you owned"
opening: "Tell me about one piece of work you owned from start to finish. What was the problem, and what was your part?"
focus: [ownership, storytelling]
context: project
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 3
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows one real episode, not a habit: a time, a problem, what the learner did, what happened and how they know. Look for a clear line between team work and the learner's part, the options weighed, what mattered then, and one change. Probes: if the answer describes what they usually do, ask for one specific time. If the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'. After any number ask 'How do you know?' or 'Where does that number come from?'. Ask 'Why this and not another way?' about a choice. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly, and ask what they would do or measure next. Never push the learner to guess a number or invent an example. Scoring: judge features, not length. An honest gap with a plan to measure scores higher than an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words as evidence. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person. If the evidence is thin, suggest how the learner could get it, for example a small project around a need of their own."
```
