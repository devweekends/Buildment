## Module 5 checklist

- [ ] I chose a story where I made a real decision and can explain it past the third why
- [ ] My founder version has four parts and takes under a minute, and a person outside my field could follow it
- [ ] My engineer version answers why this and not that (the need, the option I rejected, how hard it is to undo, when I would look again) and what happens if it fails
- [ ] I ran one area check on my project: what I did, what I did not do and what I would check first, and I do not claim a test I did not run

## Learning log

1. **Your story:** Which technical story did you choose, and which one did you almost choose? Why did you not choose it? (Or use Hadeel's: why is her page-speed work a good choice?)
2. **Your hard word:** Which word or sentence did a person outside your field stop on? How did you change it? (Or use Hadeel's: which word in her story would you test on a friend?)
3. **Your honest gap:** Which question in the engineer version or the area check had no test behind it? What would you check first? (Or use Hadeel's: what did she not test, and what would she check first?)
4. **Your hardest question:** Which question do you hope the technical founder will not ask? Answer it here in two sentences. (Or use Hadeel's: which question would be hardest for her?)

## Your task: story 3, in two versions

This task fills slot 3 of your story bank. Gate 1 started the bank with story 1, and gate 2 added an honest number to it and wrote story 2. Keep the bank as one page, or one note, for each story. Each page has the same five lines: the time, your part, the decision, the number with its source or the honest gap, and what you would change. This gate adds your best technical work to the bank, in two versions.

The founder version is for a person who does not write code. It has four parts: the problem, what you built, the key choice and the result, and it takes under a minute to say. The engineer version is for a person who does your kind of work, and it is also what a technical founder asks. It answers six questions: what you built, why this way, why not the other way, what happens if it fails, how you tested it and what you would change. Write each step with a decision, a test or an honest gap. A short answer is enough, and every term you use should be one you have explained.

Two parts of the engineer version are new since chapters 5.5 and 5.6. For "why not the other way", write one answer in four pieces. Write the need your choice served, and the option you rejected and why. Then write how hard the choice is to undo, and when you would look at it again. If you did not note these at the time, say so, and write what you would note now. Then add one area check from chapter 5.6: what happens at fifty times today's traffic, or what happens when a service you depend on is down. Write what you did, what you did not do, and what you would check first. An honest "I did not test that" with the first check is a strong answer.

Use your own work. If you have no story for this yet, use Hadeel's material and say so. If your work is not code, use your own craft: a design test, a spreadsheet, an operations process. The same six questions apply. A number needs a before, an after and a source, as chapter 2.2 taught. If you did not measure, say so, and say how you would.

A demo is optional. If your project has one that a founder can open in one click, with made-up sample data, add its link or write what it shows (chapter 5.7). Do not add keys or real user data.

Then speak. Say your founder version aloud once, and time it with a phone. Write down the number of seconds, and write one thing you changed after you heard yourself. A voice note link stays optional, because audio costs data. The timing matters because a version that reads well can still run to two minutes when spoken, and a founder will stop listening after the first minute.

## Hadeel's work, if you use it

Hadeel is the fellow you met in chapter 1.1, and her full resume is in chapter 3.6. Her best technical work was the speed of the booking page in the clinic booking app. Its load time was about 6 seconds on a low-cost phone, and she cut it to about 2 seconds. She measured 10 loads on the team's cheapest test phone on a slow connection, before and after. She reports the middle value: she puts the ten times in order and takes the middle one.

She made the clinic photos smaller and loaded them after the form. She also kept the clinic list in the browser for one hour, so it was not fetched on every visit. She did not rewrite the page with another framework, because that was about two weeks of work against two days, for a problem that was mostly images. She did not change the server, because the backend developer was busy with the text-message sending service, so a server change had to wait. One weak point: the saved list could show old hours for up to an hour, and once a patient booked a slot after a clinic had closed early. She and the backend developer added a check at booking time on the server. She did not run a load test on the booking page. If you use her, write the why-not-that answer from these facts and say which pieces her facts do not give.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your two versions part by part. A demo link is optional and is not graded. A short answer can be Met. Length is not a criterion, and a long answer with a gap in it is not better than a short one that is true.

**Part 1: the story you chose.**

- **Met:** Your own decision, with a reason it is your best and an option you rejected.
- **Partly:** Your decision, but no reason given for the choice.
- **Not yet:** You say "we" throughout, or the choice rests only on tools.

**Part 2: the founder version.**

- **Met:** Four parts in plain words, under a minute when spoken, with a number that has a before and a source.
- **Partly:** Four parts, but one term is unexplained or the number has no before.
- **Not yet:** No problem or no result, or mostly terms.

**Part 3: the engineer version.**

- **Met:** Five of six steps have a decision, test or gap, including one why-not-that answer and one area check.
- **Partly:** Three or four steps have one, or the why-not-that answer or the area check is missing.
- **Not yet:** Two steps or fewer, or claims with nothing behind them.

**Part 4: honesty and speaking.**

- **Met:** A named gap with a plan, no test or number you cannot source, and your spoken time written down.
- **Partly:** A gap is named but has no plan, or the spoken time is missing.
- **Not yet:** A test or a number that the next question would break.

**Pass rule:** no part is "Not yet", and at most one part is "Partly".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions, and often one of them is a "why". For example: "Why not run it at night?" or "Where does that number come from?" You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks you to explain your best technical work first as you would to a founder, then it digs four whys deep into the choices you made. It also asks what you chose over what, what happens at fifty times the traffic, and what happens when a service you depend on is down. It works best when you answer in plain words first and go deeper only when it asks. Paste 50 to 150 words about one story from your bank, with roles instead of names, when it asks for your project.

Do not paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead: "a payments startup", "my team lead". If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "The best technical thing you did"
opening: "What is the best technical thing you have done? Explain it first the way you would to me, a founder who does not write code."
focus: [best-technical, five-whys]
context: project
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 4
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a technical story the learner owned: a real decision between options, the reason then, and a result with a before, an after and a source, or an honest 'I did not measure that'. Check the first answer for plain words; if it is full of terms, ask 'What was the problem, for a user?'. Probes: ask for one specific time when the answer describes habits; ask 'What was your part?' when the answer says we; ask 'How do you know?' or 'Where does that number come from?' after any number. On technical answers ask 'Why this and not another choice at that layer?', what would make them change it and how hard that would be; 'What happens at fifty times today's traffic?'; 'What happens if a service you depend on is down?'; 'Who can see whose data?'; 'How did you test it?'. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I did not test that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would check first. Never push the learner to guess. Score features, not length; an honest gap with a plan beats an unsourced number, and a list of patterns they did not build scores low. Quote the learner's words. Flags by name: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Be kind and direct, about the answer, not the person. If evidence is thin, suggest how to get it, such as a small project around their own need."
```
