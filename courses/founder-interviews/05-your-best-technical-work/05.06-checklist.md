## Your task: the two versions of your best technical work

This task brings the whole module together. You write the same story twice, for two listeners. The founder version is for a person who does not write code. It has four parts: the problem, what you built, the key choice and the result, and it takes under a minute to say. The engineer version is for a person who does your kind of work. It answers six questions: what you built, why this way, why not the other way, what happens if it fails, how you tested it and what you would change.

Write each rung of the engineer version with a decision, a test or an honest gap. A short answer is enough. Do not write in terms that you have not explained. If your work is not code, use your own craft: a design test, a spreadsheet, an operations process. The same six questions apply.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, about a fictional fellow. Karim is a frontend developer. He built a page that shows a school's timetable, and his work was to make it load faster on cheap phones. His founder version: "The timetable page took about 7 seconds to load on a cheap phone. I made the pictures smaller and loaded them after the text. I chose that over rewriting the page, because the problem was mostly pictures and a rewrite would take weeks. It now loads in about 3 seconds. I measured 10 loads before and 10 after on one phone." His engineer version has an answer for each rung. For the fourth rung, "what if it fails?", he says the pictures may not load on a very slow line. The text shows first, so the page still works. For the fifth, he says he tested one phone only, and he would test two more.

Look at what makes it strong. Every number has a before, an after and a method. The key choice has an alternative and a reason. The gap, one phone only, is said plainly with a next step. A founder can follow it, and an engineer can ask about any part of it.

A weak answer to the same task looks like this: "I optimised the page using lazy loading and compression and improved performance by 57 percent. It is a best-practice approach." It fails at the first why. The founder cannot follow "lazy loading", and the percentage has no before or after. At the next why, "why this approach?", the answer is "best practice", which is a label and not a decision. At "how did you test it?", there is nothing. Nothing in it is false, but nothing in it can be checked.

If you prefer not to use your own work, use Hadeel's. Hadeel is 24, a frontend developer. In a six-month remote internship she worked on a five-person team that built a clinic booking web app used by 6 small clinics. Her best technical work was the speed of the booking page. Its load time was about 6 seconds on a low-cost phone, and she cut it to about 2 seconds. She measured 10 loads on the team's cheapest test phone on a slow connection, before and after, and reports the middle value. She made the clinic photos smaller and loaded them after the form. She also kept the clinic list in the browser for one hour, so it was not fetched on every visit. She did not rewrite the page with another framework, because that was about two weeks of work against two days, for a problem that was mostly images. She did not change the server, because the backend developer was busy with the reminders. One weak point: the saved list could show old hours for up to an hour, and once a patient booked a slot after a clinic had closed early. She and the backend developer added a check at booking time on the server.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your two versions with the table below. A short answer can be Met. Length is not a criterion, and a long answer with a gap in it is not better than a short one that is true.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Story choice | Your own decision, and a reason it is your best | Your decision, but no reason given | You say "we" throughout, or the choice is only about tools |
| Founder version | Four parts, plain words, a number with a before and a source | Four parts, but one term is unexplained or the number has no before | No problem or no result, or mostly terms |
| Engineer version | A decision, a test or an honest gap for at least five of the six questions | For three or four of the six questions | Two or fewer, or claims with nothing behind them |
| Honesty | A named gap with a plan, and no test or number you cannot source | A gap named, but no plan | A test or a number that the next question would break |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least two parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions, and often one of them is a "why". For example: "Why not run it at night?" or "Where does that number come from?" You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

The drill below plays an interviewer. It asks one opening question about your best technical work. It follows your answer with "why?" and "how do you know?", then moves to a second topic, and it ends with strengths and weaknesses, each with a fix. It works best when you answer as you would in a real interview: first in plain words, then deeper when it asks.

Use it like a real conversation. "I did not measure that", "I did not test that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers, and the interviewer is set up to accept them. Its feedback is practice, not a hiring decision, and it can be wrong. Check each point against your own words, and ask your mentor if you disagree. AI tools tend to agree with you and to like long answers.

Do not paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead: "a payments startup", "my team lead", "a logistics client". If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "The best technical thing you did"
opening: "What is the best technical thing you have done? Explain it first the way you would to me, a founder who does not write code."
focus: [best-technical, five-whys]
context: project
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 4
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a technical story the learner owned: a real decision between options, the reason then, and a result with a before, an after and a source, or an honest 'I did not measure that'. Check the first answer for plain words; if it is full of terms, ask 'What was the problem, for a user?'. Probes: ask for one specific time when the answer describes habits; ask 'What was your part?' when the answer says we; ask 'How do you know?' or 'Where does that number come from?' after any number; ask 'Why this and not another way?', 'What happens if it fails?' and 'How did you test it?'. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I did not test that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would do next. Never push the learner to guess. Score features, not length; an honest gap with a plan beats an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words. Flags by name: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Be kind and direct, about the answer, not the person. If evidence is thin, suggest how to get it, such as a small project around their own need."
```
