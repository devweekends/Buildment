## Learning log

## Your task: story 3, in two versions

This task fills slot 3 of your story bank. Gate 1 started the bank with story 1, and gate 2 added an honest number to it and wrote story 2. Keep the bank as one page, or one note, for each story. Each page has the same five lines: the time, your part, the decision, the number with its source or the honest gap, and what you would change. This gate adds your best technical work to the bank, in two versions.

The founder version is for a person who does not write code. It has four parts: the problem, what you built, the key choice and the result, and it takes under a minute to say. The engineer version is for a person who does your kind of work. It answers six questions: what you built, why this way, why not the other way, what happens if it fails, how you tested it and what you would change. Write each step of the engineer version with a decision, a test or an honest gap. A short answer is enough, and every term you use should be one you have explained.

Use your own work. If you have no story for this yet, use Hadeel's material and say so. If your work is not code, use your own craft: a design test, a spreadsheet, an operations process. The same six questions apply. A number needs a before, an after and a source, as chapter 2.2 taught. If you did not measure, say so, and say how you would.

Then speak. Say your founder version aloud once, and time it with a phone. Write down the number of seconds, and write one thing you changed after you heard yourself. A voice note link stays optional, because audio costs data. The timing matters because a version that reads well can still run to two minutes when spoken, and a founder will stop listening after the first minute.

## Hadeel's work, if you use it

Hadeel is the fellow you met in chapter 1.1, and her full resume is in chapter 3.6. Her best technical work was the speed of the booking page in the clinic booking app. Its load time was about 6 seconds on a low-cost phone, and she cut it to about 2 seconds. She measured 10 loads on the team's cheapest test phone on a slow connection, before and after, and reports the middle value: she puts the ten times in order and takes the middle one.

She made the clinic photos smaller and loaded them after the form. She also kept the clinic list in the browser for one hour, so it was not fetched on every visit. She did not rewrite the page with another framework, because that was about two weeks of work against two days, for a problem that was mostly images. She did not change the server, because the backend developer was busy with the text-message sending service, so a server change had to wait. One weak point: the saved list could show old hours for up to an hour, and once a patient booked a slot after a clinic had closed early. She and the backend developer added a check at booking time on the server.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your two versions part by part. A short answer can be Met. Length is not a criterion, and a long answer with a gap in it is not better than a short one that is true.

**Part 1: the story you chose.**

- **Met:** Your own decision, with a reason it is your best and an option you rejected.
- **Partly:** Your decision, but no reason given for the choice.
- **Not yet:** You say "we" throughout, or the choice rests only on tools.

**Part 2: the founder version.**

- **Met:** Four parts in plain words, under a minute when spoken, with a number that has a before and a source.
- **Partly:** Four parts, but one term is unexplained or the number has no before.
- **Not yet:** No problem or no result, or mostly terms.

**Part 3: the engineer version.**

- **Met:** A decision, a test or an honest gap for at least five of the six steps.
- **Partly:** A decision, a test or a gap for three or four of the six steps.
- **Not yet:** Two steps or fewer, or claims with nothing behind them.

**Part 4: honesty and speaking.**

- **Met:** A named gap with a plan, no test or number you cannot source, and your spoken time written down.
- **Partly:** A gap is named but has no plan, or the spoken time is missing.
- **Not yet:** A test or a number that the next question would break.

**Pass rule:** no part is "Not yet", and at most one part is "Partly".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions, and often one of them is a "why". For example: "Why not run it at night?" or "Where does that number come from?" You answer in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks you to explain your best technical work first as you would to a founder, then it digs four whys deep into the choices you made. It works best when you answer in plain words first and go deeper only when it asks. Paste 50 to 150 words about one story from your bank, with roles instead of names, when it asks for your project.

Do not paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead: "a payments startup", "my team lead". If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "The best technical thing you did"
opening: "What is the best technical thing you have done? Explain it first the way you would to me, a founder who does not write code."
focus: [best-technical, five-whys]
context: project
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 4
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a technical story the learner owned: a real decision between options, the reason then, and a result with a before, an after and a source, or an honest 'I did not measure that'. Check the first answer for plain words; if it is full of terms, ask 'What was the problem, for a user?'. Probes: ask for one specific time when the answer describes habits; ask 'What was your part?' when the answer says we; ask 'How do you know?' or 'Where does that number come from?' after any number; ask 'Why this and not another way?', 'What happens if it fails?' and 'How did you test it?'. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I did not test that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly and ask what they would do next. Never push the learner to guess. Score features, not length; an honest gap with a plan beats an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words. Flags by name: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Be kind and direct, about the answer, not the person. If evidence is thin, suggest how to get it, such as a small project around their own need."
```

## Written task

Example of Not yet: 'I optimised performance with lazy loading and compression and improved it by 57 percent. It is best practice.' It is jargon, a percentage with no before, and a label in place of a reason. Model answer for a different case. Founder version: at our shop's checkout, two customers could buy the last item at the same moment, and 4 orders in one month had to be cancelled. I changed the checkout so the item is held for 10 minutes when a customer starts to pay. I chose that over checking again after payment, because cancelling a paid order loses the customer. In the next 2 months there were no cancelled orders, counted from the order records. Engineer version. Why this way: the clash happened between the basket and the payment, so the hold sits there (decision). Why not check again after payment: a refund costs money and trust (decision). What if the customer leaves: the hold ends after 10 minutes and the item goes back on sale (test: I started a payment, closed the tab, and the item was for sale again after 10 minutes). How tested: two browsers on one last item, not 100 customers at once (honest gap: I would run 100 test orders on a copy). What I would change: show a countdown, because today customers are surprised when a hold ends. Spoken time: 55 seconds; I cut the sentence about the framework."
