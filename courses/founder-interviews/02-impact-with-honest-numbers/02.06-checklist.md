## Module 2 checklist

- [ ] I state a result with a before, an after, a time window and a source
- [ ] I know the places where my evidence may live, and I ask my lead before I use data
- [ ] I say what else changed, and how much of the change was mine
- [ ] I say "I did not measure that" with a plan, and I label every estimate as an estimate
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 2.2 to 2.4, and finished the quiz in 2.5
- [ ] I submitted my two stories below

## Learning log

1. **Your hardest number:** Which number on your resume would be hardest to defend at "from what?" Why? If you prefer not to use your own work, use Leen's "50 percent faster".
2. **Your sources:** For one project of yours, which of the seven places could hold a number, and who would you ask? Or use Hadeel's bakery work: where could she have counted orders?
3. **Your share:** Think of one result. What else changed at the same time? Or use Hadeel's reminders: what else changed in her second month?
4. **Your gap:** Which result of yours have you never measured? What is one thing you could count from now on?

## Your task: two stories with honest numbers

This task brings the whole module together. You will take two stories from your story bank and give each one an honest number or an honest gap. The point is not to find a big number. The point is to say each number the way a founder can check it, and to say plainly what you do not know. You can use the story you wrote in gate 1 as one of them.

For the first story, write the result in the way chapter 2.2 taught: what it was before, what it was after, the time window, and the source. Then add your share, as in chapter 2.4: what else changed in the same period, and a comparison if you have one. For the second story, you may have no number at all. That is fine. Write the honest gap: "I did not measure that", what evidence might exist (chapter 2.3), and what you would count from now on. If one of your stories uses an estimate, label it as an estimate and say how you made it.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. Salma designed the event sign-up form for a university club. For the first story, she writes: "In the term before my redesign, 62 of 100 people who started the form finished it. In the term after, 81 of 100 finished. Both numbers come from the form tool's step report. In the same term, the club emailed the link to all members for the first time, so I do not know how much of the rise is the form. I would compare the visits to the sign-up page before and after." For the second story, she writes: "The club president told me the committee stopped getting messages that said 'where do I click'. I did not count those messages. I would count them in the committee chat for two weeks, before and after the next change." Look at the shape. The first story has a before, an after, a window, a source and an honest share. The second has words quoted as words and a plan to measure.

Now a weak answer to the same task: "My form improved sign-ups by 30 percent. It was the best form the club ever had." Under the first "why", the founder asks "from what?", and there is no base. Under the second, he asks "how do you know?", and there is no source. The sentence "the best form ever" cannot be checked either. A smaller and checkable answer is stronger, because it survives every question.

If you prefer not to use your own work, use Hadeel's material. Her first story is her second resume line: "Added text-message reminders; missed appointments fell from 18 percent to 11 percent." The founder decided to add reminders. Hadeel built them, and she chose to send them at 18:00 the day before the visit, because the clinics said many patients missed early slots. Another developer chose the text-message service. She counted missed appointments out of all appointments in the clinics' booking records, with the other developer's help, for two months before and two months after. In the second month, two of the six clinics also started phoning patients. In the four clinics that did not phone, missed appointments fell from 18 percent to 12 percent. Her second story is her freelance work. She built an online order form and a stock sheet for a family bakery, and she did not count orders. The owner said that phone calls at busy times dropped, and those are the owner's words, not a number. Tell both stories as she would.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your two stories with the table below. There is no required size of result. A small, honest result can be fully Met.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before, after and window | All three are stated in plain numbers, with the unit | A percentage with one of the three missing | A percentage with no base |
| Source | A named source for each number | A source for one number | No source |
| Your share | Names what else changed and uses a comparison, or says plainly it cannot separate them | Mentions other changes but does not use them | Claims the whole change |
| Gap or estimate | A gap has what you would measure, and each estimate has how you got it | A gap without a plan | An unlabeled estimate or an invented number |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Where does that number come from?" or "What else changed that month?". You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill plays a founder. It asks one opening question, follows your answer with "why?" and "how do you know?", and then moves to a second topic. It ends with your strengths and weaknesses, and a fix for each weakness. The practice takes about ten minutes and uses text only.

Answer as you would in a real interview. "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers, and the interviewer is set up to accept them. Its feedback is practice, not a hiring decision, and it can be wrong. Check each point against your own words, and ask your mentor if you disagree.

Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. Use roles instead, such as "a logistics client" or "my team lead". If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "What changed because of your work"
opening: "Pick one piece of your work that made a difference. What changed because of it, and how do you know?"
focus: [impact, ownership]
context: project
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 3
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a result stated with a before, an after, a time window and a source, and how much of the change was the learner's own, with other changes named. Look for a clear line between what the learner did and what the team or the season did. Probes: if the answer gives a percentage, ask 'From what?'. After any number ask 'How do you know?' or 'Where does that number come from?'. If the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'. If the answer describes habits, ask for one specific time. Ask what else changed in the same period. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly, and ask what they would measure next. Never push the learner to guess a number or invent one. Scoring: judge features, not length. An honest gap with a plan to measure scores higher than an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words as evidence. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person. If the evidence is thin, suggest where it might exist, or a small project around a need of their own."
```

The hosting company also upgraded its servers that month, so part of the gain is not mine. I did not repeat the test with the old images on the new servers. I would do that to separate the two. Story 2: I also wrote an order guide for customers. The owner told me that phone questions dropped. I did not count them. I would count the questions in the shop's call notebook for two weeks before and two weeks after the next change."
