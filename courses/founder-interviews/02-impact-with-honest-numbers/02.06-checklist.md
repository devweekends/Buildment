## Module 2 checklist

- [ ] I state a result with a before, an after, a time window and a source
- [ ] I know the places where my evidence may live, and I ask my lead before I use data
- [ ] I say what else changed, and how much of the change was mine
- [ ] I say "I did not measure that" with a plan, and I label every estimate as an estimate
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 2.2 to 2.4, and finished the quiz in 2.5
- [ ] I submitted my two stories below

## Learning log

1. **Your hardest number:** Which number on your resume would be hardest to defend at "from what?" Why? Or use Leen's "50 percent faster".
2. **Your sources:** For one project of yours, which of the seven places could hold a number, and who would you ask? Or use Hadeel's bakery work: where could she have counted orders?
3. **Your share:** Think of one result. What else changed at the same time? Or use Hadeel's reminders: what else changed in her second month?
4. **Your gap:** Which result of yours have you never measured? What is one thing you could count from now on? Or use Leen's lamps: what would she count?

## Your task: story 1 with a number, and story 2

This task brings the whole module together. You will do two things with your story bank. First, you will take story 1 from gate 1 and give it an honest number or an honest gap. Second, you will write story 2, a new story with its own number or gap. The point is not to find a big number. The point is to say each number the way a founder can check it, and to say plainly what you do not know. Keep the bank as you did in gate 1: one page or one note for each story. Story 1 now has a new paragraph, and story 2 is a new page.

Use your own work. If you have no story for this yet, use Hadeel's material and say so. For the number, write the result in the way chapter 2.2 taught: what it was before, what it was after, the time window, and the source. Then add your share, as in chapter 2.4: what else changed in the same period, and a comparison if you have one. If one of your stories has no number at all, that is fine. Write the honest gap: "I did not measure that", what evidence might exist (chapter 2.3), and what you would count from now on. If you use an estimate, label it as an estimate and say how you made it.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. Salma designed the event sign-up form for a university club. For the first story, she writes: "In the term before my redesign, 62 of 100 people who started the form finished it. In the term after, 81 of 100 finished. Both numbers come from the form tool's step report. In the same term, the club emailed the link to all members for the first time, so I do not know how much of the rise is the form. I would compare the visits to the sign-up page before and after." For the second story, she writes: "The club president told me the committee stopped getting messages that said 'where do I click'. I did not count those messages. I would count them in the committee chat for two weeks, before and after the next change." Look at the shape. The first story has a before, an after, a window, a source and an honest share. The second has words quoted as words and a plan to measure.

Now a weak answer to the same task: "My form improved sign-ups by 30 percent. It was the best form the club ever had." Under the first "why", the founder asks "from what?", and there is no base. Under the second, he asks "how do you know?", and there is no source. The sentence "the best form ever" cannot be checked either. A smaller and checkable answer is stronger, because it still stands at every question.

If you use the provided case, use Hadeel's material. Her first story is her second resume line: "Added text-message reminders; missed appointments fell from 18 percent to 11 percent." The founder decided to add reminders. The other developer built the text-message sending service. Hadeel built the reminder rule and the setting screen, and she chose to send the reminders at 18:00 the day before the visit, because the clinics said many patients missed early slots. She counted missed appointments out of all appointments in the clinics' booking records, with the other developer's help, for two months before and two months after. In the second month, two of the six clinics also started phoning patients. In the four clinics that did not phone, missed appointments fell from 18 percent to 12 percent. Her second story is her freelance work. She built an online order form and a stock sheet for a family bakery, and she did not count orders. The owner said that phone calls at busy times dropped, and those are the owner's words, not a number. Tell both stories as she would.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your two stories part by part. There is no required size of result. A small, honest result can be fully Met.

**Part 1: before, after and window.**

- **Met:** All three are stated in plain numbers, with the unit.
- **Partly:** A percentage with one of the three missing.
- **Not yet:** A percentage with no base.

**Part 2: source.**

- **Met:** A named source for each number.
- **Partly:** A source for one number only.
- **Not yet:** No source.

**Part 3: your share.**

- **Met:** Names what else changed and uses a comparison, or says plainly it cannot separate them.
- **Partly:** Mentions other changes but does not use them.
- **Not yet:** Claims the whole change.

**Part 4: gap or estimate.**

- **Met:** A gap says what you would measure, and each estimate says how you got it.
- **Partly:** A gap with no plan.
- **Not yet:** An unlabelled estimate or an invented number.

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet, and at most one part is Partly.

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Where does that number come from?" or "What else changed that month?". You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks what changed because of one piece of your work, and then asks "how do you know?" about every number you give. Paste 50 to 150 words about one story from your bank, with roles instead of names. It is a good place to test whether your before, after, window and source hold up when someone asks.

Never paste your full name, phone number, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data or other people's details. If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "What changed because of your work"
opening: "Pick one piece of your work that made a difference. What changed because of it, and how do you know?"
focus: [impact, ownership]
context: project
maxTurns: 10
whyDepth: 3
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a result stated with a before, an after, a time window and a source, and how much of the change was the learner's own, with other changes named. Look for a clear line between what the learner did and what the team or the season did. Probes: if the answer gives a percentage, ask 'From what?'. After any number ask 'How do you know?' or 'Where does that number come from?'. If the answer says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'. If the answer describes habits, ask for one specific time. Ask what else changed in the same period. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, reply briefly, and ask what they would measure next. Never push the learner to guess a number or invent one. Scoring: judge features, not length. An honest gap with a plan to measure scores higher than an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words as evidence. Use these flag names when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. Tone: kind and direct, about the answer and not the person. If the evidence is thin, suggest where it might exist, or a small project around a need of their own."
```
