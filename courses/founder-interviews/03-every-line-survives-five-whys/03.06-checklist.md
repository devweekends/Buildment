## Module 3 checklist

- [ ] I can follow a why chain on a resume line and stop at a decision I made or a fact I checked
- [ ] I can tell a chain that ends at blame, at one guess or at a reason I cannot support
- [ ] I wrote what I knew and wanted at the time, and gave the reason I had, including "that was not my decision"
- [ ] I audited every line of my resume (or Hadeel's) with the five checks and rewrote the lines that failed
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 3.2 to 3.4, and finished the quiz in 3.5
- [ ] I defended one line through four whys, in writing or with the AI interviewer

## Learning log

1. **Your hardest line:** Which line on your resume was hardest to audit, and which check did it fail? Or use Hadeel's Line D and say which checks it fails.
2. **Your tidy reason:** Think of a reason you gave for a choice at work or in a project that you now think was not your reason at the time. What was the reason you had? Or use Hadeel's Line B and write her real reason for sending the reminders at 18:00.
3. **Stop points:** In your chain, which answer ended at a decision you made, and which at a fact you checked? Which answer would you most like to check again before an interview?
4. **What you kept:** Which line did you keep as it is, and why? What did you stop yourself from adding?

## Your task: audit your resume and defend one line

This task brings the module together on your own resume. You will check every line with the five questions, rewrite the two weakest lines, and then pick one line and answer four whys about it, in writing. The point is not to make your work sound bigger. It is to make sure that every line you put in front of a founder has an answer at every why.

Start with the audit. For each line, write one short answer to each of the five checks: what I did, why, what happened, how I know, what I would change. Where you do not know the answer, write "I do not know" or "I did not measure that". Those words are not a failure. They tell you what to find out, or what to say in the room. Then choose the two weakest lines and write the old line and the new line. The new line should be shorter or equal in claim, with a true verb, and with no number that you cannot source.

Then choose one line you are willing to defend. Write four whys that a founder could ask about it, and answer each one. Each answer should end at a decision you made, a fact you checked with its source, or an honest "that was not my decision" or "I did not measure that". Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Mazen is a junior developer who built an online booking form for a small gym. His resume says: "Built the online booking form for a small gym." He audits it. He built the form from a list of classes that the owner gave him, and he chose to show how many places are left in each class. He did that because the owner said members phoned the desk to ask if a class was full. The form's list showed that 38 different members booked in the first month. He did not count the phone calls before and after, so he does not know if calls fell. He would count them next time.

Mazen's four whys follow from his audit. What did you do? Built the form and chose to show places left. Why show places left? The owner said members phoned to ask. What happened? In the first month, 38 different members booked, from the form's list. What would you change? Count the phone calls before and after. Every answer is short, and every one has something behind it. Compare a weak answer to the same question: "I built a modern booking system that improved the customer experience." The founder asks which part he built, and what improved, and how he knows. The line has no fact under it, so it breaks at the first why.

If you prefer not to use your own resume, use this provided case. Hadeel is 24 and a frontend developer who applies to small remote teams. She has four lines from a six-month remote internship at a five-person team that built a clinic booking web app used by 6 small clinics. Line A: "Built the patient booking page of a clinic booking app used by 6 clinics." She built the page from the designer's screens. After she watched three patients struggle with one long form on a phone at one clinic, she proposed splitting it into three short steps, and the designer agreed. Others built the backend and the clinic dashboard. Line B: "Added text-message reminders; missed appointments fell from 18 percent to 11 percent." The founder decided to add reminders. Hadeel built them, and she chose to send them at 18:00 the day before, because the clinics said many patients missed early slots. The other developer chose the text-message service. She counted missed appointments from the clinics' booking records with that developer's help, for two months before and two months after. In the second month, two of the six clinics also began phoning patients. In the four clinics that did not phone, missed appointments fell from 18 percent to 12 percent. Line C: "Cut the booking page's load time from about 6 seconds to about 2 seconds on a low-cost phone." She loaded the page 10 times on the team's cheapest test phone on a slow connection, before and after, and reports the middle value. She made the clinic photos smaller and loaded them after the form, and she kept the clinic list in the browser for one hour. Line D: "Improved team productivity with better processes." She cannot defend this line. Audit all four lines, rewrite Line D, and defend one of A to C through four whys, using only these facts.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. Two careful people can write different honest versions of the same line. If your rewrite is different from the one your mentor would have written, that is fine, as long as every claim in it has something behind it.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audit | Every line has an answer to all five checks, and the answers say "I do not know" where that is true | Some lines or some checks are missing | Only one line is audited |
| Rewrites | Two weakest lines with old and new text; each new line uses a true verb and claims nothing it cannot source | Two rewrites, but one still has a claim without a source | No rewrite, or the new line claims more than the old one |
| Four whys | Four answers, each ending at a decision, a sourced fact, "that was not my decision" or "I did not measure that" | Three answers, or one answer is a tidy reason | The chain ends at blame, or two answers disagree |
| Real reason | At least one reason from the time, with what was and was not your decision | The reason is given but your part is not separated | No reason, or a reason that sounds invented |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions, often a "why". For example: "Where does that number come from?" or "Whose decision was the second option?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

The AI interviewer asks you one opening question about a resume line. It follows your answer with "why" and "how do you know" questions, up to the depth set for this drill. Then it moves to a second topic. At the end it gives you strengths and weaknesses, and a fix for each weakness. You choose the line to practise, so pick one you worked on in this module.

Answer as you would in a real interview. "I did not measure that" and "that was not my decision" are good answers, and the interviewer is set up to treat them that way. Its feedback is practice, not a hiring decision, and it can be wrong. Check it against your own words in the transcript, and ask your mentor if you disagree.

Privacy: do not paste your full name, phone, email, address, ID numbers, employer or client names, passwords, keys or private company data. Use roles such as "a web agency", "a logistics client" or "my team lead". Paste only the lines you are practising. If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "Your resume, line by line"
opening: "Pick the line on your resume you are proudest of. What exactly did you do?"
focus: [resume, five-whys]
context: resume
maxTurns: 12
whyDepth: 5
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a resume line the learner can follow down several whys to a decision they made or a fact they checked, with their own part separate from the team's, a number with its source or an honest gap, and the reason they had at the time. Probes: if the answer describes habits, ask for one specific time. If it says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'. After any number, ask 'Where does that number come from?'. Ask 'Why this and not another way?'. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, then ask what they would check next. Never push the learner to guess a number or invent an example. Scoring: judge features, not length. An honest gap with a plan scores higher than an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words. Use these flags by name when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk (a reason that appears only after probing and contradicts an earlier answer), jargon without explanation. Tone: kind, about the answer, not the person. If the evidence is thin, suggest how the learner could get it, for example a small project built around a need of their own."
```
