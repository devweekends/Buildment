This is the gate for module 3. Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

"Five whys" is the name of the habit, not an exact count. At this gate you check every resume line two whys deep, and you defend one line through four follow-up whys.

## Module 3 checklist

- [ ] I can follow a why chain on a resume line and stop at a decision I made or a fact I checked
- [ ] I can tell a chain that ends at blame, at one guess or at a reason I cannot support
- [ ] I wrote what I knew and wanted at the time, and gave the reason I had, including "that was not my decision"
- [ ] I audited every line of my resume (or Hadeel's) with the five checks and rewrote the lines that failed
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 3.2 to 3.4, and finished the quiz in 3.5
- [ ] I defended one line through four whys, in writing or with the AI interviewer

## Learning log

1. **Your hardest line:** Which line on your resume was hardest to audit, and which check did it fail? Or use Hadeel's Line D and say which checks it fails.
2. **Your tidy reason:** Think of a reason you gave for a choice at work or in a project that you now think was not your reason at the time. What was the reason you had? Or use Hadeel's Line B and write her real reason for sending the reminders at 18:00.
3. **Stop points:** In your chain, which answer ended at a decision you made, and which at a fact you checked? Which answer would you most like to check again before an interview? Or use Hadeel's Line A and answer for her.
4. **What you kept:** Which line did you keep as it is, and why? What did you stop yourself from adding? Or use Hadeel's Line C and say whether you would keep it as it is.

## Your task: audit your resume and defend one line

This task brings the module together on your own resume. You will check every line with the five questions, rewrite the two weakest lines, and then pick one line and answer four whys about it, in writing. The point is not to make your work sound bigger. It is to make sure that every line you put in front of a founder has an answer at every why. Use your own work. If you have no resume line for this yet, use Hadeel's material below and say so in your answer.

Start with the audit. For each line, write one short answer to each of the five checks: what I did, why, what happened, how I know, what I would change. Where you do not know the answer, write "I do not know" or "I did not measure that". Those words are not a failure. They tell you what to find out, or what to say in the room. Then choose the two weakest lines and write the old line and the new line. The new line should claim no more than the old one, with a true verb, and with no number that you cannot source.

Then choose one line you are willing to defend. Write four whys that a founder could ask about it, and answer each one. Each answer should end at a decision you made, a fact you checked with its source, or an honest "that was not my decision" or "I did not measure that". For the four whys, use the ones that fit your line: what exactly did you do, why that way, what happened and how do you know, and what would you change.

Here is a short contrast to keep in mind. Anas, from chapter 3.4, first answered a founder about his gym booking form with "I built the whole form." That failed at the third why, when she asked who chose the classes. After his audit his answer became: "I built the form from the owner's class list, and I chose to show how many places are left in each class. The owner said members phoned to ask if a class was full. I did not count the calls before and after, so I do not know if they fell." The second answer is not longer. It separates his part from the owner's, and every sentence has something behind it.

If you prefer not to use your own resume, use Hadeel's. She is the fictional fellow from chapter 1.1, 24 years old and a frontend developer who applies to small remote teams. Here is her full resume. After a six-month remote internship with a five-person team that built a clinic booking web app used by 6 small clinics, she has these lines.

**Line A:** "Built the patient booking page of a clinic booking app used by 6 clinics." She built the page from the designer's screens. On one visit to a clinic near her home, she watched three patients struggle with the one long form on their phones. She proposed splitting it into three short steps, and the designer agreed. Others built the backend and the clinic dashboard.

**Line B:** "Added text-message reminders; missed appointments fell from 18 percent to 11 percent." The founder decided to add reminders. The other developer built the text-message sending service. Hadeel built the reminder rule and the setting screen, and she chose to send the reminder at 18:00 the day before, because the clinics said many patients missed early slots. She counted missed appointments from the clinics' booking records, with that developer's help, for two months before and two months after. In the second month, two of the six clinics also began phoning patients. In the four clinics that did not phone, missed appointments fell from 18 percent to 12 percent.

**Line C:** "Cut the booking page's load time from about 6 seconds to about 2 seconds on a low-cost phone." She loaded the page 10 times on the team's cheapest test phone on a slow connection, before and after. Each time she put the ten times in order and reports the middle one. She made the clinic photos smaller and loaded them after the form, and she kept the clinic list in the browser for one hour.

**Line D:** "Improved team productivity with better processes." She cannot defend this line.

**Freelance, four months:** "Built an online order form and a stock sheet for a family bakery." She did not count orders. The owner said phone calls at busy times dropped, which is the owner's words and not a number.

**Volunteer:** "Taught basic spreadsheet skills to 12 students at a community centre, in 8 weekly sessions."

Audit all the lines, rewrite Line D, and defend one of A to C through four whys, using only these facts.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer part by part. Two careful people can write different honest versions of the same line. If your rewrite is different from the one your mentor would have written, that is fine, as long as every claim in it has something behind it.

**Part 1: audit.**

- Met: every line has an answer to all five checks, and the answers say "I do not know" where that is true.
- Partly: some lines or some checks are missing.
- Not yet: only one line is audited.

**Part 2: rewrites.**

- Met: the two weakest lines have old and new text; each new line uses a true verb and claims nothing it cannot source.
- Partly: two rewrites, but one still has a claim without a source.
- Not yet: no rewrite, or the new line claims more than the old one.

**Part 3: four whys.**

- Met: four answers, each ending at a decision, a sourced fact, "that was not my decision" or "I did not measure that".
- Partly: three answers, or one answer is a tidy reason.
- Not yet: the chain ends at blame, or two answers disagree.

**Part 4: real reason.**

- Met: at least one reason from the time, with what was and was not your decision.
- Partly: the reason is given but your part is not separated.
- Not yet: no reason, or a reason that sounds invented.

**Pass rule:** no part is "Not yet", and at most one part is "Partly".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions, often a "why". For example: "Where does that number come from?" or "Whose decision was the second option?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.

## Practice with the AI interviewer

This drill asks about one resume line and follows it down up to five whys, one more than the gate asks for, then moves to a second line. Paste your resume lines with names, contact details and client names removed. Use roles such as "a web agency" or "my team lead". Do not paste keys, passwords, ID numbers or private company data. If you do not have a key, the written task below completes this gate.

```aiinterview
title: "Your resume, line by line"
opening: "Pick the line on your resume you are proudest of. What exactly did you do?"
focus: [resume, five-whys]
context: resume
maxTurns: 12
whyDepth: 5
required: false
rubric: "A strong interview shows a resume line the learner can follow down several whys to a decision they made or a fact they checked, with their own part separate from the team's, a number with its source or an honest gap, and the reason they had at the time. Probes: if the answer describes habits, ask for one specific time. If it says 'we', ask 'What was your part?'. After any number, ask 'Where does that number come from?'. Ask 'Why this and not another way?'. Stop rules: accept 'I did not measure that', 'I don't know' and 'that was not my decision' as full answers, then ask what they would check next. Never push the learner to guess a number or invent an example. Scoring: judge features, not length. An honest gap with a plan scores higher than an unsourced number. Quote the learner's words. Use these flags by name when they apply: vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk (a reason that appears only after probing and contradicts an earlier answer), jargon without explanation. Tone: kind, about the answer, not the person. If the evidence is thin, suggest how the learner could get it, for example a small project built around a need of their own."
```
