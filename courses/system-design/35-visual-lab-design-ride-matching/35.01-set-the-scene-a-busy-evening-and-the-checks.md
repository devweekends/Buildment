You open a ride app and little cars slide around a map. You tap Request, and a driver nearby turns toward you. Behind that tap, thousands of phones are telling the service where they are, again and again, and one of them has just gone through a tunnel. What should the service do when one of its own machines stops at that moment? Keep that question in mind. In this module you build the service on a board, and then the board breaks it for you.

Safar (the made-up ride service from module 24) is in its first city on a busy evening. About 16,000 drivers are online, and each phone reports its place every 4 seconds. That is 4,000 reports a second, plus the riders searching and a few asking for a ride. It is far below the 250,000 a second of chapter 24.1, and it is the size where one wrong choice already shows.

Before you read on, make a guess. A shop has one till. When it is half busy, a customer waits about a minute. When it is 90 percent busy, how long do you think a customer waits? Choose, and keep your guess in mind. The board will show you the answer with real numbers.

## Three jobs a ride service does

<MermaidDiagram title="What Safar must do" chart={`flowchart LR
  D["Driver phone"] -->|"1. report my place"| S["Safar"]
  R["Rider"] -->|"2. who is near me?"| S
  S -->|"nearby cars"| R
  R -->|"3. request a ride"| S
  S -.->|"save the trip"| T[("Trips")]`} />

Safar must **store and search places** (keep each driver's latest place, and find the drivers near a rider), **save a trip** when a ride is requested, and **record trip events** as a trip happens. These are the **functional** requirements: what the system does. On the board, each is a check that asks whether a request can travel from the client to the part that does the job.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the three checks look at">

The board does not run your code, because there is none. It looks at the links you drew. "Drivers' places are stored and searched" is true when a request can travel from a client, through an app server, to a cache. In this lab the cache is the location map of chapter 24.3: a store in memory that holds only each driver's latest place. A place report and a nearby search are the same kind of request on the board, so one check covers both.

"A ride request becomes a saved trip" is true when a request can travel from a client, through an app server, to a database. A trip is money and a promise to two people, so unlike a place, it goes in a store that keeps it (chapter 24.3).

"Trip events are recorded" is true when an event can travel from the app server to a queue, from the queue to a worker, and from the worker to the database. A trip event is what happens as a ride is requested, offered to a driver and accepted. Only a few requests make one: chapter 24.1 puts ride requests at about 580 a second against about 290,000 reports and searches, 0.2%, and chapter 24.4 says 7 offers in 10 are accepted, so a ride needs about 1.4 offers. Counting a request, each offer and the acceptance gives about 2.4 events a ride (a course assumption), so the lab uses 1 request in 200. That is 20 events a second at the busiest moment. They are worth keeping, but they must not slow a request, which is what the queue and the worker are for (chapter 20.4). The location reports are not trip events: the map keeps only the latest place.

If a design cannot do one of the three jobs, nothing else about it matters, so these checks come first.

</GoDeeper>

## Eight ways to be good, or not

<MermaidDiagram title="One design, four scenes" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["1. Peak traffic<br>4,000 requests a second"] --> B["2. An app server dies"]
  B --> C["3. A node of the map dies"]
  C --> D["4. The trip worker dies"]`} />

A design that does the three jobs can still be slow, fall over when a machine dies, or cost too much. These are the **non-functional** requirements: how well the system does its jobs. The board measures eight of them, in four scenes, when you press Run.

| In this scene | The board measures | What a good design shows |
|---|---|---|
| Peak traffic | capacity, the slowest 1 in 100 requests (p99), staying up (availability), cost | all 4,000 a second served, p99 under 72 ms, under a budget per hour |
| An app server dies | the share of requests still served | everyone is still served with one server fewer |
| A node of the map dies | the share served, and p99 | everyone is still served, and p99 stays under 100 ms |
| The trip worker dies | events lost, and how long to catch up | no event is lost, and the backlog drains within 30 seconds |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading the table, and what to do before you open the board">

The slowest 1 in 100 requests (**p99**) is the time that 99 out of 100 requests beat. It is the wait of the unlucky one in a hundred, and chapter 2.4 showed why that number matters more than the average. A **cache** is a fast store, here kept in memory. A **node** is one machine of a group that works together, so a map of two nodes is two machines each holding part of the drivers.

When you press Run, the board plays all four scenes in order. In scene 2 one app server turns red. In scene 3 one node of the location map goes dark. If the map was a single node, nothing is left to answer from, and the database fills up. In scene 4 the worker stops for a minute, and trip events wait in the queue. After the last scene, every check is listed as pass or fail, with the number the board measured, the number it needed, and the reason when it failed.

Before you open the board, look at the table and ask yourself one thing: which of these eight do you think a first, simple design would fail? There is no wrong guess. Writing it down makes the result more interesting.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the numbers come from, and how far to trust them">

The board is a model, not a city. Each part has a capacity that comes from its size and count, and a request waits longer as a part gets busier (the point where waiting climbs, called the utilisation knee in chapter 2.5). Prices are illustrative. Latency is in model milliseconds, so compare one design with another on the board and do not compare the board with a stopwatch.

The load is chosen, not measured. Safar is made up, and so are its 16,000 drivers and the 4-second report gap, which come from the method of chapter 24.1. The targets were tuned on the model so that a good design passes with a little room, and a design with one missing idea fails for one clear reason. The lab spec records every number and how it was tuned. There is no code lab for this design yet, so none of the numbers has been checked against a real stack. It is coming.

</GoDeeper>

  - "Storing places is the job, and the design does it. What fails is staying up when a part dies, which is a quality of the service, not a feature."
  - "A crash is exactly the kind of event a design must expect. Surviving one is a requirement the board tests on purpose."
  - "The app server answers requests, but the places live in the cache. Where data is kept is a design choice, not a kind of requirement."
