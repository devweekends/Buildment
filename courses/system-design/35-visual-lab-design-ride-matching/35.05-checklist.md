## Module 35 checklist

- [ ] I can say which of the board's checks are functional and which are non-functional, and why a design can pass the first kind and fail the second
- [ ] I can follow a driver's place report and a rider's search through the parts of my design: load balancer, app server, location map, database, and the queue and worker for the events
- [ ] I ran the starting design before changing it, and I can say which checks it failed and which part each failure named
- [ ] I can explain why a map of one node can take down a database that was nearly idle a moment before, and why a second node fixes it
- [ ] I can work out how many trip events a second reach the queue from the share of requests that leave one, and how long one worker takes to clear the events that waited while it was away
- [ ] I can say why a side job that few requests need (1 in 200) is cheap to move off the request path, and what would change if every request made one
- [ ] I can name what a queue gives a request that would otherwise save its own event: no waiting, and no loss while the worker is away, and say what the queue costs
- [ ] My design passes all eleven checks on the lab board, or I can say which check I chose to fail and why
- [ ] I made my guess in chapter 35.1 before my first run, and I wrote what I found in the reflection below
- [ ] I submitted the defence below

## Learning log

1. **Your first run:** Which checks did the starting design fail, and which one surprised you most?
2. **Your busiest part:** In your final design, which part is the busiest at peak, and how busy is it? What would you raise next, and what would it cost?
3. **Your location map:** What does your design do when a node of the map dies? Which part of it makes that survivable?
4. **Your events:** About 1 request in 200 leaves a trip event. What did your queue and worker cost an hour, and what would you do if every request left an event?
5. **Your crowded box:** The board spreads load evenly, so it cannot show a stadium after a match. What would you change in your design to survive one, and what would it cost?
6. **Your first guess:** In chapter 35.1 you guessed how the wait changes when a till goes from half busy to 90 percent busy. Was your guess right, and where did you see the same thing on the board?

## Your gate answer: defend your design

This is the lab again, in words. You built a design on the board and it told you, check by check, where it was weak. A design review in a real team works the same way. Someone shows a design, someone else asks what happens when a part fails, and the designer answers with a number, not a feeling. Write the answer you would give in that room, about 200 words, about the design that passed or came closest.

A weak answer lists the parts: a load balancer, three app servers, a location map, a database, a queue and a worker. It reads like a shopping list, and it could describe any design. A strong answer starts from a number the board showed you and lets that number decide the parts. Say which check was the hardest to pass, what you changed to pass it, and the number before and after. For example, the database at 168% busy in the map-node scene, and 9% after you gave the map a second node.

Then pick one trade-off and write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a second map node at $0.15 an hour, so the map survives losing one", or "one small worker for the 20 trip events a second, which clears a minute of waiting events in about a second". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good "when would you change it" line also has a trigger, such as "if the peak doubles" or "if one box gets most of the traffic".

End with the failure your design is built to survive, and the one it is not. Every design has one. Saying so is not a weakness. It is the part of the answer that shows you looked. The board cannot show a crowded box, so say what you would do for a stadium, or say that you did not plan for one and what it would cost to do so.

A good order for 200 words is the order of the board. Start with the hardest check and the number that moved. Then the trade-off. Last, the failure you did not fix. If you run short of words, cut the list of parts, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is tuning until the board is green without reading the reasons, and ending with every part at the largest size, which passes everything and fails on cost. The second is treating the map as a copy of the data. The map holds only the latest places and has no database behind it, so a lost node comes back empty and refills from the drivers, and the board's second node shows only that the others carry on. The third is reading the worker check as a sizing puzzle. With 1 request in 200 leaving a trip event, one small worker is plenty, and the check is about having a queue and a worker at all.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the choice to a check, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to the events if the queue itself fails, or what you would change if every location report were kept in a history log. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
