## Module 30 checklist

- [ ] I can say which of the board's checks are functional and which are non-functional, and why a design can pass the first kind and fail the second
- [ ] I can follow a short link through the parts of my design: load balancer, app server, cache, database, and the queue and worker for the click
- [ ] I ran the starting design before changing it, and I can say which checks it failed and which part each failure named
- [ ] I can explain why losing the cache can take down a database that was nearly idle a moment before
- [ ] I can compute how many workers a backlog needs, from the arrival rate, a worker's capacity and the time allowed to catch up
- [ ] I can say what a batch size changes for the database, and what it costs
- [ ] I can name the difference between counting a click inline and counting it through a queue, with the price of each
- [ ] My design passes all eleven checks on the lab board, or I can say which check I chose to fail and why
- [ ] I submitted the defence below

## Learning log

1. **Your first run:** Which checks did the starting design fail, and which one surprised you most?
2. **Your busiest part:** In your final design, which part is the busiest at peak, and how busy is it? What would you raise next, and what would it cost?
3. **Your cache:** What does your design do when the cache dies? Which part of it makes that survivable?
4. **Your clicks:** Did you count clicks inline or through a queue? What did you pay for that choice?
5. **Your shared failure:** In the real stack one Redis was both the cache and the queue. Is there anything in your design that two parts share? Name it, or say why there is none.

## Your gate answer: defend your design

This is the lab again, in words. You built a design on the board and it told you, check by check, where it was weak. A design review in a real team works the same way. Someone shows a design, someone else asks what happens when a part fails, and the designer answers with a number, not a feeling. Write the answer you would give in that room, about 200 words, about the design that passed or came closest.

A weak answer lists the parts: a load balancer, three app servers, a cache, a database, a queue and a worker. It reads like a shopping list, and it could describe any design. A strong answer starts from a number the board showed you and lets that number decide the parts. Say which check was the hardest to pass, what you changed to pass it, and the number before and after. For example, the database at 124% busy in the cache scene, and 62% after you added a replica.

Then pick one trade-off and write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a read replica at $0.30 an hour, so the database survives the cache dying", or "two workers at batch 500, which leave 1,800 clicks a second to spare for a backlog". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good "when would you change it" line also has a trigger, such as "if the peak doubles" or "if the cache gets its own replica".

End with the failure your design is built to survive, and the one it is not. Every design has one. Saying so is not a weakness. It is the part of the answer that shows you looked. If you shared a Redis between the cache and the queue, or put every part in one zone, say so, and say what you would pay to remove it.

A good order for 200 words is the order of the board. Start with the hardest check and the number that moved. Then the trade-off. Last, the failure you did not fix. If you run short of words, cut the list of parts, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is tuning until the board is green without reading the reasons, and ending with every part at the largest size, which passes everything and fails on cost. The second is treating the cache as redundancy. A cache makes reads fast, but it is not a copy that keeps the service up, and the cache scene is there to show it. The third is sizing the worker for the arrival rate and nothing more, so a backlog, once it exists, can never be cleared.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the choice to a check, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to the clicks if the queue itself fails, or why you chose batch 500 and not 50. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
