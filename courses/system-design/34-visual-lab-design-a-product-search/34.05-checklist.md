## Learning log

1. **Your first run:** Which checks did the starting design fail, and which one surprised you most?
2. **Your busiest part:** In your final design, which part is the busiest at peak, and how busy is it? What would you raise next, and what would it cost?
3. **Your search index:** How many search nodes did you use, of what size, and how busy are they at peak and after one dies? What did that choice cost per hour?
4. **Your changes:** How did a seller's change reach the index in your design? How long does it take to clear 5 minutes of waiting changes, and what makes that number smaller?
5. **What the board hid:** The board cannot rank results or show two copies of the index disagreeing. Which of the two would hurt Dalil more, and how would you test for it?
6. **Your first guess:** In chapter 34.1 you guessed how the wait changes when a till goes from half busy to 90 percent busy. Was your guess right, and where did you see the same thing on the board?

## Your gate answer: defend your design

This is the lab again, in words. You built a design on the board and it told you, check by check, where it was weak. A design review in a real team works the same way. Someone shows a design, someone else asks what happens when a part fails, and the designer answers with a number, not a feeling. Write the answer you would give in that room, about 200 words, about the design that passed or came closest.

A weak answer lists the parts: a load balancer, three app servers, a cache, eight search nodes, a database, a queue and a worker. It reads like a shopping list, and it could describe any design. A strong answer starts from a number the board showed you and lets that number decide the parts. Say which check was the hardest to pass, what you changed to pass it, and the number before and after. For example, the search index at 500% busy in the starting design, and 64% after you gave it eight small nodes.

Then pick one trade-off and write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "eight small search nodes instead of four medium ones at the same $1.20 an hour, so a lost node leaves seven at 73% busy and not three at 85%", or "one worker, which leaves 2,325 changes a second to spare to clear a build-up of waiting changes". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good "when would you change it" line also has a trigger, such as "if the peak doubles" or "if a big seller starts uploading thousands of changes at once".

End with the failure your design is built to survive, and the one it is not. Every design has one. Saying so is not a weakness. It is the part of the answer that shows you looked. If you could not check ranking quality, or two copies of the index showing different prices for a few seconds, or you put every part in one zone, say so, and say what you would pay to remove it.

A good order for 200 words is the order of the board. Start with the hardest check and the number that moved. Then the trade-off. Last, the failure you did not fix. If you run short of words, cut the list of parts, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is tuning until the board is green without reading the reasons, and ending with every part at the largest size, which passes everything and fails on cost. The second is reading a lower cost or a lower p99 as a win without checking the functional checks first: a design with no queue is cheaper and faster because it never updates the index. The third is sizing the worker for the arrival rate and nothing more, so a build-up of waiting changes, once it exists, can never be cleared in time.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the choice to a check, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to the index if the queue itself fails, or why you chose eight nodes and not six. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
