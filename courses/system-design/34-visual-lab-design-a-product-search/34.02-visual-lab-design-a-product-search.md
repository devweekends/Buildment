This page is the board and nothing else. Chapter 34.1 listed the twelve checks. Here you build a design, press Run, and watch it meet them. The board starts with the smallest design that works: one load balancer (it spreads requests over the servers), one app server (it runs the code), one search node (a machine that holds the index) and one database. It already returns products. It will not pass much more than that, and finding out why is the lab.

There is no single right answer. Many designs pass, and one that passes at $2.93 an hour beats one that passes at $3. Aim for green first, then make it cheaper without turning anything red.

## How the board works

<MermaidDiagram title="The board, in four moves" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Palette<br>on the left"] -->|"drag or tap"| B["Board<br>link the dots"]
  B -->|"hover a part, click Tune"| S["Settings cassette<br>beside the part"]
  B -->|"press Run"| R["Four scenes,<br>then every check"]`} />

The palette is on the left: drag a part onto the board, or tap it. Link two parts by dragging from a dot on one to a dot on another. Settings are not on the page until you ask: hover a part and click Tune on its corner, and a dark cassette opens beside it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cassette, names, and what Run shows">

The board only allows links where requests really flow, and it tells you when a link is not allowed. The settings drawer holds the part's size, how many there are, which zones they live in, and any setting special to that part, such as a cache's hit rate or the number of search nodes. Only one cassette is open at a time. On a phone it docks along the bottom while the part moves into view above it, and you tap a part to show its Tune key. Press Escape or the close key to put it away. The Names switch in the header changes the parts to real AWS names and sizes, such as Amazon OpenSearch Service and m5.large.search, with a line on what each one means. The numbers do not change.

Press Run and the board plays four scenes. Dots travel along the links, a part turns amber and then red as it fills, and a part that dies is marked DOWN. When the scenes end, each check shows pass or fail, the number the board measured, the number it needed, and why a failed check failed. Press Show on any check to replay its scene on the board.

</GoDeeper>

## Build it on the board

<Lab slug="hld-visual-product-search" />

Run the starting design first, before you change anything. Read the failures in order. Each one names a part and a number, and each number points at a setting.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a failure">

A failure has three lines. The first says what was measured and what was needed. The second says why, usually naming the busiest part and how busy it was. For example, "Search is 333% busy, so it cannot serve the whole load" says that more lookups arrive than that part can answer, and every request behind it waits. Fix the part named, run again, and a different part is usually the next one named. That is the loop of chapter 4.9: the busiest part on the path sets the ceiling, and you move the ceiling by raising one part at a time.

Two failures deserve a closer look. "Everyone is still served when an app server dies" runs the scene with one app server fewer. A fleet of one has nothing left, so it fails at once. And "No product change is lost when the indexing worker dies" needs the changes to wait somewhere while the worker is down, which is what a queue is for, and then needs the workers to be fast enough, once back, to clear the waiting changes (the backlog) as well as the new ones.

</GoDeeper>

## Put a cache in front

<MermaidDiagram title="Where 2,000 lookups a second go" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers<br>2,000 req/s"] --> C["Cache<br>90% hits"]
  APP -->|"misses: about 200 req/s"| DB[("Product database")]
  APP --> S["Search index"]`} />

Link a cache from the app server and set its hit rate. Suggestions are served from the cache, so the suggestion check needs one. A hit never reaches the database, only misses go on. At a 90% hit rate the database sees about 200 requests a second instead of 2,000.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the cache does and does not protect">

Each link on the board shows its own numbers: lookups going to the cache with the share it answers, and the misses going on to the database. A cache hit means the database is never asked. You may also link the cache onward to the database, so the cache loads from it on a miss. The database gets the same load either way.

In this model a cache can be followed only by a database, so it cannot shield the search index. In real Dalil a cache of popular searches would take load off the index too. The board does not show that, and chapter 34.3 comes back to it. What the board does show is the other job of the cache: it keeps 1,800 of 2,000 lookups away from a database that could serve only about 1,200 a second.

</GoDeeper>

## Indexing and searching share the nodes

<MermaidDiagram title="One index, two kinds of work" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App server"] -->|"2,000 lookups/s"| S["Search nodes<br>4,000/s in all"]
  APP -->|"2,000 changes/s"| Q["Queue"] --> W["Workers"] -->|"2,000 writes/s"| S`} />

Add a queue and workers, and the search nodes get busier. They answer lookups and also take the changes. Raise the node count until capacity and p99 pass. Then compare cost: the index is the biggest line on the bill.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the index is the busiest part">

A search node answers about 300 lookups a second for each vCPU, which is far fewer than an app server does, because a lookup reads and combines lists (chapter 23.2). So a memory-small node (2 vCPU) handles 600 a second. To carry 4,000 a second with room to spare you need about ten of them. That is the point of chapter 23.2: shards cut the size of an index, and copies cut the load. On the board the node count is the number of copies.

A design with no queue and no worker looks cheaper and faster, because it never updates the index and the nodes carry only the lookups. Look at its cost and p99, then look at which check it fails. A cheap design that skips a job is not a good design.

</GoDeeper>

## Many small nodes or a few big

<MermaidDiagram title="The same cost, two ways to lose one" chart={`flowchart LR
  subgraph small["10 small nodes"]
    A["Lose 1 of 10<br>9 left, 74% busy"]
  end
  subgraph big["5 medium nodes"]
    B["Lose 1 of 5<br>4 left, 83% busy"]
  end`} />

Ten small search nodes and five medium ones cost the same and carry the same load. They differ when one dies. Losing one of ten leaves nine, each a little busier. Losing one of five leaves four, each much busier, and the slowest 1 in 100 grows.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers for each way to lose a node">

With ten small nodes, the index is 67% busy at the busiest moment and 74% busy after a loss, and the slowest 1 in 100 takes 146 ms. With five medium nodes it is the same 67% at peak but 83% after a loss, and the slowest 1 in 100 takes 200 ms, over the 160 ms the check allows. The queueing knee of chapter 2.5 explains why: waiting grows faster than busyness, so a part that moves from 67% to 83% pays far more than a part that moves to 74%.

Smaller parts also make the spare machine cheaper. One spare among ten is a tenth of the fleet, and one spare among five is a fifth. The price per core is the same for every size on the board, so the only difference is how much one failure costs you. Do not take this as a rule that small is always better: many small nodes mean more to run and to keep in step. The board does not charge for that.

</GoDeeper>

## Make it cheaper

<MermaidDiagram title="Pass first, then trim" chart={`flowchart LR
  G["All checks green"] --> T["Trim the part with spare room"]
  T --> R["Run again"]
  R -->|"a check turns red"| U["Undo that trim"]
  R -->|"still green"| T`} />

When every check is green, look at the cost. Find the part with the most spare room, shrink it one step, and run again. If a check turns red, undo that step. The budget is $3 an hour, and a design that passes costs a little less. One extra instance or one size too large fails on cost alone.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: stuck? One design that passes">

One design that passes all twelve checks: a load balancer of size general-small in two zones. Two app servers of size compute-small in two zones. One cache node of size memory-small at a 90% hit rate in two zones. Ten search nodes of size memory-small in two zones. A product database of size memory-small, set to 95% reads, with an extra copy that takes over (a failover standby). A queue, and two workers of size compute-small. The app server links to the cache, the search index, the database and the queue. The queue links to the workers. The workers link to the search index.

The board reports a ceiling of 3,000 requests a second, p99 near 125 ms, availability near 99.97% and about $2.93 an hour. The two checks that depend most on one choice are the search-node checks, which need the tenth node, and the worker check, which needs the second worker. Try taking each away, and read what the board says.

</GoDeeper>
