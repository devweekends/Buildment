A run is a small experiment, and every red check is a result. This chapter reads the scenes one at a time. For each, it says what the board did, what the numbers mean, and what to change. All numbers come from the board's model and are in model milliseconds. There is no code lab for Dalil yet, so nothing here was measured on a real stack. Read the numbers as shapes.

## Scene 2: an app server dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of three app servers" chart={`flowchart LR
  LB["Load balancer"] --> A1["App server<br>42% busy"]
  LB --> A2["App server<br>42% busy"]
  LB -.->|"stopped"| A3["App server"]
  A1 -.->|"now 63% busy"| X["Everyone still served"]`} />

The reference design has three app servers, each 42% busy at the busiest moment. When one stops, the other two are 63% busy. Everyone is still served, and the slowest 1 in 100 grows from 119 to 138 ms. With only two servers, a loss leaves one at 125% busy, and only 80% are served.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 2">

One app server handles about 2,400 requests a second (2 vCPU of the compute family, 1,200 a second for each). Three servers share 3,000 a second, so each carries 1,000, which is 42% of 2,400. After a loss the two survivors carry 1,500 each, which is 63%. With two servers to start with, the survivor must carry 3,000 against a capacity of 2,400. That is 125%, so 2,400 of 3,000, or 80%, are served, and the check fails.

The check looks only at the share served. It does not look at how slow it is. The board shows 138 ms in the app-server scene, above the 119 ms of a healthy peak, and nothing turns red. A fourth app server would keep p99 lower after a loss, at $0.12 an hour more, which puts the design at $2.57 and over its $2.50 budget. That is a real trade: you can pay for a fast failure or accept a slower one. Say which you chose.

The lesson is the one spare machine rule (N+1) from chapter 4.2. The third server is not waste. It is the part that makes the death survivable.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 3: a search node dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Seven nodes carry what eight carried" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers"] -->|"3,075/s in all"| S["Search index<br>8 nodes, 64% busy"]
  S -.->|"one node dies"| S9["7 nodes, 73% busy<br>p99 143 ms"]`} />

The index carries the 3,000 lookups and the few product changes, 3,075 a second. With eight small nodes it is 64% busy. After one dies, seven nodes are 73% busy and the slowest 1 in 100 takes 143 ms, inside the 160 ms the check allows. A thinner index fails: seven nodes to start with are already 73% busy, and a loss takes them to 85% and 221 ms.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 3, and shards versus copies">

Each small search node handles 600 lookups a second, because a search node does about 300 for each vCPU. Eight nodes give 4,800 a second against a load of 3,075, which is 64%. After a loss, seven nodes give 4,200, which is 73%. With six nodes the index is already 85% busy at peak, and a loss leaves five at 102%, so only 97.5% of requests are served and the slowest 1 in 100 takes 1,716 ms.

Chapter 23.2 splits an index into shards, which cut its size, and copies, which cut its load. The board shows only the copies: the node count is the number of copies that share the work. That is why adding load means adding nodes. It is also why the board prices the index above everything else. Chapter 23.2 planned 56 nodes for the full 7,000 searches a second. The lab uses a smaller slice, so eight is enough.

Four bigger nodes (medium) cost the same as eight small ones and pass at peak. After one dies, three nodes carry the load at 85% busy and p99 reaches 221 ms, over the 160 ms target. One failure costs a quarter of the fleet instead of an eighth. The queueing knee of chapter 2.5 does the rest.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 4: the indexing worker dies

<MermaidDiagram title="The queue fills while the worker is away" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App server"] -->|"75 changes/s"| Q["Queue<br>backlog grows"]
  Q -.->|"stopped"| W["Worker"]
  W --> S["Search index"]`} />

Searches do not notice a dead worker. The queue keeps each change. About 1 lookup in 40 leaves a change, so 75 a second wait. After 5 minutes the queue holds 22,500, and the worker must clear them as well as the new ones. One worker has 2,325 a second to spare and needs 10 seconds, well inside the 30 seconds Dalil promised sellers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 4">

A worker of the board's default size handles 2,400 changes a second. The new changes need 75 of that, so 2,325 a second are spare, and 22,500 divided by 2,325 is about 10 seconds. A second worker would clear them in 5 seconds and cost $0.12 an hour more, which is over budget. The same arithmetic as the short-link service of chapter 30.3 applies, but here the arrival rate is small, so the worker is not the hard part.

That would change if changes were not rare. A seller who uploads a new price for every product at once could send thousands of changes a second. If 3,000 a second arrived, one worker with a capacity of 2,400 could never catch up. The share of 1 in 40 is the ratio of chapter 23.1, a peak ratio from the course's own assumptions. The board does not show a burst.

The 30 seconds is the freshness allowance of chapter 23.1: a product change must be searchable within 30 seconds. In a real index there is one more delay, the refresh interval of chapter 23.2, because changes become searchable only when the next small segment is made. The board does not model that, so a design that passes here still has to leave room for it.

</GoDeeper>

## A cheap design that skips a job

<MermaidDiagram title="Why no queue looks almost free" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Design with a queue<br>$2.45/h, p99 119 ms"] --> B["Changes reach the index"]
  C["Design with no queue<br>$2.31/h, p99 116 ms"] --> D["Changes never arrive"]`} />

Take the queue and the worker away and the bill falls by $0.14 an hour to $2.31, and p99 is 116 ms. It looks almost the same. It is worse: the index no longer takes any changes, so the change check and the worker check both fail. Cheaper and faster is only a win when the same checks still pass.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a red check against a green number">

The board reports many numbers at once, and some of them improve when a job is skipped. The saving is small here because changes are rare, which makes the trap easier to fall into: the numbers hardly move. A system that drops a hard requirement often shows lower cost and lower latency, because it is doing less. The only protection is to read the functional checks first. If a path fails, no measurement downstream is worth reading.

The same trap appears with the cache, but it shows up more loudly. A design with no cache fails the suggestion check at once, and the database is 238% busy, so its capacity falls to 1,263 a second and p99 reaches 583 ms. The first thing the board says is the path check, not the number.

</GoDeeper>

## Solution walkthrough

<MermaidDiagram title="From a failing run to the reference design" chart={`flowchart LR
  R["Run once"] --> S["Solution"]
  S --> P["Pick a part:<br>why, checks, without it"]
  S --> C["Compare with mine"]
  S --> K["Copy to my board"]`} />

After you press Run once, the Solution button opens the reference design on the same board. Pick any part to see why it is there, which checks it helps pass, and what fails without it. Compare with mine lists what differs from your design. Try first: a failing run you can explain teaches the most.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to use the solution well">

The solution is never locked behind a score, and nothing is recorded when you open it. Your own board stays untouched while you look: Back to my design returns to it, and Copy to my board asks before it replaces anything. The link labels in the solution show each hop on its own: lookups going to the cache with the share the cache answers, the misses going on to the database, and the share of requests that send a change to the queue.

A good way to use it is to compare after one more try. Fix the failure you understand, run again, and only then press Compare with mine. It lists parts, settings and links that differ, in words such as "lower" and "smaller", and names the failing checks each one helps explain. It does not print the reference's numbers.

A code lab for the same design is coming. It will run a stack with the same parts, where you stop each one yourself, and the names of the parts and the checks will match so that a design you built on the board can be replayed there.

</GoDeeper>

## What the board cannot show

<MermaidDiagram title="Two failures with no red check" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Product change<br>saved in the database"] -->|"queue, worker"| I1["Index copy 1<br>refreshed"]
  P -->|"queue, worker"| I2["Index copy 2<br>not yet"]
  I1 --> U1["Shopper A sees the new price"]
  I2 --> U2["Shopper B sees the old price"]`} />

The board checks that the index answers and takes changes. It never asks whether the answers are good, or whether every copy agrees. It has no ranking, so a design can return the wrong product first and pass. It has no refresh, so two shoppers can see different prices and pass. What would you check next?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: ranking quality and stale results, and what else the board hides">

**Ranking quality.** Chapter 23.2 scored products with tf-idf and added business signals such as sales and ratings. The board treats the index as a box that answers. A bad ranking costs sales and trust, yet no part on the board can fix it, and no check can see it. Quality would need a set of test queries with known good answers, scored on every change to the ranking. That is a separate piece of work from capacity.

**Stale results.** Each copy of an index refreshes on its own timer (chapter 23.2), so for a few seconds two shoppers can see different results. A seller who saves a change and searches at once may not see it. The board counts only how quickly the queue empties.

**Other limits.** A lookup on the board goes to both the cache and the index, while in real Dalil a suggestion never reaches the index and a cache of popular searches would take load off it. The board has one pool of equal search nodes, not eight shards with copies, so it cannot show the slow-shard tail of chapter 23.2. Its share of changes is a single peak ratio, 1 in 40, so it cannot show a burst from a seller who changes thousands of products at once. And there is no hot key: the letter s, asked by thousands of shoppers a second (chapter 23.3), is no busier than any other.

Each of these is a reason to run the design for real, with a code lab, and to watch what people see.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: in an interview">

**In an interview.** Walk the failures in order. Say what you lose when each part stops, and what each choice costs. "If an app server dies, the other two carry on at 63% busy, because I kept one spare machine and the servers hold no state. If a search node dies, the other seven carry its share at 73%, because I used eight small nodes. With four bigger nodes the survivors would be at 85% and p99 would be over the target. If the indexing worker dies, changes wait in the queue and clear in 10 seconds after a 5 minute outage, because changes are rare and one worker has room. Two things my board cannot show are ranking quality and stale results from copies that refresh at different times, so I would test them separately."

Interviewers like the last sentence most. They want to hear that you know what your measurements leave out.

</GoDeeper>
