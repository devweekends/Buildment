A run is a small experiment, and every red check is a result. This chapter reads the scenes one at a time. For each, it says what the board did, what the numbers mean, and what to change. All numbers come from the board's model and are in model milliseconds. There is no code lab for Dalil yet, so nothing here was measured on a real stack. Read the numbers as shapes.

## Scene 2: an app server dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of two app servers" chart={`flowchart LR
  LB["Load balancer"] --> A1["App server<br>42% busy"]
  LB -.->|"stopped"| A2["App server"]
  A1 -.->|"now 83% busy"| X["Everyone still served"]`} />

The reference design has two app servers, each 42% busy at the busiest moment. When one stops, the other takes everything and is 83% busy. Everyone is still served, and the slowest 1 in 100 grows from 125 to 210 ms. With one server, nothing is left and nobody is served.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 2">

One app server handles about 2,400 requests a second (2 vCPU of the compute family, 1,200 a second for each). Two servers share 2,000 a second, so each carries 1,000, which is 42% of 2,400. After a loss the survivor carries 2,000, which is 83%. Under 100% means everyone is served, so the check passes.

The check looks only at the share served. It does not look at how slow it is. The board shows 210 ms in the app-server scene, well over the 125 ms of a healthy peak, and nothing turns red. A third app server would leave two survivors at 42% each and p99 near 125 ms after a loss, at $0.12 an hour more, which puts the design at $3.05 and over its $3.00 budget. That is a real trade: you can pay for a fast failure or accept a slow one. Say which you chose.

The lesson is the one spare machine rule (N+1) from chapter 4.2. The extra server is not waste. It is the part that makes the death survivable.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 3: a search node dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Nine nodes carry what ten carried" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers"] -->|"4,000/s in all"| S["Search index<br>10 nodes, 67% busy"]
  S -.->|"one node dies"| S9["9 nodes, 74% busy<br>p99 146 ms"]`} />

The index carries the lookups and the product changes together, 4,000 a second. With ten small nodes it is 67% busy. After one dies, nine nodes are 74% busy and the slowest 1 in 100 takes 146 ms, inside the 160 ms the check allows. A thinner index fails: nine nodes to start with are already 74% busy, and a loss takes them to 83% and 200 ms.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 3, and shards versus copies">

Each small search node handles 600 lookups a second, because a search node does about 300 for each vCPU. Ten nodes give 6,000 a second against a load of 4,000, which is 67%. After a loss, nine nodes give 5,400, which is 74%. With seven nodes the index is already 95% busy at peak and the slowest 1 in 100 takes 575 ms. A loss then leaves six nodes at 111%, so only 90% of requests are served.

Chapter 23.2 splits an index into shards, which cut its size, and copies, which cut its load. The board shows only the copies: the node count is the number of copies that share the work. That is why adding load means adding nodes. It is also why the board prices the index above everything else. Chapter 23.2 planned 56 nodes for the full 7,000 searches a second. The lab uses a smaller slice, so ten is enough.

Five bigger nodes (medium) cost the same as ten small ones and pass at peak. After one dies, four nodes carry the load at 83% busy and p99 reaches 200 ms, over the 160 ms target. One failure costs a fifth of the fleet instead of a tenth. The queueing knee of chapter 2.5 does the rest.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 4: the indexing worker dies

<MermaidDiagram title="The queue fills while the worker is away" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App server"] -->|"2,000 changes/s"| Q["Queue<br>backlog grows"]
  Q -.->|"stopped"| W["Workers"]
  W --> S["Search index"]`} />

Searches do not notice a dead worker. The queue keeps each change. After 30 seconds at 2,000 changes a second it holds 60,000, and the workers must clear them as well as the new ones. Two workers take 22 seconds, three take 12, and one needs 150, far past the 30 seconds Dalil promised sellers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 4">

Each worker of the board's default size handles 2,400 changes a second. Two workers have 4,800 of capacity. They must handle the new 2,000 a second and the waiting changes (the backlog), so they have 2,800 a second to spare, and 60,000 divided by 2,800 is about 22 seconds. Three workers have 5,200 to spare and need 12 seconds. One worker has a capacity of 2,400 against 2,000 arriving, so only 400 a second are spare and the backlog takes 150 seconds. The same arithmetic as the short-link service of chapter 30.3 applies, only with a tighter deadline.

The 30 seconds is the freshness allowance of chapter 23.1: a product change must be searchable within 30 seconds. The board turns the promise into a test. In a real index there is one more delay, the refresh interval of chapter 23.2, because changes become searchable only when the next small segment is made. The board does not model that, so a design that passes here still has to leave room for it.

A worker that barely keeps up in normal times has no spare capacity to clear a build-up. Size the workers for the arrival rate plus the rate at which you want to recover.

</GoDeeper>

## A cheap design that skips a job

<MermaidDiagram title="Why no queue looks like a bargain" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Design with a queue<br>$2.93/h, p99 125 ms"] --> B["Index busy: 4,000/s"]
  C["Design with no queue<br>$2.39/h, p99 87 ms"] --> D["Index busy: 2,000/s<br>changes never arrive"]`} />

Take the queue and the workers away and the bill falls to $2.39 an hour, and p99 falls to 87 ms. It looks better. It is worse: the index no longer takes any changes, so the change check and the worker check both fail. Cheaper and faster is only a win when the same checks still pass.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a red check against a green number">

The board reports many numbers at once, and some of them improve when a job is skipped. This is a common trap in real reviews too. A system that drops a hard requirement often shows lower cost and lower latency, because it is doing less. The only protection is to read the functional checks first. If a path fails, no measurement downstream is worth reading.

The same trap appears with the cache. A design with no cache fails the suggestion check at once, and the database is 158% busy, so its capacity falls to 1,263 a second and p99 reaches 583 ms. That one is not a bargain at all, but notice that the first thing the board says is the path check, not the number.

</GoDeeper>

## Solution walkthrough

<MermaidDiagram title="From a failing run to the reference design" chart={`flowchart LR
  R["Run once"] --> S["Solution"]
  S --> P["Pick a part:<br>why, checks, without it"]
  S --> C["Compare with mine"]
  S --> K["Copy to my board"]`} />

After you press Run once, the Solution button opens the reference design on the same board. Pick any part to see why it is there, which checks it helps pass, and what fails without it. Compare with mine lists what differs from your design. Try first: a failing run you can explain teaches the most.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to use the solution well">

The solution is never locked behind a score, and nothing is recorded when you open it. Your own board stays untouched while you look: Back to my design returns to it, and Copy to my board asks before it replaces anything. The link labels in the solution show each hop on its own: lookups going to the cache with the share the cache answers, the misses going on to the database, and the changes going to the index.

A good way to use it is to compare after one more try. Fix the failure you understand, run again, and only then press Compare with mine. It lists parts, settings and links that differ, in words such as "lower" and "smaller", and names the failing checks each one helps explain. It does not print the reference's numbers.

A code lab for the same design is coming. It will run a stack with the same parts, where you stop each one yourself, and the names of the parts and the checks will match so that a design you built on the board can be replayed there.

</GoDeeper>

## What the board cannot show

<MermaidDiagram title="Two failures with no red check" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Product change<br>saved in the database"] -->|"queue, worker"| I1["Index copy 1<br>refreshed"]
  P -->|"queue, worker"| I2["Index copy 2<br>not yet"]
  I1 --> U1["Shopper A sees the new price"]
  I2 --> U2["Shopper B sees the old price"]`} />

The board checks that the index answers and takes changes. It never asks whether the answers are good, or whether every copy agrees. It has no ranking, so a design can return the wrong product first and pass. It has no refresh, so two shoppers can see different prices and pass. What would you check next?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: ranking quality and stale results, and what else the board hides">

**Ranking quality.** Chapter 23.2 scored products with tf-idf and added business signals such as sales and ratings. The board treats the index as a box that answers. A bad ranking costs sales and trust, yet no part on the board can fix it, and no check can see it. Quality would need a set of test queries with known good answers, scored on every change to the ranking. That is a separate piece of work from capacity.

**Stale results.** Each copy of an index refreshes on its own timer (chapter 23.2), so for a few seconds two shoppers can see different results. A seller who saves a change and searches at once may not see it. The board counts only how quickly the queue empties.

**Other limits.** The board sends every lookup to both the cache and the index, while in real Dalil a suggestion never reaches the index and a cache of popular searches would take load off it. The board has one pool of equal search nodes, not eight shards with copies, so it cannot show the slow-shard tail of chapter 23.2. Its sale night makes product changes as common as lookups, which they never are in a normal day. And there is no hot key: the letter s, asked by thousands of shoppers a second (chapter 23.3), is no busier than any other.

Each of these is a reason to run the design for real, with a code lab, and to watch what people see.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: in an interview">

**In an interview.** Walk the failures in order. Say what you lose when each part stops, and what each choice costs. "If an app server dies, the other carries on at 83% busy, because I kept one spare machine and the servers hold no state. If a search node dies, the other nine carry its share at 74%, because I used ten small nodes. With five bigger nodes the survivors would be at 83% and p99 would be over the target. If the indexing worker dies, changes wait in the queue and clear in 22 seconds, because two workers have spare capacity. Two things my board cannot show are ranking quality and stale results from copies that refresh at different times, so I would test them separately."

Interviewers like the last sentence most. They want to hear that you know what your measurements leave out.

</GoDeeper>
