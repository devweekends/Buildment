Exam week is coming, and Daftar expects 10,000 requests a second at peak. Hana has one load balancer, two app servers and one database. In this lab you build the design that carries exam week on a board, press Run, and see each target pass or fail. Everything you need is in chapters 4.2 to 4.6: scaling out, stateless app servers, load balancers and health checks, and spreading a fleet over zones.

There is no single right answer. Many designs pass, and some cost twice as much as others. Aim for a design that passes every target, then make it cheaper without breaking one. You will defend that design in this module's checklist.

## The brief: four parts, four targets

<MermaidDiagram title="Daftar today, and what exam week needs" chart={`flowchart LR
  U["Students<br>10,000 req/s at peak"] --> LB["Load balancer<br>1 small, 1 zone"]
  LB --> APP["App servers<br>2 small, 1 zone"]
  APP --> DB[("Database<br>1 medium, no standby")]
  T["Targets: 10,000 req/s, p99 under 200 ms,<br>99.9% available, under $4.50 an hour"]`} />

This is the board you start from. It must serve 10,000 requests a second with p99 under 200 ms, stay up 99.9% of the time, and cost less than $4.50 an hour. Today it passes only the budget. Your job is to change sizes, counts and zones until all four targets pass.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each target checks">

**Requests a second.** The lab finds the busiest part on the request path. Its capacity is the design's ceiling. If the ceiling is below 10,000, the design cannot serve exam week, whatever the other parts can do.

**p99 under 200 ms.** Each part adds its own service time, and a part that is busy adds waiting time on top. Waiting grows slowly up to about 70% busy and then climbs steeply. That is the knee from chapter 2.5. A design can serve 10,000 a second and still fail p99 because one part runs at 90%.

**99.9% available.** Every part can fail, and a part that lives in one zone fails with that zone. Chapter 2.6 showed that parts in a chain multiply: three parts at 99.9% each give about 99.7%. Spreading a part over two zones, or giving the database a standby, removes most of that risk.

**Under $4.50 an hour.** Each part has an hourly price from its size and count. The prices are illustrative, but the shape is real: a bigger part costs more, and a standby doubles the database's price. A design that passes every other target by buying the biggest of everything fails here.

</GoDeeper>

## Build it on the board

<Lab slug="hld-m4-three-tiers" />

Click a part to change its size, count or zones. Drag new parts in from the drawer and link them along the request path. The panel scores every change against the four targets. Start with the part that turns red first, fix it, then look for the next one.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how the board works out the numbers">

The board uses one model for every lab, and its rules are stated so you can check them by hand.

A part's capacity comes from its size and count. A size has a number of virtual CPUs, and each role serves a set number of requests per CPU: about 800 a second for a general load balancer, 1,200 for a compute app server, 600 for a memory-optimised database. A small load balancer has 2 CPUs, so 2 x 800 = 1,600 requests a second per zone. A load balancer in two zones has two copies of that.

The database splits its traffic. In this lab 80% of database traffic is reads and 20% is writes. Reads can go to read replicas (chapter 8 explains how they stay in step). Every write still goes to the primary, so 10,000 requests a second means about 2,000 writes on one machine.

Utilisation is the load a part sees divided by its capacity. Latency is each part's service time plus waiting time, and the waiting time follows the queueing curve from chapter 2.5. The availability of the design multiplies the availability of each part on the path, with zones and standbys counted the way chapter 2.6 counts parallel copies.

The numbers are a model, not a cloud. They compare the shapes of designs. They will not match a real bill or a real load test, and that is fine: the point is to see which part is the bottleneck and what each fix costs.

</GoDeeper>

## Find the part that sets the ceiling

<MermaidDiagram title="Every request passes every tier" chart={`flowchart LR
  U["10,000 req/s"] --> LB["Load balancer<br>about 1,600 per small zone"]
  LB --> APP["App servers<br>about 2,400 each"]
  APP --> DB[("Database primary<br>takes every write")]
  LB -. "fix: bigger size, more zones" .-> LB
  DB -. "fix: replicas for reads, bigger size" .-> DB`} />

Every request passes every tier, so the slowest tier decides. First it is the load balancer. Give it a bigger size or a second zone, and the app tier or the database becomes the busiest part. Fix them one at a time, and watch the cost after each change.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: one design that passes, and why">

One design that passes level 1: a large load balancer in two zones, eight small app servers spread over two zones, and a large database with one read replica and a standby in a second zone. The board reports a ceiling of about 12,000 requests a second, p99 near 110 ms, availability above 99.99%, and about $3.56 an hour.

It is not the only one. An extra-large database with no replica also gets there, for a little more money. Twelve app servers instead of eight passes too, and wastes money, because the app tier was never the bottleneck at eight. Try to find the cheapest design that still passes every target. That is the same trade-off you defend in this module's checklist.

Notice what did not help. Making the app servers bigger before the balancer was fixed changed nothing, and adding read replicas did not help the writes. In a real system, the same rule holds: measure which part is busiest before you buy anything.

</GoDeeper>

## One zone is one failure

<MermaidDiagram title="Two zones survive a bad one" chart={`flowchart LR
  LB["Load balancer<br>zone A and zone B"] --> A1["App servers<br>zone A"]
  LB --> B1["App servers<br>zone B"]
  A1 --> P[("Primary<br>zone A")]
  B1 --> P
  P -. "standby takes over if zone A fails" .-> S[("Standby<br>zone B")]`} />

A design that lives in one zone fails when that zone does, however big it is. Put the balancer and the app servers in two zones, and give the database a standby in the second zone. Turn the standby off and watch availability drop below 99.9%, while the cost falls.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the standby costs, and in an interview">

In the lab, removing the database standby saves about $0.60 an hour and drops availability from above 99.99% to about 99.85%. That is the trade the target forces: 99.9% is not reachable with a single database in one zone. Chapter 2.6's arithmetic says why: a part at 99.9% on its own leaves no room for anything else on the path to fail.

A standby is a second copy of the database that receives every write as it happens and takes over if the primary's zone fails. It serves no traffic while it waits, which is why it doubles the price without adding capacity. Read replicas add capacity for reads but are a different thing; module 8 compares them.

**In an interview.** Walk the request path from the user to the database and name the capacity of each part. Say which part is the bottleneck and how you would remove it, then say what one zone failing would take down and how two zones fix it. Interviewers often follow up with "what if traffic doubles?" The answer is the same walk again: find the new busiest part.
