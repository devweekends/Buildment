Think of the last time you waited for something to go on sale. You sat with the page open. At ten o'clock you pressed the button, and the page thought about it for a long time. Somewhere, a machine was being asked for the same seat by thousands of people at once. What would you want that machine to do? Keep that question in mind. In this module you build the system behind a ticket drop on a board, and then the board breaks it for you.

Tazkara (a made-up concert ticket service from module 25) has a big show going on sale at ten o'clock. Chapter 25.1 counted 400,000 fans for 20,000 seats. This lab uses a smaller drop of 8,000 requests a second at the busiest moment, so each part stays readable on the board. The shape is the same: a crowd much bigger than the system, and few seats.

Before you read on, make a guess. A shop has one till. When it is half busy, a customer waits about a minute. When it is 90 percent busy, how long do you think a customer waits? Choose, and keep your guess in mind. The board will show you the answer with real numbers.

## Three jobs a drop must do

<MermaidDiagram title="What Tazkara must do" chart={`flowchart LR
  F["A fan at ten o'clock"] -->|"1. hold a seat"| T["Tazkara"]
  F -->|"2. look at the seat map"| T
  T -->|"seats that are free"| F
  F -->|"3. pay"| T
  T -.->|"confirm the payment"| P[("Tickets sold")]`} />

Tazkara must **hold** a seat (keep it for one fan for a short time), **show the seat map** (which seats are free) and **confirm a payment** (turn a hold into a ticket). These are the **functional** requirements: what the system does. On the board, each is a check that asks whether a request can travel from the client to the part that does the job.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the three checks look at">

The board does not run your code, because there is none. It looks at the links you drew. "A seat can be held" is true when a request can travel from a client, through an app server, to a database that saves the hold. "The seat map shows what is free" is true when the app server can read seat availability from a cache, which is a fast copy kept in memory.

"A payment is confirmed" is true when the payment can travel from the app server to a queue, from the queue to a worker, and from the worker to the database. A queue is a line where work waits until a worker takes it (chapter 15). Unlike module 30, there is no shortcut here. A payment that has nowhere to wait is lost when its worker is away, so the board wants the queue.

Notice what these checks do not look at. They check that a request can reach the database. They do not check which seat it asks for. Two fans pressing buy on seat 7 look, to the board, like two fans pressing buy on two different seats. Chapter 36.3 comes back to this, because it is the most important thing the board cannot show.

</GoDeeper>

## Eight ways to be good, or not

<MermaidDiagram title="One design, four scenes" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["1. Peak traffic<br>8,000 requests a second"] --> B["2. An app server dies"]
  B --> C["3. The seat-map cache dies"]
  C --> D["4. The payment worker dies"]`} />

A design that does the three jobs can still be slow, fall over when a machine dies, or cost too much. These are the **non-functional** requirements: how well the system does its jobs. The board measures eight of them, in four scenes, when you press Run.

| In this scene | The board measures | What a good design shows |
|---|---|---|
| Peak traffic | capacity, the slowest 1 in 100 requests (p99), staying up (availability), cost | at least 3,000 buyers a second served, p99 under 90 ms, under a budget per hour |
| An app server dies | capacity, and p99 | buyers are still served, and p99 stays under 130 ms |
| The seat-map cache dies | p99 | the slowest 1 in 100 stays under 110 ms |
| The payment worker dies | payments lost, and how long to catch up | no payment is lost, and the backlog clears within two minutes |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the waiting room, and what to do before you open the board">

There is one new idea compared with module 30. At the busiest moment 8,000 requests a second arrive at the door, and a cheap system cannot carry that. So the design may put a **waiting room** in front: a rate limiter (chapter 6.5) that lets in only up to a limit and turns the rest away on purpose. Chapter 25.2 explains why real ticket sellers do this. The board shows a waiting room as over 100% busy, because it is refusing people. Here that is its job, not a fault.

The slowest 1 in 100 requests (**p99**) is the wait of the unlucky one in a hundred, and chapter 2.4 showed why that number matters more than the average. When you press Run, the board plays all four scenes in order, and then lists every check as pass or fail, with the number it measured, the number it needed and the reason when it failed.

Before you open the board, look at the table and ask yourself one thing: which of these eight do you think a first, simple design would fail? There is no wrong guess. Writing it down makes the result more interesting.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the numbers come from, and how far to trust them">

The board is a model, not a cloud. Each part has a capacity that comes from its size and count, and a request waits longer as a part gets busier (the point where waiting climbs, called the utilisation knee in chapter 2.5). Prices are illustrative. Latency is in model milliseconds, so compare one design with another on the board and do not compare the board with a stopwatch.

The targets were tuned so that a good design passes with a little room, and a design with one missing idea fails for one clear reason. The lab spec records every number and how it was tuned. There is no code lab for the ticket drop yet, so nothing in this module has been measured on a real stack.

</GoDeeper>
