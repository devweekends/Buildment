A run is a small experiment, and every red check is a result. This chapter reads the scenes one at a time. For each, it says what the board did and what to change.

All the numbers here come from the board's model. There is no code lab for the ticket drop yet (it is coming), so nothing in this chapter has been measured on a real stack. Read the numbers as a comparison between designs, not as a stopwatch.

## Scene 1: the waiting room at peak

<MermaidDiagram title="The waiting room shields the system" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["Crowd<br>8,000 req/s"] --> W["Waiting room<br>limit 3,500"]
  W --> LB["Load balancer<br>73% busy"]
  LB --> APP["App servers<br>49% busy"]`} />

At peak, 8,000 requests a second reach the door and the waiting room lets in 3,500. The load balancer runs at 73% and the app servers at 49%, so the slowest 1 in 100 requests takes 66 ms. Take the waiting room away and the same parts are 167% and 111% busy, and p99 is 1,486 ms.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 1">

With no waiting room the board shows three failures at once: p99, p99 with an app server down (1,486 ms), and the cache scene (2,010 ms, with the database 178% busy). The parts were sized for 3,500, and now 8,000 arrive. Setting the limit to 8,000 is the same as having no waiting room, and fails the same three.

Making every part big enough for the full 8,000 passes ten of eleven checks and costs $4.14 an hour against the $1.75 budget. The waiting room is the cheaper idea. Its price is that 4,500 requests a second wait outside, and chapter 25.2 asks the product question: who goes first, and who does that hurt?

The limit is a window, not a point. Between 3,000 and about 3,600 passes with the reference design's other parts. At 4,000 the balancer is 83% busy, p99 with an app server down reaches 152 ms and the cache scene 141 ms. At 2,000 only 2,000 buyers a second get in, and the capacity check fails. Remember that a waiting room shows over 100% busy: it is refusing people, not failing.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 2: an app server dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of three app servers" chart={`flowchart LR
  LB["Load balancer"] --> A1["App server"]
  LB --> A2["App server"]
  LB -.->|"stopped"| A3["App server"]`} />

One app server of three goes dark, and the load balancer sends its share to the others. Buyers are still served, and p99 rises from 66 to 101 ms. The lesson is one spare machine (N+1): size the fleet so that losing one still leaves enough.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 2">

On the board, three servers each carry 49% of their capacity. With one gone, the two left carry 73% each, and a busier part makes every request wait longer, which is why p99 rises. With only two servers to begin with, the one left would be 146% busy and p99 would be 1,360 ms. With one server, losing it leaves nothing: the ceiling falls to 0 a second.

Notice that with two servers the capacity check still passes. The waiting room caps the capacity number, so an overloaded part behind it does not show there. The p99 check catches it. This is why the lab has two checks for this scene. One is about buyers being served at all. The other is about the unlucky one in a hundred having to wait.

When an app server dies, the load balancer is the same size as before and its load does not change, so the board names the app server as the busiest part. If it named the load balancer, the balancer was too small before the failure.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 3: the seat-map cache dies

<MermaidDiagram title="When the cache goes, the database finds out" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers<br>3,500 req/s"] -.->|"cache gone"| C["Cache"]
  APP -->|"all 3,500 req/s"| DB[("Database<br>primary and 2 replicas")]`} />

The cache answers 90% of views, so the database sees about 350 a second. When the cache dies, it sees all 3,500. With two read-only copies it is 78% busy and p99 is 93 ms. With none it is 234% busy and p99 is 591 ms. A cache protects the database only while it is there.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 3">

On the board, a database with two read-only copies shares the views three ways and ends at 78% busy. With one copy it is 117% busy and p99 is still 591 ms, so one copy is not enough. With three copies it passes the cache scene with room (p99 77 ms) but costs $0.15 an hour more, which puts the bill $0.01 over the budget.

A design that needs its cache to survive has a hidden dependency. It must also carry the full load without it, or the cache's death becomes the database's death. Read-only copies are the cheap way to buy that room.

This is also where the cache earns its place on cost. Remove the cache and keep the database as it is: the database is 78% busy all the time and p99 is 93 ms, over the 90 ms target. The seat map has nowhere to be read from too, so a functional check fails. A cache placed in only one zone passes everything except availability (99.82%).

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 4: the payment worker dies

<MermaidDiagram title="The queue fills while the worker is away" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App server"] -->|"about 438 payments/s"| Q["Queue<br>backlog grows"]
  Q -.->|"stopped"| W["Worker"]
  W --> DB[("Database")]`} />

Fans can still hold seats when the worker is away. The queue keeps each payment. About 1 request in 8 that gets in is a payment (chapter 25.2), so after 60 seconds about 26,250 are waiting. One worker, with about 1,960 a second to spare, clears them in 14 seconds. A queue buys time only if the worker has spare capacity.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 4">

The lab sets the payment share on the queue: `shares: { queue: 0.125 }`. Each admitted fan makes about 4 requests and half of them buy, so 1 request in 8 is a payment, about 438 a second out of the 3,500 let in. The worker of the board's default size handles 2,400 a second, so it has about 1,960 a second to spare, and 26,250 divided by 1,960 is 14 seconds. With no worker the payments are never confirmed, and with no queue they have nowhere to wait. A second worker clears the backlog in 7 seconds and costs $1.73 an hour, still under the $1.75 budget. A third costs $1.85 and fails on cost. The share is a course assumption from chapter 25.2, not a measurement. If more fans bought, the queue and the workers would need to grow.

The batch size decides how many writes the database sees. With a batch size of 100, about 438 payments a second become about 4 database writes a second. With a batch size of 1 the database sees about 438 writes a second, and in the cache scene it is 88% busy and p99 reaches 121 ms, over the 110 ms target. Batching has a price: if a worker dies halfway through a batch, those payments are handled again after the restart. So confirming a payment must be safe to repeat, which is the idempotency habit of chapters 6.4 and 25.4.

</GoDeeper>

## Solution walkthrough

<MermaidDiagram title="From a failing run to the reference design" chart={`flowchart LR
  R["Run once"] --> S["Solution"]
  S --> P["Pick a part:<br>why, checks, without it"]
  S --> C["Compare with mine"]
  S --> K["Copy to my board"]`} />

After you press Run once, the Solution button opens the reference design on the same board. Pick any part to see why it is there, which checks it helps pass, and what fails without it. Compare with mine lists what differs from your design. Try first: a failing run you can explain teaches the most.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to use the solution well">

The solution is never locked behind a score, and nothing is recorded when you open it. Your own board stays untouched while you look: Back to my design returns to it, and Copy to my board asks before it replaces anything. The link labels in the solution show each hop on its own: views going to the cache with the share the cache answers, the misses going on to the database, and the payment writes counted separately.

A good way to use it is to compare after one more try. Fix the failure you understand, run again, and only then press Compare with mine. It lists parts, settings and links that differ, in words such as "lower" and "smaller", and names the failing checks each one helps explain. It does not print the reference's numbers.

The same design will run for real in a code lab for the ticket drop (coming soon), the way the URL shortener of module 30 does. The names of the parts and the checks are meant to match, so a design you build on the board can be replayed there.

</GoDeeper>

## The failure the board cannot show

<MermaidDiagram title="Two fans, one seat" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Fan A: hold seat 7"] --> APP["App server"]
  B["Fan B: hold seat 7"] --> APP
  APP -->|"the board sees: two requests"| DB[("Seat 7 row<br>who wins?")]`} />

Two fans press buy on seat 7 in the same millisecond. The board sees two requests to a database. It does not know they want the same row. A design with no protection can pass all eleven checks and still sell seat 7 twice. The fix is not a bigger part. It is one conditional update, from chapter 25.2. How would you test for it?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what no capacity model can see">

The board counts requests, sizes and busy percentages. Selling a seat twice is a correctness failure, not a capacity failure. The seat row is checked and then written in two steps, and a second fan fits between them (chapter 7.1). At 20 fans per seat, every popular seat has a rush on it, so this happens thousands of times in the first ten seconds. The fix is chapter 25.2: hold the seat with one conditional update that changes the row only if it is free, so exactly one fan wins and the other is told "taken" at once.

Other things the board cannot show are in the same family. A late payment taking a seat that was re-held for someone else (the confirm step must check the hold is still current). A payment message delivered twice (chapter 25.4). A fair order of entry into the waiting room, and bots that jump the queue. A seat map that is a few seconds old. The board also lets a waiting room be set to any limit, without asking who is let in first.

So a green board is necessary and not enough. It says the system can carry the load and survive three failures. It says nothing about whether the seats are sold once each. To check that, you need a test that sends many requests for one seat and counts the winners. What would you change in your design after reading this?

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: in an interview">

**In an interview.** Walk the failures in order, and say what you lose when each part stops. "I put a waiting room in front that admits about as many buyers as the system can carry, so the rest wait outside and the parts behind stay small. If an app server dies, the others carry on, because I kept one spare. If the cache dies, the database takes the full load, so I gave it read-only copies. If the payment worker dies, payments wait in the queue and clear after it returns, because the worker has spare capacity."

Then add the line interviewers like most: "None of that stops two fans getting the same seat. For that, a hold is one conditional update on the seat row, with an expiry, and the confirm step checks the hold is still current." Capacity gets you the first half of the answer. Correctness gets you the second.

</GoDeeper>
