This page is the board and nothing else. Chapter 36.1 listed the eleven checks. Here you build a design, press Run, and watch it meet them. The board starts with the smallest design that works: one load balancer (it spreads requests over the servers), one app server (it runs the code) and one database. It already holds seats. It will not pass much more than that, and finding out why is the lab.

There is no single right answer. Many designs pass, and one that passes at $1.60 an hour beats one that passes at $4. Aim for green first, then make it cheaper without turning anything red.

## How the board works

<MermaidDiagram title="The board, in four moves" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Palette<br>on the left"] -->|"drag or tap"| B["Board<br>link the dots"]
  B -->|"hover a part, click Tune"| S["Settings cassette<br>beside the part"]
  B -->|"press Run"| R["Four scenes,<br>then every check"]`} />

The palette is on the left: drag a part onto the board, or tap it. Link two parts by dragging from a dot on one to a dot on another. Settings are not on the page until you ask: hover a part and click Tune on its corner, and a dark cassette opens beside it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cassette, names, and what Run shows">

The board only allows links where requests really flow, and it tells you when a link is not allowed. For example, the client may link to a waiting room or a load balancer, but not straight to an app server. The settings drawer holds the part's size, how many there are, which zones they live in, and any setting special to that part, such as the waiting room's limit, a cache's hit rate or a worker's group size (batch size). Only one cassette is open at a time. Press Escape or the close key to put it away. The Names switch in the header changes the parts to real AWS names and sizes, with a line on what each one means. The numbers do not change.

Press Run and the board plays four scenes. Dots travel along the links, a part turns amber and then red as it fills, and a part that dies is marked DOWN. When the scenes end, each check shows pass or fail, the number the board measured, the number it needed, and why a failed check failed. Press Show on any check to replay its scene on the board.

</GoDeeper>

## Build it on the board

<Lab slug="hld-visual-ticket-drop" />

Run the starting design first, before you change anything. It has a load balancer, one app server and one database. Read the failures in order. Each one names a part and a number, and each number points at a setting.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a failure">

A failure has three lines. The first says what was measured and what was needed. The second says why, usually naming the busiest part and how busy it was. For example, "Load balancer is 500% busy, so it cannot serve the whole load" says that five times more requests arrive than that part can pass on, and every part behind it is starved. Fix the part named, run again, and a different part is usually the next one named. That is the loop of chapter 4.9: the busiest part on the path sets the ceiling, and you move the ceiling by raising one part at a time.

Two things are different once you add a waiting room. The capacity number can never be higher than the waiting room's limit, because the waiting room turns away everything above it. So an overloaded part behind the room does not lower the capacity number. It shows up in the slowest 1 in 100 requests (p99) and in the busy percentages instead, so read those too. And "Buyers are still served when an app server dies" runs the scene with one app server fewer. A fleet of one has nothing left, so it fails at once.

</GoDeeper>

## Let only some of the crowd in

<MermaidDiagram title="Eight thousand at the door, 3,500 let in" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["Crowd<br>8,000 req/s"] --> W["Waiting room<br>limit 3,500"]
  W -->|"3,500 req/s"| LB["Load balancer<br>and app servers"]
  W -.->|"4,500 req/s turned away"| X["Wait outside"]`} />

Put a rate limiter, the waiting room, between the client and the load balancer, and set its limit. The parts behind it now see at most the limit, so they calm down. It shows over 100% busy because it is refusing people. Too high a limit and the parts behind it fill. Too low and too few buyers get in.

```predict
prompt: "You put a waiting room in front of the load balancer but set its limit to 8,000, the same as the crowd. What happens to the parts behind it?"
options:
  - id: calm
    label: "They are protected, because the waiting room in front turns the extra people away"
  - id: same
    label: "They see all 8,000 a second, as if there were no waiting room"
correctOptionId: same
explanation: "A waiting room only turns away what is above its limit. With a limit equal to the crowd it turns away nothing, so the load balancer and the app servers see all 8,000 a second and fill up. The limit is the whole point of the part."
allowRetry: true
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why not just buy bigger parts?">

You can. Every part sized for all 8,000 requests a second, with a bigger balancer, six app servers and a larger database, passes ten of the eleven checks, and the one it fails is cost: about $4.14 an hour against a budget of $1.75. The database alone is $2.40 of that. The waiting room costs a cent or two an hour and lets every other part be small.

The trade is that 4,500 requests a second wait outside. Real ticket sellers accept that, because the alternative is to let everyone in and serve nobody well (chapter 25.2). Who is let in first is a fairness rule: first come first served, a random draw, or a lottery. The board does not pick one. It only counts how many get in.

With the other parts of the reference design, a limit from 3,000 to about 3,600 passes. At 4,000 the load balancer and database run hot enough that the fault checks fail on p99. At 2,000 the capacity check fails, because too few buyers are let in.

</GoDeeper>

## Show the seat map from a cache

<MermaidDiagram title="Where 3,500 requests a second go" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers<br>3,500 req/s"] --> C["Cache<br>90% hits"]
  APP -->|"misses: about 350 req/s"| DB[("Database")]`} />

Most requests are fans looking at the seat map. Link a cache from the app server and set its hit rate. A hit never reaches the database, only misses go on. At 90% the database sees about 350 requests a second instead of 3,500. Then run again and watch the cache scene.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cache scene">

In the cache scene the cache goes dark and every seat view asks the database. A database with no read-only copy (read replica) cannot serve that, so the check fails and the board names the database as the busiest part. Read-only copies share the views and buy back the room, at a price you can read in the drawer. The cache is a speed-up, and the design must also carry the load without it. Chapter 36.3 follows this failure in detail.

Each link on the board shows its own numbers: views going to the cache with the share it answers, the misses going on to the database, and the payment writes. The seat map may be a few seconds old, because the real check is the hold request itself (chapter 25.1 promised this). The board does not model a stale map.

</GoDeeper>

## Confirm payments off the busy path

<MermaidDiagram title="Payments wait in a line" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App server<br>3,500 req/s"] -->|"1 in 8 is a payment:<br>about 438/s"| Q["Queue"]
  Q --> W["Worker<br>batch of 100"]
  W -->|"about 4 writes/s"| DB[("Database")]`} />

Add a queue and a worker and link app server to queue to worker to database. Only about 1 request in 8 that gets in is a payment (chapter 25.2), so about 438 a second reach the queue. The worker saves them in groups (batches), so the database sees about 4 writes a second. Set the batch size above 1.

```predict
prompt: "About 438 payments arrive each second and one worker handles 2,400 a second. The worker stops for 60 seconds, so 26,250 payments wait. How long does it take to clear them once it is back?"
options:
  - id: long
    label: "About 60 seconds, because it needs as long as it was away"
  - id: short
    label: "About 14 seconds, using its spare 1,960 a second"
correctOptionId: short
explanation: "The worker must handle the new payments and the waiting ones. It handles 2,400 a second, minus about 438 arriving, which leaves about 1,960 a second to spare. 26,250 divided by 1,960 is about 14 seconds. A queue buys time only while the worker has spare capacity."
allowRetry: true
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the payment share and the batch size">

Not every request is a payment. Chapter 25.2 says each admitted fan makes about 4 requests and half of the admitted fans end up buying. That is one payment for every 8 requests, or 12.5%. The lab sets this share on the queue, so the link into it on the board says "12.5% of requests send work here". The links to the cache and the database still carry every request, because every request looks at a seat map or holds a seat. The share is a course assumption, not a measurement.

A batch size of 1 sends every payment to the database as its own write: about 438 a second. The database then runs hotter, and in the cache scene it reaches 88% busy and the slowest 1 in 100 requests fails its target. A batch of 100 turns that into about 4 writes a second. Batching has a price: a payment is confirmed a little later, and if the worker dies halfway through a batch, those payments are handled again after the restart. That is why chapter 25.4 insists that confirming a payment must be safe to repeat.

</GoDeeper>

## Make it cheaper

<MermaidDiagram title="Pass first, then trim" chart={`flowchart LR
  G["All checks green"] --> T["Trim the part with spare room"]
  T --> R["Run again"]
  R -->|"a check turns red"| U["Undo that trim"]
  R -->|"still green"| T`} />

When every check is green, look at the cost. Find the part with the most spare room, shrink it one step, and run again. If a check turns red, undo that step. The budget is $1.75 an hour, and a design that passes costs a little less. A third worker, a third read-only copy or one size too large fails on cost alone.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: stuck? One design that passes">

One design that passes all eleven checks: a waiting room with a limit of 3,500. A load balancer of size general-small in three zones. Three app servers of size compute-small in two zones. One cache node of size memory-small at a 90% hit rate in two zones. A database of size memory-small with an extra copy that takes over (a failover standby) in a second zone, two read-only copies, set to 80% reads. A queue, and one worker of size compute-small with a batch size of 100. The client links to the waiting room, the waiting room to the balancer, the balancer to the app servers. The app server links to the cache, the database and the queue. The queue links to the workers, and the workers link to the database.

The board reports a ceiling of 3,500 requests a second, p99 near 66 ms, availability near 99.98% and about $1.61 an hour. The checks that depend most on one choice are the cache check, which needs two read-only copies, and the payment checks, which need the queue and the worker. Try taking each away, and read what the board says.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Your design passes every check except cost, and its parts are all sized for the full 8,000 requests a second. It has no waiting room. What is the cheapest change that is likely to keep the other checks green?"

  - "Add a second cache node, because the cache is cheap"
  - "Add a waiting room, then shrink every part behind it"
  - "Add read-only copies (read replicas) to the database"
  - "Add another worker, to share the payments"

explanations:
  - "A second cache node adds cost. The cache answers views, and the cost here comes from sizing every part for a crowd the waiting room could have held back."
  - "Right. A waiting room costs a cent or two an hour and turns the extra crowd away, so the load balancer, app servers, queue and worker can all go back to a size for 3,500."
  - "Read-only copies share reads. They add cost, and they do not lower what the other parts must carry."
  - "More workers add cost. The cost problem is every part being sized for 8,000, and one more worker makes the bill bigger."
```
