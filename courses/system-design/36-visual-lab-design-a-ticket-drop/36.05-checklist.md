## Learning log

1. **Your first run:** Which checks did the starting design fail, and which one surprised you most?
2. **Your waiting room:** What limit did you set, and what happened when you tried one that was higher and one that was lower? Which check showed it?
3. **Your busiest part:** In your final design, which part is the busiest at peak, and how busy is it? What would you raise next, and what would it cost?
4. **Your cache:** What does your design do when the cache dies? Which part of it makes that survivable?
5. **Your payments:** About 1 admitted request in 8 is a payment (chapter 25.2). How many workers did you use, and why that many? What did a second or third worker do to your bill?
6. **Your double sale:** The board passed your design. Could it still sell one seat to two fans? Say why, and say what in chapter 25.2 would stop it.
7. **Your first guess:** In chapter 36.1 you guessed how the wait changes when a till goes from half busy to 90 percent busy. Was your guess right, and where did you see the same thing on the board?

## Your gate answer: defend your design

This is the lab again, in words. You built a design on the board and it told you, check by check, where it was weak. A design review in a real team works the same way. Someone shows a design, someone else asks what happens when a part fails, and the designer answers with a number, not a feeling. Write the answer you would give in that room, about 200 words, about the design that passed or came closest.

A weak answer lists the parts: a waiting room, a load balancer, three app servers, a cache, a database, a queue and a worker. It reads like a shopping list, and it could describe any design. A strong answer starts from a number the board showed you and lets that number decide the parts. Say which check was the hardest to pass, what you changed to pass it, and the number before and after. For example, the database at 234% busy in the cache scene, and 78% after you added two read-only copies.

Then pick one trade-off and write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a waiting room with a limit of 3,500, so the other parts can be sized for 3,500 and not 8,000, at the price that 4,500 requests a second wait outside", or "one worker at batch 100, which has about 1,960 payments a second to spare to clear a backlog". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good "when would you change it" line also has a trigger, such as "if the show is twice as big" or "if the fans in line complain more than we can accept".

End with the failure your design is built to survive, and the one it is not. Every design has one. Saying so is not a weakness. It is the part of the answer that shows you looked. The board cannot see two fans being sold the same seat, so say how your design stops that, in the language of chapter 25.2: a hold as one conditional update on the seat row, an expiry, and a confirm step that checks the hold is still current. If you left it out, say so, and say what you would pay to add it.

A good order for 200 words is the order of the board. Start with the hardest check and the number that moved. Then the trade-off. Last, the failure you did not fix. If you run short of words, cut the list of parts, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is tuning until the board is green without reading the reasons, and ending with every part at the largest size, which passes everything and fails on cost. The second is reading the waiting room as a fault because it shows over 100% busy. It is the one part that is meant to say no, and the lab is checking that you set its limit with the parts behind it in mind. The third is believing a green board means the system is correct. The board checks capacity, speed, survival and cost. It does not check that a seat is sold once.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the choice to a check, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to the waiting payments if the queue itself fails, or why you chose a limit of 3,500 and not 3,000. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
