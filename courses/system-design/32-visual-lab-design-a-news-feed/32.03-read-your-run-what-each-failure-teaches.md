A run is a small experiment, and every red check is a result. This chapter reads the scenes one at a time. For each, it says what the board did, what it teaches about a feed, and what to change.

Every number here comes from the board's model and the lab spec that tuned it. There is no code lab for the feed yet, so no real stack was measured. Read the numbers as shapes: compare one design with another, and do not compare the board with a stopwatch.

## Scene 2: an app server dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of three app servers" chart={`flowchart LR
  LB["Load balancer"] --> A1["App server"]
  LB --> A2["App server"]
  LB -.->|"stopped"| A3["App server"]`} />

One app server of three goes dark, and the load balancer sends its share to the others. Everyone is still served, and p99 rises from about 60 to about 84 ms. The lesson is one spare machine (N+1): size the fleet so that losing one still leaves enough. The servers keep nothing of their own, so any server can open any feed.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 2">

On the board, three servers each carry 44% of their capacity. With one gone, the two left carry 67% each, and a busier part makes every request wait longer, which is why p99 rises. With only two servers to begin with, the one left would need to carry 133% of what it can, and about 75% of requests would be served. With only one server, losing it leaves nothing, and no request is served.

One spare machine is the rule from chapter 4.2 meeting a failure. The extra server is not waste, it is the part that makes the death invisible. When an app server dies, the load balancer is the same size as before, and its load does not change, so the board names the app server as the busiest part. If it named the load balancer, the balancer would have been too small before the failure, and "Serves the whole peak" would already be red.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 3: the feed cache dies

<MermaidDiagram title="When the cache goes, the database finds out" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers<br>3,200 req/s"] -.->|"cache gone"| C["Cache of ready feeds"]
  APP -->|"all 3,200 req/s"| DB[("Database<br>primary and replica")]`} />

The cache answers 95% of feed opens, so the database sees about 160 a second. When the cache dies, it sees all 3,200. With a read-only copy it rises from 3% to 65% busy and everyone is served. Without one it reaches 131%, and about a quarter of requests fail. A cache protects the database only while it is there.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 3">

On the board, a database with one read-only copy rises from 3% to 65% busy, p99 goes from 60 to 75 ms, and everyone is still served. A database with no read-only copy goes to 131% busy, serves only 76.5% of requests, and p99 jumps to about 585 ms. Nothing was wrong while the cache worked, which is why the failure is easy to miss.

A design that needs its cache to survive has a hidden dependency. It must also carry the full load without it, or the cache's death becomes the database's death. The read-only copy is the cheap way to buy that room, and the board charges you $0.30 an hour for it. A bigger database with no copy also passes this scene, and costs $2.84 an hour in all, over the budget.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the board is kinder than a real feed here">

On the board, a feed open that misses the cache costs the database one read. In the real Hikaya, a feed that is not ready must be built from scratch (chapter 21.3): the service looks up the posts of each account the reader follows, about 150 lookups, so 3,200 opens a second would mean about 480,000 lookups a second. The board counts one. So when the cache of ready feeds dies for real, the fall is far steeper than the board shows. The board teaches the shape: the cache's death moves its whole load onto the database. Chapter 21.1 puts the real size of that load at 2.6 million lookups a second at the peak of 17,400 opens.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 4: the fan-out worker dies

<MermaidDiagram title="The queue fills while the worker is away" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App server"] -->|"3,200 posts/s"| Q["Queue<br>backlog grows"]
  Q -.->|"stopped"| W["Worker"]
  W --> C["Cache of ready feeds"]`} />

Feeds still open, because the ready feeds are still in the cache. But no new post reaches them. After 45 seconds at 3,200 posts a second the queue holds 144,000, and the workers must clear them as well as the new posts. Three workers take 36 seconds, two take 90, and one never catches up. A queue buys time only if the workers have spare capacity.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 4, and what the reader sees">

Each worker of the board's default size handles 2,400 posts a second. Three workers have 7,200 of capacity. They must handle the new 3,200 a second and the waiting posts, so they have 4,000 a second to spare, and 144,000 divided by 4,000 is 36 seconds. Two workers have 1,600 to spare and need 90 seconds, over the 60 second limit. One worker has a capacity of 2,400, below the 3,200 arriving, so the waiting posts never shrink. Four workers pass too, but the design then costs $2.66 an hour, over the $2.60 budget.

What does a follower see during the outage? Not an error. The feed opens, a little out of date. Chapter 21.1 allows exactly this: a new post reaches most followers within about 10 seconds, and when something is slow, Hikaya shows a slightly older feed instead of an error. The author sees her own post at once (chapter 21.2). A worker's death is a freshness problem, not an outage, and that is why the check measures how fast the backlog clears and not whether feeds open.

The board sends every request past the queue, where Hikaya has about 1 post for every 50 feed opens. So the board's workers are busier than the real ones would be, and the real fan-out is much heavier per post (one write for each follower, chapter 21.3). The lesson holds: size the workers for the arrival rate plus the rate at which you want to recover.

</GoDeeper>

## Solution walkthrough

<MermaidDiagram title="From a failing run to the reference design" chart={`flowchart LR
  R["Run once"] --> S["Solution"]
  S --> P["Pick a part:<br>why, checks, without it"]
  S --> C["Compare with mine"]
  S --> K["Copy to my board"]`} />

After you press Run once, the Solution button opens the reference design on the same board. Pick any part to see why it is there, which checks it helps pass, and what fails without it. Compare with mine lists what differs from your design. Try first: a failing run you can explain teaches the most.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to use the solution well">

The solution is never locked behind a score, and nothing is recorded when you open it. Your own board stays untouched while you look: Back to my design returns to it, and Copy to my board asks before it replaces anything. The link labels in the solution show each hop on its own: reads going to the cache with the share the cache answers, and the misses going on to the database.

A good way to use it is to compare after one more try. Fix the failure you understand, run again, and only then press Compare with mine. It lists parts, settings and links that differ, in words such as "lower" and "smaller", and names the failing checks each one helps explain. It does not print the reference's numbers.

A code lab for the feed, with the same parts running for real in Docker, is coming. Only the short-link service has one today. The names of the parts and the checks are meant to match, so a design you built on the board can be replayed there.

</GoDeeper>

## What the board cannot show

<MermaidDiagram title="One post, very different work" chart={`flowchart LR
  P1["A post from an everyday account"] -->|"100 writes"| F1["100 feeds"]
  P2["A post from Karim<br>20 million followers"] -->|"20,000,000 writes"| F2["20,000,000 feeds"]`} />

The board gives every request the same weight. In Hikaya a post from an everyday account is about 100 writes, and one post from Karim is 20 million, which holds every worker for over two minutes while ordinary posts wait (chapter 21.3). The board has no per-account fan-out, so it cannot show this celebrity problem. What would you change in your design?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to change, and three more gaps">

Chapter 21.3 gave the answer for the celebrity problem: pull the few huge accounts and push everyone else (the hybrid). Then a reader's feed is the pushed feed merged with the lists of the stars they follow, and Karim's post is saved once. The board cannot test that design, so use the board for the part it can judge, and use chapter 21.3 for the rest.

There are three more gaps. A cache that comes back after a restart is empty (cold), so every feed misses until the workers and readers fill it again. The board's cache is always warm. Ranking (chapter 21.4) costs time on every open, and the board has no ranker. And a post can reach some feeds and not others while a worker is mid-way through its list, so two readers may see the same post at different seconds. The board counts a post as delivered or waiting, never half delivered.

Honest answers to "what would you change": add a second queue for big accounts so ordinary posts keep flowing; pull the stars at read time; keep a copy of the feed on disk so a restart is not cold; or accept a stale feed and say so. Each costs something, and a good design says which cost it chose.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: in an interview">

**In an interview.** Walk the failures in order. Say what you lose when each part stops, and what each choice costs. "If an app server dies, the others carry on, because I kept one spare machine and the servers hold no state. If the feed cache dies, the database takes the full load, so I sized it, with a read-only copy, to survive that. If the fan-out worker dies, feeds still open, a little stale, and the posts wait in the queue and drain after the restart, because the workers have spare capacity. And I know my model weighs every post the same, so a post from an account with 20 million followers would need a different rule: I would pull the big accounts."

Interviewers like the last sentence most. They want to hear that you looked for the case your numbers hide, and that you can name what you would do about it.

</GoDeeper>
