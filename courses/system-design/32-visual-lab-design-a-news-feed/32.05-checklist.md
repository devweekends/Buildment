## Learning log

1. **Your first run:** Which checks did the starting design fail, and which one surprised you most?
2. **Your busiest part:** In your final design, which part is the busiest at peak, and how busy is it? What would you raise next, and what would it cost?
3. **Your cache:** What does your design do when the feed cache dies? Which part of it makes that survivable?
4. **Your spreading:** Did your design spread posts through a queue and a worker, or build feeds on read? What did you pay for that choice?
5. **Your hidden case:** The board gives every post the same weight. Which account in Hikaya would break your design, and what would you change for it? Use chapter 21.3.
6. **Your first guess:** In chapter 32.1 you guessed how the wait changes when a till goes from half busy to 90 percent busy. Was your guess right, and where did you see the same thing on the board?

## Your gate answer: defend your design

This is the lab again, in words. You built a design on the board and it told you, check by check, where it was weak. A design review in a real team works the same way. Someone shows a design, someone else asks what happens when a part fails, and the designer answers with a number, not a feeling. Write the answer you would give in that room, about 200 words, about the design that passed or came closest.

A weak answer lists the parts: a load balancer, three app servers, a cache, a database, a queue and a worker. It reads like a shopping list, and it could describe any design. A strong answer starts from a number the board showed you and lets that number decide the parts. Say which check was the hardest to pass, what you changed to pass it, and the number before and after. For example, the database at 131% busy when the cache stopped, and 65% busy after you added a read-only copy.

Then pick one trade-off and write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a read-only copy (read replica) at $0.30 an hour, so the database survives the cache dying", or "one worker instead of two, which saves $0.12 an hour because only 1 request in 50 is a post". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good "when would you change it" line also has a trigger, such as "if the peak doubles" or "if feeds may be a minute stale".

End with the failure your design is built to survive, and the one it is not. Every design has one. Saying so is not a weakness. It is the part of the answer that shows you looked. The board cannot test a post from an account with 20 million followers, so say what your design would do with one, and say what you would pay to handle it.

A good order for 200 words is the order of the board. Start with the hardest check and the number that moved. Then the trade-off. Last, the failure you did not fix. If you run short of words, cut the list of parts, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is tuning until the board is green without reading the reasons, and ending with every part at the largest size, which passes everything and fails on cost. The second is treating the cache as redundancy. A cache makes reads fast, but it is not a copy that keeps the service up, and the cache scene is there to show it. The third is sizing the worker from the wrong rate. Count only the requests that are posts (1 in 50 in chapter 21.1), not every request, or you pay for workers the board shows idle. And leave room above the arrival rate, so a waiting backlog, once it exists, can be cleared.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the choice to a check, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to the feeds if the cache comes back empty, or why you chose one worker and not two. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.

modelAnswer: "The hardest check was 'everyone is still served when the feed cache dies'. My first design had a cache at a 95% hit rate and a database with no read-only copy. At peak the database was 3% busy. In the cache scene it went to 131% busy, only 76.5% of requests were served, and p99 was about 585 ms. I added one read-only copy. The database then ran at 65% busy without the cache, everyone was served, and p99 was 75 ms. Trade-off: I kept the cache at 95% and paid for the copy. It buys survival of a cache failure. It costs $0.30 an hour, about 16% of my $1.85 design, and one more part to keep in sync. I would drop it if the cache had copies in several zones, or if the load fell to what one database serves alone. The design survives the loss of one app server (three servers, 67% busy with two left), the loss of the feed cache, and a 2 minute worker outage (one worker delivers the 7,680 waiting posts 4 seconds after it returns).
