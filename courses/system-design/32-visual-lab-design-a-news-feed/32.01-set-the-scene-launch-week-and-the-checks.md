Open a social app and your feed is already there, full of posts from the people you follow. Nobody went looking for it while you waited. Something prepared it earlier. Now picture that something stopping at the exact moment a friend posts. What would you want to happen to their post? Keep that question in mind. In this module you build the feed service on a board, and then the board breaks it for you.

Hikaya (a made-up app from module 21) is in its first launch week. At the busiest moment, 3,200 people a second open their feed. That is about a fifth of the 17,400 a second of chapter 21.1, and it is the size where one wrong choice already shows.

Before you read on, make a guess. A shop has one till. When it is half busy, a customer waits about a minute. When it is 90 percent busy, how long do you think a customer waits? Choose, and keep your guess in mind. The board will show you the answer with real numbers.

## Three jobs a home feed must do

<MermaidDiagram title="What Hikaya must do" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["An author"] -->|"1. post"| H["Hikaya"]
  H -->|"2. spread the post"| F[("Ready feeds<br>of her followers")]
  R["A reader"] -->|"3. open the feed"| H
  H -->|"a page of posts"| R`} />

Hikaya must **save** a post, **spread** it (put it into each follower's ready feed, called fan-out on write) and **open** a feed (give a reader a page of posts). These are the **functional** requirements: what the system does. On the board, each is a check that asks whether a request can travel from the client to the part that does the job.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the three checks look at">

The board does not run your code, because there is none. It looks at the links you drew. "A post is saved" is true when a request can travel from a client, through an app server, to a database that keeps the post.

"A feed can be opened" is true when the app server can read posts from the database. A cache in between is allowed, but it is optional, because a cache only remembers answers and never holds the truth. A design with no cache builds each feed when it is opened. That is fan-out on read (pull), the other way of chapter 21.3. It works, and the board shows what it costs.

"A new post reaches followers' feeds" has one way to pass. A post must travel from the app server to a queue, from the queue to a worker, and from the worker to the cache that holds the ready feeds. Chapter 21.2 explains why the author gets her answer first and the spreading happens later, in the background. If a design cannot do one of the three jobs, nothing else about it matters, so these checks come first.

</GoDeeper>

## Eight ways to be good, or not

<MermaidDiagram title="One design, four scenes" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["1. Peak traffic<br>3,200 feed opens a second"] --> B["2. An app server dies"]
  B --> C["3. The feed cache dies"]
  C --> D["4. The fan-out worker dies"]`} />

A design that does the three jobs can still be slow, fall over when a machine dies, or cost too much. These are the **non-functional** requirements: how well the system does its jobs. The board measures eight of them, in four scenes, when you press Run.

| In this scene | The board measures | What a good design shows |
|---|---|---|
| Peak traffic | capacity, the slowest 1 in 100 requests (p99), staying up (availability), cost | all 3,200 a second served, the slowest 1 in 100 under 65 ms, under a budget per hour |
| An app server dies | the share of requests still served | everyone is still served with one server fewer |
| The feed cache dies | the share served, and the slowest 1 in 100 | everyone is still served, and the slowest 1 in 100 stays under 100 ms |
| The fan-out worker dies | posts lost, and how long to catch up | no post is lost, and the waiting posts reach the feeds within 10 seconds |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading the table, and what to do before you open the board">

The slowest 1 in 100 requests (**p99**) is the time that 99 out of 100 requests beat. It is the wait of the unlucky one in a hundred, and chapter 2.4 showed why that number matters more than the average. A **replica** is one of several identical copies of a machine, so that losing one does not stop the service. A **read replica** is a read-only copy of a database.

When you press Run, the board plays all four scenes in order. In scene 2 one app server turns red. In scene 3 the cache goes dark and the database fills up. In scene 4 the worker stops, and posts pile up in the queue. After the last scene, every check is listed as pass or fail, with the number the board measured, the number it needed, and the reason when it failed.

Before you open the board, look at the table and ask yourself one thing: which of these eight do you think a first, simple design would fail? There is no wrong guess. Writing it down makes the result more interesting.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the numbers come from, and how far to trust them">

The board is a model, not a cloud. Each part has a capacity that comes from its size and count, and a request waits longer as a part gets busier (the point where waiting climbs, called the utilisation knee in chapter 2.5). Prices are illustrative. Latency is in model milliseconds, so compare one design with another on the board and do not compare the board with a stopwatch, or with the 200 ms of chapter 21.1.

The targets were tuned so that a good design passes with a little room, and a design with one missing idea fails for one clear reason. There is no code lab for this challenge yet, so no real Docker stack was measured: every number you see on the board comes from the model, and the lab spec records how each was tuned. A code lab for the feed is coming. The board gives every post the same weight, which hides some of the hardest parts of a feed. Chapter 32.3 ends with what it cannot show.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "A design saves every post and opens every feed correctly, but the whole service stops when its single app server crashes. Which kind of requirement does it fail?"
options:
  - "A non-functional one, because it works but is not resilient"
  - "A functional one, because it did not open feeds during the crash"
  - "Neither, because a crash is an operations problem"
  - "A functional one, because the app server holds the posts"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. The design does its jobs while it is up. How well it keeps doing them when a part dies is a non-functional requirement: availability."
  - "Opening a feed is the job, and the design does it. What fails is staying up when a part dies, which is a quality of the service, not a feature."
  - "A crash is exactly the kind of event a design must expect. Surviving one is a requirement the board tests on purpose."
  - "The app server answers requests but the posts live in the database. Where data is kept is a design choice, not a kind of requirement."
```
