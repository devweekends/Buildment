This page is the board and nothing else. Chapter 32.1 listed the eleven checks. Here you build a design, press Run, and watch it meet them. The board starts with the smallest design that works: one load balancer (it spreads requests over the servers), one app server (it runs the code) and one database. It already saves posts and opens feeds, by asking the database every time. It will not pass much more than that, and finding out why is the lab.

There is no single right answer. Many designs pass, and one that passes at $2.54 an hour beats one that passes at $5. Aim for green first, then make it cheaper without turning anything red.

## How the board works

<MermaidDiagram title="The board, in four moves" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Palette<br>on the left"] -->|"drag or tap"| B["Board<br>link the dots"]
  B -->|"hover a part, click Tune"| S["Settings cassette<br>beside the part"]
  B -->|"press Run"| R["Four scenes,<br>then every check"]`} />

The palette is on the left: drag a part onto the board, or tap it. Link two parts by dragging from a dot on one to a dot on another. Settings are not on the page until you ask: hover a part and click Tune on its corner, and a dark cassette opens beside it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cassette, names, and what Run shows">

The board only allows links where requests really flow, and it tells you when a link is not allowed. The settings drawer holds the part's size, how many there are, which zones they live in, and any setting special to that part, such as a cache's hit rate. Only one cassette is open at a time. On a phone it docks along the bottom while the part moves into view above it, and you tap a part to show its Tune key. Press Escape or the close key to put it away. The Names switch in the header changes the parts to real AWS names and sizes, such as Amazon ElastiCache for Redis and c6i.large, with a line on what each one means. The numbers do not change.

Press Run and the board plays four scenes. Dots travel along the links, a part turns amber and then red as it fills, and a part that dies is marked DOWN. When the scenes end, each check shows pass or fail, the number the board measured, the number it needed, and why a failed check failed. Press Show on any check to replay its scene on the board.

</GoDeeper>

## Build it on the board

<Lab slug="hld-visual-news-feed" />

Run the starting design first, before you change anything. It has one load balancer, one app server and one database. Read the failures in order. Each one names a part and a number, and each number points at a setting.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a failure">

A failure has three lines. The first says what was measured and what was needed. The second says why, usually naming the busiest part and how busy it was. For example, "Load balancer is 200% busy, so it cannot serve the whole load" says that more requests arrive than that part can pass on, and every part behind it is starved. Fix the part named, run again, and a different part is usually the next one named. That is the loop of chapter 4.9: the busiest part on the path sets the ceiling, and you move the ceiling by raising one part at a time.

Two failures deserve a closer look. "Everyone is still served when one app server dies" runs the scene with one app server fewer. A fleet of one has nothing left, so it fails at once. A fleet of two may serve only 75%, because the one left cannot carry the whole load. And "No posts are lost when the fan-out worker dies" needs the posts to wait somewhere while the worker is down, which is what a queue is for. Then it needs the workers to be fast enough, once back, to deliver the waiting posts (the backlog) as well as the new ones.

</GoDeeper>

## Put a cache of ready feeds in front

<MermaidDiagram title="Where 3,200 feed opens a second go" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers<br>3,200 req/s"] --> C["Cache of ready feeds<br>95% hits"]
  APP -->|"misses: about 160 req/s"| DB[("Database")]`} />

Link a cache from the app server and set its hit rate. A hit means the feed was ready, so the database is never asked, and only misses go on. At 95% hits the database sees about 160 requests a second instead of 3,200, and p99 drops. Then run again and watch the cache scene.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cache scene">

In the cache scene the cache goes dark and every request asks the database. A database with no read-only copy (read replica) cannot serve 3,200 a second, so the check fails and the board names the database as the busiest part. A read-only copy shares the reads and buys back the room, at a price you can read in the drawer. The cache is a speed-up, and the design must also carry the load without it. Chapter 32.3 follows this failure in detail.

Each link on the board shows its own numbers: reads going to the cache with the share it answers, and the misses going on to the database. The cache is also where the workers put each new post, so keep a cache on the board even when you could do without it for speed alone. The delivery check needs it.

</GoDeeper>

## Spreading is where designs differ

<MermaidDiagram title="Two ways to give a reader a feed" chart={`flowchart LR
  subgraph onread["No queue: build on read"]
    A1["App server"] -->|"every open asks"| D1[("Database")]
  end
  subgraph onwrite["Queue and worker: spread on write"]
    A2["App server"] --> Q["Queue"] --> W["Worker"] --> C["Cache of ready feeds"]
  end`} />

You can build each feed when it is opened, with no extra part. The board lets you, and shows the price: the database does the work of every open, and no post is ever delivered. Or add a queue and a worker, so that each new post is written into the ready feeds. Try both, and compare the cost and the busiest part each time.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the queue and the worker are worth their price">

The simplest way to give a reader a feed needs no extra part: ask the database when the feed is opened. That is fan-out on read (pull), and chapter 21.3 shows how its cost grows with the number of accounts a reader follows. On the board it shows as a database that must stay busy on every open and that falls over when it has no read-only copy.

The other way is fan-out on write (push). The app server saves the post, puts a note on a queue and answers the author at once. A worker takes the note and writes the post into the followers' ready feeds. The author never waits for the spreading. The price is two more parts to run and pay for, and a short delay before a follower sees the post. The queue is priced per message, so it costs more as traffic grows.

</GoDeeper>

## Make it cheaper

<MermaidDiagram title="Pass first, then trim" chart={`flowchart LR
  G["All checks green"] --> T["Trim the part with spare room"]
  T --> R["Run again"]
  R -->|"a check turns red"| U["Undo that trim"]
  R -->|"still green"| T`} />

When every check is green, look at the cost. Find the part with the most spare room, shrink it one step, and run again. If a check turns red, undo that step. The budget is $2.60 an hour, and a design that passes costs a little less. One extra instance or one size too large fails on cost alone.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: stuck? One design that passes">

One design that passes all eleven checks: a load balancer of size small in three zones. Three app servers of size compute-small in two zones. One cache node of size memory-small at a 95% hit rate in two zones. A database of size memory-medium with an extra copy that takes over (a failover standby) in a second zone and one read-only copy, set to 98% reads. A queue, and three workers of size compute-small. The app server links to the cache, the database and the queue. The queue links to the workers. The workers link to the cache.

The board reports a ceiling of 4,800 requests a second, p99 near 60 ms, availability near 99.99% and about $2.54 an hour. The two checks that depend most on one choice are the cache checks, which need the read-only copy, and the worker check, which needs a third worker. Try taking each away, and read what the board says.

</GoDeeper>
