## The timestamp looks certain

Server A records a write at 12:00:00.000. Server B records another at 12:00:00.001. It is tempting to say the first write happened one millisecond before the second. That conclusion depends on both clocks agreeing more closely than one millisecond. If A's clock is fast and B's is slow, the order of their displayed timestamps can differ from the order people actually observed.

Imagine Alice removes Bob from a private sharing list. She waits until the application says the change is complete. Only then does she upload a photo. A history view must not show the photo at a time when Bob was still on the list. The two actions may reach different servers that never exchange a message with one another. Their wall clocks cannot safely settle the order unless the database accounts for uncertainty.

## Give each clock an interval

Instead of claiming that a server knows the exact time, let it report a lower and an upper bound. If its clock reads 20 milliseconds and may be wrong by 4 milliseconds, the real time is somewhere from 16 to 24 milliseconds. A second server might report a range that overlaps it. Overlap means the ranges alone cannot prove which event happened first. It does not prove the events were simultaneous.

The next lesson uses a live model to show how a database can wait until a chosen timestamp is definitely in the past before it replies. That wait has a latency cost. Your task is to explain why the wait protects Alice's observable order and when you would pay for that guarantee.

## Before the lab

Draw two short horizontal ranges that overlap. Put A's timestamp at the upper end of its range, then try the same for B. Ask whether the numbers alone can show that Alice's photo followed the privacy change. Keep your first answer so you can compare it with the simulation.
