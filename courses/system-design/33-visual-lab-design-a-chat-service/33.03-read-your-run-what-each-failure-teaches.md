A run is a small experiment, and every red check is a result. This chapter reads the scenes one at a time. For each, it says what the board did, what the numbers mean, and what to change.

All the numbers come from the lab board and its reference design: three gateways, three app servers, a cache, a two-node message store, a queue and three workers. Read them as relative. The shape matters, and the model milliseconds are not a stopwatch. There is no code lab for Dardasha yet, so none of these numbers was compared with a real stack.

## Scene 2: a connection server dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of three gateways" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Phones"] --> G1["Gateway 56%"]
  P --> G2["Gateway 56%"]
  P -.->|"stopped"| G3["Gateway"]`} />

One gateway of three goes dark, and its phones reconnect to the other two. Everyone is still served, and p99 rises from 72 to 90 ms. The two left are 83% busy, up from 56%. The lesson is one spare machine (N+1): size the fleet so that losing one still leaves enough.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 2">

On the board, three gateways of size compute-small can pass on 7,200 requests a second together, and each carries a third of 4,000. With one gone, the two left carry half each, which is 83% of what they can do, and a busier part makes every request wait longer. That is why p99 rises. With only two gateways to begin with, the one left would carry 4,000 of the 2,400 it can pass on, and only 60% of requests would be served. Even with both alive, two gateways are 83% busy and p99 is 90 ms, over the 74 ms target.

This is the rule from chapter 4.2 meeting a failure. The extra gateway is not waste, it is the part that makes the death invisible. If the board named the app server as the busiest part, the gateways would have been big enough. If it names the gateways, they are the ceiling.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 3: the cache dies

<MermaidDiagram title="When the cache goes, the store finds out" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers<br>4,000 req/s"] -.->|"cache gone"| C["Cache"]
  APP -->|"all 4,000 req/s"| ST[("Message store<br>2 nodes")]`} />

The cache answers 90% of requests, so the store sees about 400 a second and is 5% busy. When the cache dies, it sees all 4,000. With two nodes it rises to 50% busy and p99 moves from 72 to 76 ms. With one node it reaches 100% busy and p99 jumps to 400 ms. A cache protects the store only while it is there.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 3">

Each small store node can serve 4,000 requests a second on the board, so two nodes serve 8,000. With the cache alive, 400 requests a second is 5% of that. With the cache gone, 4,000 is 50%, and the wait added by the store rises from under 1 ms to 5 ms. One node of the same size carries exactly 4,000, so it is 100% busy and the wait added is 328 ms. Nothing was wrong while the cache worked, which is why the failure is easy to miss.

A design that needs its cache to survive has a hidden dependency. It must also carry the full load without it, or the cache's death becomes the store's death. The second node is the cheap way to buy that room, and the board charges you $0.15 an hour for it. One store node of size memory-medium has the same capacity and the same price as two small ones, so that is an equal choice. It leaves one machine in one place, though, which matters for availability (see the next fold).

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: zones, and why a cache hit rate matters less than you think">

Both the store and the cache are placed in two zones in the reference. With both store nodes in one zone, availability falls from 99.93% to 99.88%, under the 99.9% target. With the cache in one zone, it falls to 99.78%. A zone is a separate data centre, so spreading a part over two of them means one zone's trouble does not stop the part.

The cache hit rate is a setting, and the board shows that it matters little here. At 50% hits the reference still passes everything, with p99 at 73 ms. The hit rate helps while the store has room. What decides survival is whether the store can carry the load alone. A hit rate is a speed-up, and a second node is the safety.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 4: the alert worker dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Alerts wait while the worker is away" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers"] -->|"4,000 jobs/s"| Q["Queue<br>240,000 waiting after 60 s"]
  Q -.->|"workers stopped"| W["Workers"]
  W --> PH["Push alerts"]`} />

Chats do not notice a dead worker. The queue keeps each alert job. After 60 seconds at 4,000 a second it holds 240,000, and the workers must clear them as well as the new ones. Three workers take 75 seconds, two take 300, and one never catches up. A queue buys time only if the consumer has spare capacity.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 4, and what the queue costs">

Each worker of the board's default size handles 2,400 alerts a second. Three workers have 7,200 of capacity. They must handle the new 4,000 a second and the waiting alerts (the backlog), so they have 3,200 a second to spare, and 240,000 divided by 3,200 is 75 seconds. Two workers have 800 to spare and need 300 seconds. One worker has a capacity of 2,400, below the 4,000 arriving, so the waiting alerts never shrink. Four workers drain in 43 seconds, and cost $2.24 an hour against the $2.20 budget.

The queue is paid for per message. At 4,000 jobs a second it costs $0.59 an hour, the most of any single part in the reference, because the board sends one alert job for every request. A real design would send an alert job only for the share of messages whose friend is offline (about 10% in chapter 22.2). The board cannot do that, and the cost shows what a careless "send everything" design would pay.

</GoDeeper>

## Solution walkthrough

<MermaidDiagram title="From a failing run to the reference design" chart={`flowchart LR
  R["Run once"] --> S["Solution"]
  S --> P["Pick a part:<br>why, checks, without it"]
  S --> C["Compare with mine"]
  S --> K["Copy to my board"]`} />

After you press Run once, the Solution button opens the reference design on the same board. Pick any part to see why it is there, which checks it helps pass, and what fails without it. Compare with mine lists what differs from your design. Try first: a failing run you can explain teaches the most.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to use the solution well">

The solution is never locked behind a score, and nothing is recorded when you open it. Your own board stays untouched while you look: Back to my design returns to it, and Copy to my board asks before it replaces anything. The link labels in the solution show each hop on its own: reads going to the cache with the share the cache answers, and the misses going on to the store.

A good way to use it is to compare after one more try. Fix the failure you understand, run again, and only then press Compare with mine. It lists parts, settings and links that differ, in words such as "lower" and "smaller", and names the failing checks each one helps explain. It does not print the reference's numbers.

A code lab for Dardasha, with the same parts running for real, is coming. Module 30 has one for the short-link service already.

## What the board cannot show

<MermaidDiagram title="Four things the board leaves out" chart={`flowchart LR
  B["The board counts<br>requests a second"] --> X1["Message order"]
  B --> X2["Delivery receipts"]
  B --> X3["Reconnect storm"]
  B --> X4["Only some need an alert"]`} />

The board passes a design that serves everyone and still gets four things wrong. It does not check that every phone sees messages in the same order (chapter 22.4). It does not show the ticks for sent, delivered and read. A dead gateway's phones all return at once, which a real design must spread out. And only some messages need an alert. What would you add for each?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: four honest answers">

**Order.** Give each message the next number in its conversation, inside one store partition with one writer, and let the phone hold back a message until the one before it has arrived (chapter 22.4). The board has no numbers, so a design that skips this still passes.

**Receipts.** Sent, delivered and read are three separate facts, and a receipt can be a cursor ("I have up to 41") that covers every earlier message with one small update. The board counts requests and has no receipt traffic to size.

**A reconnect storm.** When one gateway dies, its 50,000 phones (the plan of chapter 22.3) reconnect at once, and a new connection costs far more than a request. Phones must wait a random time that grows after each failure, and a deploy should restart a few gateways at a time. The board shows the requests the other gateways take, and not the cost of the connections.

**Only some need an alert.** The board sends an alert job for every request. A real design checks the friend's online status first and queues a job only when the app is closed, which is a smaller queue, fewer workers and a smaller bill. Each answer costs something, and a good design says which cost it chose.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: in an interview">

**In an interview.** Walk the failures in order. Say what you lose when each part stops, and what each choice costs. "If a gateway dies, its phones reconnect to the others, because I kept one spare gateway, so the others stay below 85% busy. If the cache dies, the message store takes the full load, so I sized it to carry every request alone, in two zones. If the alert worker dies, alert jobs wait in the queue and drain after the restart, because the workers have spare capacity. And I would add a random wait before phones reconnect, because the board does not show what a thousand phones do at once."

Interviewers like the last sentence most. They want to hear that you know what your test did not cover, and that you can name what you would add.

</GoDeeper>
