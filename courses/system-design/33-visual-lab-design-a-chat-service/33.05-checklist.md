## Learning log

1. **Your first run:** Which checks did the starting design fail, and which one surprised you most?
2. **Your busiest part:** In your final design, which part is the busiest at peak, and how busy is it? What would you raise next, and what would it cost?
3. **Your cache:** What does your design do when the cache dies? Which part of it makes that survivable?
4. **Your alerts:** How many workers did you choose, and how long do they take to clear the alerts that wait during a 15 minute outage? What did the queue cost you?
5. **Your gaps:** The board cannot show message order, delivery receipts, a reconnect storm or the share of messages that need an alert. Which one would hurt your design most, and what would you add?
6. **Your first guess:** In chapter 33.1 you guessed how the wait changes when a till goes from half busy to 90 percent busy. Was your guess right, and where did you see the same thing on the board?

## Your gate answer: defend your design

This is the lab again, in words. You built a design on the board and it told you, check by check, where it was weak. A design review in a real team works the same way. Someone shows a design, someone else asks what happens when a part fails, and the designer answers with a number, not a feeling. Write the answer you would give in that room, about 200 words, about the design that passed or came closest.

A weak answer lists the parts: gateways, app servers, a cache, a key-value store, a queue and workers. It reads like a shopping list, and it could describe any design. A strong answer starts from a number the board showed you and lets that number decide the parts. Say which check was the hardest to pass, what you changed to pass it, and the number before and after. For example, the store at 100% busy and 400 ms in the cache scene, and 50% busy and 76 ms after you added a second node.

Then pick one trade-off and write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a second store node at $0.15 an hour, so the store survives the cache dying", or "two workers, which leave 4,400 alerts a second to spare to clear a build-up of waiting alerts". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good "when would you change it" line also has a trigger, such as "if the peak doubles" or "if the share of messages that need an alert rises from 1 in 10 to 1 in 2".

End with the failure your design is built to survive, and the one it is not. Every design has one. Saying so is not a weakness. It is the part of the answer that shows you looked. If you never thought about what 50,000 phones do when a gateway dies, or about message order, say so, and say what you would pay to cover it.

A good order for 200 words is the order of the board. Start with the hardest check and the number that moved. Then the trade-off. Last, the failure you did not fix. If you run short of words, cut the list of parts, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is tuning until the board is green without reading the reasons, and ending with every part at the largest size, which passes everything and fails on cost. The second is treating the cache as redundancy. A cache makes lookups fast, but it is not a copy that keeps the service up, and the cache scene is there to show it. The third is sizing the workers for the arrival rate and nothing more, so a waiting alert, once it exists, can never be cleared.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the choice to a check, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to the alerts if the queue itself fails, or why you chose two workers and not three. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.

modelAnswer: "The hardest check was 'slowest 1 in 100 while the cache is down'. My first design had a cache at a 90% hit rate and a message store of one small node. In the peak scene the store was almost idle. In the cache scene it went to 100% busy and p99 reached 400 ms. I added a second store node in a second zone. The store then ran at 50% busy without the cache, everyone was served, and p99 was 76 ms. Trade-off: I kept the cache and paid for the second node. It buys survival of the cache's death. It costs $0.15 an hour, about 10% of my $1.48 design. I would drop the second node if the cache were replicated, or if the load fell to a level one node serves alone. The design survives the loss of one gateway (three gateways, 83% busy with two left), the loss of the cache, and a 15 minute worker outage (two workers drain 360,000 alerts in 82 seconds). It does not cover message order or delivery receipts, which the board cannot show. It also assumes 1 request in 10 needs an alert, and does not spread out the 50,000 phones that reconnect when a gateway dies, so I would add a random wait on the phone."
