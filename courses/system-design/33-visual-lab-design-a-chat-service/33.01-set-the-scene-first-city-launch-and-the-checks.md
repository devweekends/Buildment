You send a message to a friend. Their phone is in a pocket with the app closed. A moment later it buzzes, and a small alert shows your words. Somewhere, a server saved your message, saw that your friend was not looking, and asked a queue to send an alert. Now picture one of those servers stopping at that exact moment. What would you want to happen? Keep that question in mind. In this module you build the back end of a chat app on a board, and then the board breaks it for you.

Dardasha (a made-up chat app from module 22) is launching in its first city. At the busiest moment, 4,000 requests a second reach it: people sending messages, opening chats and checking who is online. That is far below the 14,000 messages a second of chapter 22.1, and it is the size where one wrong choice already shows.

Before you read on, make a guess. A shop has one till. When it is half busy, a customer waits about a minute. When it is 90 percent busy, how long do you think a customer waits? Choose, and keep your guess in mind. The board will show you the answer with real numbers.

## Three jobs a chat service must do

<MermaidDiagram title="What Dardasha must do" chart={`flowchart LR
  U["Layla sends a message"] -->|"1. store it"| S["Dardasha"]
  S --> M[("Message store")]
  S -->|"2. who is online?"| P["Online dots"]
  S -.->|"3. friend's app is closed"| A["Alert to the phone"]`} />

Dardasha must **store** each message so it is never lost, **show who is online** (the green dot next to a friend), and **alert** a phone whose app is closed. These are the **functional** requirements: what the system does. On the board, each is a check that asks whether a request can travel from the client to the part that does the job.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the three checks look at">

The board does not run your code, because there is none. It looks at the links you drew. "A message is stored" is true when a request can travel from a client, through a gateway and an app server, to a key-value store that saves the message. A gateway is the server that holds the open line to each phone (chapter 22.2), and a key-value store keeps each item under a key, so it is a good fit for messages found by conversation and number.

"A friend's online dot can be read" is true when the app server can reach a cache, which is memory that answers fast. Chapter 22.4 explains why online status lives in a store with a time limit. "An alert reaches a closed app" is true when the request can travel on from the app server to a queue and from the queue to a worker, the part that sends the push alert (chapter 22.5). If a design cannot do one of the three jobs, nothing else about it matters, so these checks come first.

</GoDeeper>

## Eight ways to be good, or not

<MermaidDiagram title="One design, four scenes" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["1. Peak traffic<br>4,000 requests a second"] --> B["2. A connection server dies"]
  B --> C["3. The online-dot cache dies"]
  C --> D["4. The alert worker dies"]`} />

A design that does the three jobs can still be slow, fall over when a machine dies, or cost too much. These are the **non-functional** requirements: how well the system does its jobs. The board measures eight of them, in four scenes, when you press Run.

| In this scene | The board measures | What a good design shows |
|---|---|---|
| Peak traffic | capacity, the slowest 1 in 100 requests (p99), staying up (availability), cost | all 4,000 a second served, the slowest 1 in 100 under 74 ms, under a budget per hour |
| A connection server dies | the share of requests still served | everyone is still served with one gateway fewer |
| The cache dies | the share served, and the slowest 1 in 100 | everyone is still served, and the slowest 1 in 100 stays under 120 ms |
| The alert worker dies | alerts lost, and how long to catch up | no alert is lost, and the backlog drains within two minutes |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading the table, and what to do before you open the board">

The slowest 1 in 100 requests (**p99**) is the time that 99 out of 100 requests beat. It is the wait of the unlucky one in a hundred, and chapter 2.4 showed why that number matters more than the average. A **fleet** is a group of identical machines that share work, so that losing one does not stop the service. A **zone** is a separate data centre, so a fault in one does not touch the others.

When you press Run, the board plays all four scenes in order. In scene 2 one gateway turns red. In scene 3 the cache goes dark and the message store fills up. In scene 4 the worker stops, and alert jobs pile up in the queue. After the last scene, every check is listed as pass or fail, with the number the board measured, the number it needed, and the reason when it failed.

Before you open the board, look at the table and ask yourself one thing: which of these eight do you think a first, simple design would fail? There is no wrong guess. Writing it down makes the result more interesting.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the numbers come from, and how far to trust them">

The board is a model, not a cloud. Each part has a capacity that comes from its size and count, and a request waits longer as a part gets busier (the point where waiting climbs, called the utilisation knee in chapter 2.5). Prices are illustrative. Latency is in model milliseconds, so compare one design with another on the board and do not compare the board with a stopwatch.

The targets were tuned so that a good design passes with a little room, and a design with one missing idea fails for one clear reason. There is no code lab for Dardasha yet, so these targets were not compared with a real Docker stack the way the short-link lab (module 30) was. The lab spec records every number and how it was tuned. The board also leaves things out on purpose, and chapter 33.3 ends by naming them.

</GoDeeper>
