This page is the board and nothing else. Chapter 33.1 listed the eleven checks. Here you build a design, press Run, and watch it meet them. The board starts with a design that does the basic job: two gateways (the servers that hold the open line to each phone), two app servers (they run the chat rules) and one key-value store (it keeps the messages). It saves messages. It will not pass much more than that, and finding out why is the lab.

There is no single right answer. Many designs pass, and one that passes at $1.50 an hour beats one that passes at $5. Aim for green first, then make it cheaper without turning anything red.

## How the board works

<MermaidDiagram title="The board, in four moves" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Palette<br>on the left"] -->|"drag or tap"| B["Board<br>link the dots"]
  B -->|"hover a part, click Tune"| S["Settings cassette<br>beside the part"]
  B -->|"press Run"| R["Four scenes,<br>then every check"]`} />

The palette is on the left: drag a part onto the board, or tap it. Link two parts by dragging from a dot on one to a dot on another. Settings are not on the page until you ask: hover a part and click Tune on its corner, and a dark cassette opens beside it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cassette, names, and what Run shows">

The board only allows links where requests really flow, and it tells you when a link is not allowed. The settings drawer holds the part's size, how many there are, which zones they live in, and any setting special to that part, such as a cache's hit rate. Only one cassette is open at a time. On a phone it docks along the bottom while the part moves into view above it, and you tap a part to show its Tune key. Press Escape or the close key to put it away. The Names switch in the header changes the parts to real AWS names and sizes, with a line on what each one means. The numbers do not change.

Press Run and the board plays four scenes. Dots travel along the links, a part turns amber and then red as it fills, and a part that dies is marked DOWN. When the scenes end, each check shows pass or fail, the number the board measured, the number it needed, and why a failed check failed. Press Show on any check to replay its scene on the board.

</GoDeeper>

## Build it on the board

<Lab slug="hld-visual-chat" />

Run the starting design first, before you change anything. It has two gateways, two app servers and one key-value store. Read the failures in order. Each one names a part and a number, and each number points at a setting.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a failure">

A failure has three lines. The first says what was measured and what was needed. The second says why, usually naming the busiest part and how busy it was. For example, "the NoSQL store is 100% busy" says that as many requests arrive as that part can pass on, and anything more would wait in a growing line. Fix the part named, run again, and a different part is usually the next one named. That is the loop of chapter 4.9: the busiest part on the path sets the ceiling, and you move the ceiling by raising one part at a time.

Two failures deserve a closer look. "Everyone is still served when a connection server dies" runs the scene with one gateway fewer. A fleet of one has nothing left, so it fails at once. A fleet of two may serve only 60% if each gateway cannot carry the whole load alone. And "No alerts are lost when the worker dies" needs the alerts to wait somewhere while the worker is down, which is what a queue is for, and then needs the workers to be fast enough, once back, to catch up on the waiting alerts (the backlog) as well as the new ones.

</GoDeeper>

## A gateway holds the lines

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of three gateways" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Phones"] --> G1["Gateway"]
  P --> G2["Gateway"]
  P -.->|"its phones move"| G3["Gateway: down"]`} />

Every phone keeps one open line to a gateway, so every request passes through one. When a gateway dies, its phones reconnect to the others. Those gateways must then carry the whole peak. Count what is left, not what you started with.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the gateway is a separate part">

In chapter 22.2 the gateways only hold lines, and the chat service holds the rules. They are separate because a gateway has state (its open lines) and the chat service has none. Stopping a gateway drops its phones, so it is treated with more care than an app server.

On the board, a gateway is a fleet with a size and a count. It does not count open lines, so the board shows only the request side of the story: how many requests a second the fleet can pass on, and how that changes when one machine is gone. Chapter 33.3 returns to what that leaves out.

</GoDeeper>

## Put a cache beside the store

<MermaidDiagram title="Where 4,000 requests a second go" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App servers<br>4,000 req/s"] --> C["Cache<br>90% answered"]
  APP -->|"misses: about 400 req/s"| ST[("Message store")]`} />

Link a cache from the app server and set its hit rate. A hit means the cache answered from memory, so the store is not asked. At 90% hits the store sees about 400 requests a second instead of 4,000. Then run again and watch the cache scene.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cache scene">

In the cache scene the cache goes dark and every request asks the store. A store that was sized only for the 400 misses cannot take 4,000, so the checks fail and the board names the store as the busiest part. The cache is a speed-up, and the design must also carry the load without it. Chapter 33.3 follows this failure in detail.

On this board the cache stands for the memory that holds who is online and the latest messages of busy chats. A real message is always written to the store, and the board does not show that: it counts the store's load as the cache's misses. The lab spec says so in its limits.

</GoDeeper>

## Alerts wait in a queue

<MermaidDiagram title="Alerts that wait for a worker" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["App server"] -->|"1 request in 10"| Q["Queue"]
  Q --> W["Workers"]
  W -.->|"push alert"| P["Phone with the app closed"]`} />

Link the app server to a queue, and the queue to workers. About 1 request in 10 leaves an alert job in the queue, and a worker sends the push alert. Try one, two and three workers and compare the drain time and the cost.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why only 1 request in 10 reaches the queue">

In chapter 22.2 about 10% of deliveries find a phone off, so about 1 request in 10 needs an alert. The lab sets that share on the queue: 400 of the 4,000 requests a second leave a job, and the worker behind the queue gets the same 400. The link label on the board says "10% of requests send work here". The other links, to the cache and the store, still carry the whole stream.

The share is small, so the queue and the workers are cheap while everything is healthy. The worker check is about the other side: a 15 minute outage lets 360,000 alerts pile up, and the workers must clear them as well as the new ones.

</GoDeeper>

## Make it cheaper

<MermaidDiagram title="Pass first, then trim" chart={`flowchart LR
  G["All checks green"] --> T["Trim the part with spare room"]
  T --> R["Run again"]
  R -->|"a check turns red"| U["Undo that trim"]
  R -->|"still green"| T`} />

When every check is green, look at the cost. Find the part with the most spare room, shrink it one step, and run again. If a check turns red, undo that step. The budget is $1.55 an hour. One extra instance or one size too large fails on cost alone.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: stuck? One design that passes">

One design that passes all eleven checks: three gateways of size compute-small. Three app servers of size compute-small in two zones. One cache node of size memory-small at a 90% hit rate in two zones. A key-value store of two nodes of size memory-small in two zones. A queue, and two workers of size compute-small. The gateways link to the app servers. The app servers link to the cache, the store and the queue. The queue links to the workers.

The board reports a ceiling of 7,200 requests a second, p99 near 72 ms, availability near 99.93% and about $1.48 an hour. The checks that depend most on one choice are the cache checks, which need a store that can carry all 4,000 requests alone, and the worker check, which needs a second worker. Try taking each away, and read what the board says.

</GoDeeper>

  - "Workers send alerts after the request is done. They do not change how many requests reach the store."
