## Learning log

1. **Your first run:** Which checks did the starting design fail, and which one surprised you most?
2. **Your busiest part:** In your final design, which part is the busiest at peak, and how busy is it? What would you raise next, and what would it cost?
3. **Your CDN:** What does your design do when a CDN network stops? Which part of it makes that survivable, and what did it cost?
4. **Your uploads:** How long does your design take to convert the waiting jobs after the workers return? How many workers did that need?
5. **Your hit rate:** Where did the number on your CDN come from? What would change in your design if it fell to 0.3?
6. **Your first guess:** In chapter 38.1 you guessed how the wait changes when a till goes from half busy to 90 percent busy. Was your guess right, and where did you see the same thing on the board?

## Your gate answer: defend your design

This is the lab again, in words. You built a design on the board and it told you, check by check, where it was weak. A design review in a real team works the same way. Someone shows a design, someone else asks what happens when a part fails, and the designer answers with a number, not a feeling. Write the answer you would give in that room, about 200 words, about the design that passed or came closest.

A weak answer lists the parts: two CDNs, a load balancer, two app servers, a database, a queue and two workers. It reads like a shopping list, and it could describe any design. A strong answer starts from a number the board showed you and lets that number decide the parts. Say which check was the hardest to pass, what you changed to pass it, and the number before and after. For example, one CDN serving 0.0% when it stopped, and everyone served after you added a second.

Then pick one trade-off and write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a second CDN at $0.02 an hour on the board, so a network outage serves everyone, though a real second CDN starts with a cold cache", or "two workers, which leave 4,050 jobs a second to spare, so 45,000 waiting jobs are converted in 12 seconds". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good "when would you change it" line also has a trigger, such as "if the hit rate falls to 0.3" or "if conversion jobs arrive twice as fast".

End with the failure your design is built to survive, and the one it is not. Every design has one. Saying so is not a weakness. It is the part of the answer that shows you looked. If your database has one standby and no second region, or if you set the hit rate from hope and not from the logs, say so, and say what you would pay to remove it.

A good order for 200 words is the order of the board. Start with the hardest check and the number that moved. Then the trade-off. Last, the failure you did not fix. If you run short of words, cut the list of parts, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is tuning until the board is green without reading the reasons, and ending with every part at the largest size, which passes everything and fails on cost. The second is typing a high hit rate to make the bill small. The board accepts it and every check passes, and the design is a design for a service that does not exist. The third is sizing the workers for the arrival rate and nothing more, so a build-up of waiting jobs, once it exists, can never be cleared.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the choice to a check, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to a video if the database is down when an upload finishes, or why you chose two zones for the app servers. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.

modelAnswer: "The hardest check was 'everyone is still served when one CDN network goes down'. My first design had one CDN, and when it stopped the board served 0.0 percent of requests. I added a second CDN, linked the same way: the client, the storage and the load balancer. When one stopped, the other took all 1,500 requests a second and everyone was served. On the board it costs about $0.02 an hour, because the two share the same requests, but a real second CDN costs a contract and starts with a cold cache, so I would watch the hit rate in the first hour. I would drop it only if one provider offered a guarantee I trusted. The design survives one lost CDN, one lost app server (31 percent busy with one left), and a 60 second worker outage (two workers convert 45,000 waiting jobs in 12 seconds).
