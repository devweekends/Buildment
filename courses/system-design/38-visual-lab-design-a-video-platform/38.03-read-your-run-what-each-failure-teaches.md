A run is a small experiment, and every red check is a result. This chapter reads the scenes one at a time. For each, it says what the board did and what to change. There is no real stack to compare with yet, because the code lab for this case study is coming. So every number below comes from the board, and the board is a model. Read the numbers as shapes, not as milliseconds.

## Scene 2: a CDN network goes down

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of two CDN networks" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["Client<br>1,500 req/s"] --> A["CDN A"]
  C -.->|"stopped"| B["CDN B"]
  A -->|"takes all 1,500"| O["Storage and<br>app servers"]`} />

Two CDNs share the 1,500 requests, 750 each. One stops, and the other takes all of them. Everyone is still served and p99 stays at 50 ms. With a single CDN there is no other way in, and the board serves 0.0%. The CDN protects your servers, and it is also a single door.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 2">

On the board, the second CDN adds $0.02 an hour, because the two share the same requests and the request charge is the same. In real life it adds more: a contract, a second set of settings, and a cold cache. A CDN that has just taken over has not yet stored the popular videos, so its hit rate starts low, and your servers see more misses. The board keeps the old hit rate, so it is kinder than real life here.

There is another way to survive a lost CDN: let the viewers go straight to your own load balancer. The board lets you try it by linking the client to the load balancer as well. The load balancer then receives the full 1,500 plus the misses from the CDN, 2,250 a second. The slowest 1 in 100 requests takes 130 ms, the worker check fails because more jobs reach the queue, and the cost is $9.86 an hour. It can be made to work with bigger parts, but your servers must then carry what the CDN used to carry, and that is what the CDN was there to avoid.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 3: an app server dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of two app servers" chart={`flowchart LR
  LB["Load balancer"] --> A1["App server"]
  LB -.->|"stopped"| A2["App server"]`} />

One app server of two goes dark, and the load balancer sends its share to the other. It goes from 16% to 31% busy. Everyone is still served, and p99 rises from 50 to 55 ms. With one server there is no other, and the board serves 0.0%. The lesson is one spare machine (N+1).

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 3">

The app servers are not the busy part. At peak only 750 requests a second reach them, because the CDN answered the rest. So a single server could carry the load, and still the design needs two. The check is not about speed. It asks whether anything is left when one stops, and a fleet of one has nothing left. Chapter 4.2 calls this N+1: size the fleet so that losing one still leaves enough.

Where the two servers live matters too. Two servers in one zone fail together if that zone fails. On the board, that costs availability: 99.91% against 99.96% when they sit in two zones, under the 99.95% target. A third server would serve very well but costs $7.63 an hour, over the $7.60 budget. A spare machine is the right size when it is the smallest one that the check accepts.

</GoDeeper>

## Scene 4: the transcoding workers stop

<MermaidDiagram title="The queue fills while the workers are away" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App server"] -->|"750 jobs/s"| Q["Queue<br>backlog grows"]
  Q -.->|"stopped"| W["Workers"]
  W --> S[("Object storage")]`} />

Plays and uploads do not notice a dead worker. The queue keeps each job. After 60 seconds it holds 45,000, and the workers must clear them as well as the new jobs. Two workers take 12 seconds, one takes 28, and the board allows 20. A queue buys time only if the workers have spare capacity.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind scene 4">

Each worker of the default size handles 2,400 jobs a second on the board. Two workers have 4,800 of capacity. They must handle the new 750 a second, so 4,050 a second are spare, and 45,000 divided by 4,050 is about 12 seconds. One worker has 2,400, so 1,650 are spare, and the same backlog takes 28 seconds. With no queue at all, the jobs have nowhere to wait: the board counts 90,000 jobs lost in the 60 seconds, and an upload can never become playable.

A real worker is far slower. Chapter 27.3 splits a 5 minute video into 250 encode jobs of about 3.6 seconds each (course numbers), and its answer to a worker that dies halfway is that the job goes back in line and runs again under the same output name. A job may run twice and is never lost. The board does not show that, but it does show the part that matters first: the queue holds the work, and the workers need room to catch up.

</GoDeeper>

## The hit rate is not yours to pick

<MermaidDiagram title="One number moves the whole bill" chart={`flowchart LR
  H["CDN hit rate"] -->|"0.3"| A["Storage $3.03/h<br>total $8.42/h"]
  H -->|"0.5, from the logs"| B["Storage $2.17/h<br>total $7.51/h"]
  H -->|"0.95, a wish"| C["total $5.47/h<br>every check passes"]`} />

Change the CDN's hit rate and the board changes with it. At 0.3 the total is $8.42 an hour, over the budget. At 0.95 every check passes for $5.47. Neither number is a design choice. Lamha's catalogue sets it, and the logs measure it. A design reviewer will ask where your number comes from.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a few points of hit rate matter so much">

Chapter 27.2 asked what happens when the hit rate falls from 95% to 90%. The misses double, from 5 in 100 to 10 in 100, and your servers and storage carry twice as much. The same arithmetic runs here at a lower level. At 0.5, half of the requests miss. At 0.3, seven in ten miss, 40% more than before, and the storage bill rises from $2.17 to $3.03 an hour.

Chapter 27.4 gave the reason the number is hard to know: views are skewed, a few videos get most of them, and a long tail of videos gets almost none. Each rarely watched video pays the full trip to storage. A new catalogue, with a longer tail than an old one, has a lower hit rate. So the number should be measured, kept under review, and treated as a risk when the catalogue grows.

</GoDeeper>

## Solution walkthrough

<MermaidDiagram title="From a failing run to the reference design" chart={`flowchart LR
  R["Run once"] --> S["Solution"]
  S --> P["Pick a part:<br>why, checks, without it"]
  S --> C["Compare with mine"]
  S --> K["Copy to my board"]`} />

After you press Run once, the Solution button opens the reference design on the same board. Pick any part to see why it is there, which checks it helps pass, and what fails without it. Compare with mine lists what differs from your design. Try first: a failing run you can explain teaches the most.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to use the solution well">

The solution is never locked behind a score, and nothing is recorded when you open it. Your own board stays untouched while you look: Back to my design returns to it, and Copy to my board asks before it replaces anything.

A good way to use it is to compare after one more try. Fix the failure you understand, run again, and only then press Compare with mine. It lists parts, settings and links that differ, in words such as "lower" and "smaller", and names the failing checks each one helps explain. It does not print the reference's numbers.

The same design will run for real in a code lab (coming soon), with the same parts and the same checks, so a design you built on the board can be replayed there.

</GoDeeper>

## The failures the board cannot show

<MermaidDiagram title="What the board sees and what Lamha pays for" chart={`flowchart LR
  B["The board<br>counts requests"] --> R["1,500 requests a second"]
  L["Lamha<br>sends bytes"] --> T["12.5 Tbit/s at peak<br>(course number)"]
  R -.->|"no bytes in the model"| T`} />

The board counts requests and has no bytes. It cannot show bandwidth, the bill for data sent out of the cloud, or the player choosing a quality from the network speed. On the board a CDN costs more per request than storage. In real life it pays for itself in bytes. What is the first thing you would add to the model?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: four things the model leaves out">

**Bandwidth.** Chapter 27.1 found that Lamha sends about 12.5 Tbit/s at peak. If one sending machine pushes 100 Gbit/s (a course number), 125 machines do nothing but send video. That, and not the number of requests, is why a CDN exists. The board has no bytes, so it cannot see the load that makes the CDN worth its price.

**Data out (egress).** Clouds charge for each gigabyte that leaves them. For video that line is often the largest on the bill (chapter 27.1). The board charges per request, so it gets the order of the cost lines wrong: it shows the CDN and storage as large because they handle many requests, and the real reason is the bytes.

**Adaptive bitrate.** The player picks one of five versions, from 0.4 to 6 Mbit/s, and changes it as the network changes (chapter 27.4). That choice, and the rule for flapping between versions, happens in the player. The board has no player and no network speed.

**Resumable uploads and job time.** A 300 MB upload sent in chunks that resume after a drop (chapter 27.3), and a transcoding job that takes seconds, are the hard parts of the upload path. The board treats every job as a request that takes milliseconds, so it checks the shape of the queue and not the size of the fleet. Also, its play check accepts any route from the CDN to storage, so read its green tick as a link test and not a proof.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: in an interview">

**In an interview.** Walk the failures in order. Say what you lose when each part stops, and what each choice costs. "If a CDN network stops, I want a second one, so the other takes the whole load, and I know the second one starts cold. If an app server dies, the other carries on, because I kept one spare. If the transcoding workers stop, uploads wait in the queue and drain after the restart, because the workers have spare capacity. And I would say that my hit rate comes from logs, not from hope."

Interviewers like the last sentence most. They want to hear that you know which number your design leans on, and where it comes from.

</GoDeeper>
