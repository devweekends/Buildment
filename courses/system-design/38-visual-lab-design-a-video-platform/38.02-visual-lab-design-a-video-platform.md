This page is the board and nothing else. Chapter 38.1 listed the nine checks. Here you build a design, press Run, and watch it meet them. The board starts with the smallest design that works: one CDN, one load balancer (it spreads requests over the servers), one app server (it runs the code), one database and object storage. It already plays videos and records uploads. It will not pass much more than that, and finding out why is the lab.

There is no single right answer. Many designs pass, and one that passes at $7.51 an hour beats one that passes at $9. Aim for green first, then make it cheaper without turning anything red.

## How the board works

<MermaidDiagram title="The board, in four moves" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Palette<br>on the left"] -->|"drag or tap"| B["Board<br>link the dots"]
  B -->|"hover a part, click Tune"| S["Settings cassette<br>beside the part"]
  B -->|"press Run"| R["Four scenes,<br>then every check"]`} />

The palette is on the left: drag a part onto the board, or tap it. Link two parts by dragging from a dot on one to a dot on another. Settings are not on the page until you ask: hover a part and click Tune on its corner, and a dark cassette opens beside it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cassette, names, and what Run shows">

The board only allows links where requests really flow, and it tells you when a link is not allowed. The settings drawer holds the part's size, how many there are, which zones they live in, and any setting special to that part, such as a CDN's hit rate (the share of requests it answers itself) or a database's read-only copies. Only one cassette is open at a time. On a phone it docks along the bottom while the part moves into view above it, and you tap a part to show its Tune key. Press Escape or the close key to put it away. The Names switch in the header changes the parts to real AWS names and sizes, with a line on what each one means. The numbers do not change.

Press Run and the board plays four scenes. Dots travel along the links, a part turns amber and then red as it fills, and a part that dies is marked DOWN. When the scenes end, each check shows pass or fail, the number the board measured, the number it needed, and why a failed check failed. Press Show on any check to replay its scene on the board.

</GoDeeper>

## Build it on the board

<Lab slug="hld-visual-video-platform" />

Run the starting design first, before you change anything. Read the failures in order. Each one names a part and a number, and each number points at a setting. Two things to know: the CDN's hit rate is 0.5, taken from Lamha's logs, and the worker's batch size does nothing here, so leave it at 1.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a failure">

A failure has three lines. The first says what was measured and what was needed. The second says why, usually naming the busiest part and how busy it was. For example, "App server is down: requests through it fail" says that the design had one app server, and when it stopped, nothing was left to answer. Fix the part named, run again, and a different part is usually the next one named. That is the loop of chapter 4.9: the busiest part on the path sets the ceiling, and you move the ceiling by raising one part at a time.

Three failures deserve a closer look. "Everyone is still served when one CDN network goes down" runs the scene with the first CDN gone. A design with one CDN has nothing left, so it fails at once. "Everyone is still served when one app server dies" does the same to the app fleet. And "No conversion jobs are lost when the workers stop" needs the jobs to wait somewhere while the workers are away, which is what a queue is for, and then needs the workers to be quick enough, once back, to catch up on the waiting jobs (the backlog) as well as the new ones.

</GoDeeper>

## Put the CDN in front

<MermaidDiagram title="Where 1,500 requests a second go" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["Client<br>1,500 req/s"] --> CDN["CDN<br>hit rate 0.5"]
  CDN -->|"750 req/s answered at the edge"| C
  CDN -->|"misses: 750 req/s"| O["Storage and<br>app servers"]`} />

Every request enters at a CDN. A hit (the copy is at the edge) never goes on. Only misses reach your own parts. At a hit rate of 0.5, half of 1,500 a second, 750, go on. The board charges the CDN for each request it handles, so this part is a large share of the bill. Look at the cost of each part after you run.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the hit rate is a measurement, not a setting">

The board lets you type any hit rate from 0 to 1, and it is a free setting. At 0.5 the reference design costs $7.51 an hour. At 0.3 the storage bill rises, and the total is $8.42, over the budget. At 0.95 every check passes for $5.47 an hour, and that proves nothing.

In a real service the hit rate is not chosen. It is measured, and it depends on the catalogue. Chapter 27.4 showed that a long catalogue has a long tail of videos that few people watch, and a copy of a video nobody asks for is never a hit. So the lab fixes the number at what Lamha's logs say, 0.5, and asks you to design for it. If you type a bigger number, you are designing for a service that does not exist.

</GoDeeper>

## Add a second CDN

<MermaidDiagram title="Two CDNs, one can fail" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["Client"] -->|"750 req/s"| A["CDN A"]
  C -->|"750 req/s"| B["CDN B"]
  A --> O["Storage and<br>app servers"]
  B --> O`} />

With one CDN, the client has no other way in. If that CDN stops, nothing is served. Add a second CDN and link it the same way: the client, the storage and the load balancer. In peaceful times the two share the 1,500 requests, and when one stops, the other takes them all. The second one costs only its hourly fee.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a second CDN costs so little here">

The board charges a CDN for each request it handles. Two CDNs that share the requests handle the same number in total, so the request charge does not change. Only the small hourly fee is added: $0.02. In real life a second CDN is not free: you pay for a contract, you run two sets of settings, and a CDN that has just taken over starts with an empty cache, so its hit rate is low for a while. The board has none of that, so it makes the second CDN look cheaper than it is. Keep that in mind when you read the cost.

</GoDeeper>

## Converting uploads is a queue's job

<MermaidDiagram title="Uploads wait for a worker" chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["App server"] -->|"750 jobs/s"| Q["Queue<br>holds the jobs"]
  Q --> W["Workers<br>convert"]
  W -->|"playable versions"| S[("Object storage")]`} />

Converting a video takes time, so the upload does not wait for it. The app server puts a job on a queue and carries on. Workers take jobs and save the playable versions in object storage. Link them in that order. If the workers stop, the jobs wait, and the workers must clear them as well as the new jobs.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how many workers it takes">

Each worker of the default size handles 2,400 jobs a second on the board. Two workers have 4,800 of capacity. They must handle the 750 new jobs a second, so 4,050 a second are spare. In a 60 second outage 45,000 jobs build up (750 times 60), and 45,000 divided by 4,050 is about 12 seconds. One worker has only 1,650 a second to spare, so it needs 28 seconds, over the 20 allowed.

The real transcoding of chapter 27.3 is different in size. A 5 minute video is 250 small encode jobs, each about 3.6 seconds of one machine (course numbers), so Lamha needs thousands of workers at peak. The board's worker runs a job at the speed of any request, so the board checks the shape of the problem (a queue holds jobs, and workers need spare capacity to catch up), not the number of machines.

</GoDeeper>

## Make it cheaper

<MermaidDiagram title="Pass first, then trim" chart={`flowchart LR
  G["All checks green"] --> T["Trim the part with spare room"]
  T --> R["Run again"]
  R -->|"a check turns red"| U["Undo that trim"]
  R -->|"still green"| T`} />

When every check is green, look at the cost. Most of it is the CDNs and the storage, which you can change only through the hit rate. So trim the parts you size: find the one with the most spare room, shrink it one step, and run again. If a check turns red, undo that step. The budget is $7.60 an hour, so one extra instance fails on cost alone.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: stuck? One design that passes">

One design that passes all nine checks: two CDNs at a hit rate of 0.5, each linked from the client and to object storage and to the load balancer. A load balancer of size general-small in two zones. Two app servers of size compute-small in two zones. A database of size memory-small with one read-only copy (a read replica), an extra copy that takes over (a failover standby) in a second zone, set to 95% reads. A queue, and two workers of size compute-small, linked to object storage. The app server links to the database and the queue. The queue links to the workers.

The board reports a ceiling of 5,053 requests a second, p99 near 50 ms, availability near 99.96% and about $7.51 an hour. The checks that depend most on one choice are the CDN check, which needs the second CDN, the p99 check, which needs the read-only copy, and the worker check, which needs a second worker. Try taking each away, and read what the board says.

</GoDeeper>
