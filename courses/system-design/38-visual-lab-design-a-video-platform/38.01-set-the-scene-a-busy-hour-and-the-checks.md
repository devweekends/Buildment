You press play on a video. The picture appears in about a second, and the file is not on your phone. It is stored far away, yet it did not travel all that way for you. A copy was waiting close to your home. Now picture the network that holds that copy stopping at the exact moment you press play. What would you want to happen? Keep that question in mind. In this module you build the video platform on a board, and then the board breaks it for you.

Lamha (a made-up video platform from module 27) is a very big service. The board shows one small slice of it: one region, at its busiest second. 1,500 requests a second arrive at the front door, a CDN. A CDN (a content delivery network) is a set of machines near the viewers that keep copies of popular videos. That is far smaller than module 27's billions of views a day, and it is the size where one wrong choice already shows.

Before you read on, make a guess. A shop has one till. When it is half busy, a customer waits about a minute. When it is 90 percent busy, how long do you think a customer waits? Choose, and keep your guess in mind. The board will show you the answer with real numbers.

## Three jobs a video platform must do

<MermaidDiagram title="What Lamha must do" chart={`flowchart LR
  V["A viewer presses play"] -->|"1. play"| L["Lamha"]
  L -->|"pieces of video"| V
  U["An uploader sends a file"] -->|"2. start and record"| L
  L -.->|"3. convert so it can play"| P[("Playable versions")]`} />

Lamha must **play** a video (send it as small pieces from a CDN), **record** an upload (save a row about the new video), and **convert** it (make versions that play on any screen). These are the **functional** requirements: what the system does. On the board, each is a check that asks whether a request can travel from the client to the part that does the job.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the three checks look at">

The board does not run any code, because there is none. It looks at the links you drew. "A video plays" is true when a request can travel from a client, through a CDN, to object storage (a store for big files, such as video). "An upload is started and recorded" is true when a request can travel from a client, through a load balancer and an app server, to a database that saves the row. In chapter 27.2, the file itself skips the app server and goes straight to storage, so the app server only starts and finishes the upload.

"An upload becomes playable" is true when a conversion job can travel from the app server to a queue, from the queue to a worker, and from the worker to object storage. Converting a video into several versions is called **transcoding** (chapter 27.3). It is slow, so it never happens inside the upload request. A queue holds each job until a worker is free.

If a design cannot do one of the three jobs, nothing else about it matters, so these checks come first.

</GoDeeper>

## Six ways to be good, or not

<MermaidDiagram title="One design, four scenes" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["1. Peak traffic<br>1,500 requests a second"] --> B["2. A CDN network goes down"]
  B --> C["3. An app server dies"]
  C --> D["4. The transcoding workers stop"]`} />

A design that does the three jobs can still be slow, fall over when a machine dies, or cost too much. These are the **non-functional** requirements: how well the system does its jobs. The board measures six of them, in four scenes, when you press Run.

| In this scene | The board measures | What a good design shows |
|---|---|---|
| Peak traffic | the slowest 1 in 100 requests (p99), staying up (availability), cost | p99 under 55 ms, availability at least 99.95%, a cost under $7.60 an hour |
| A CDN network goes down | the share of requests still served | everyone is still served |
| An app server dies | the share of requests still served | everyone is still served with one server fewer |
| The transcoding workers stop | jobs lost, and how long to catch up | no job is lost, and the backlog is converted within 20 seconds of the workers returning |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading the table, and what to do before you open the board">

The slowest 1 in 100 requests (**p99**) is the time that 99 out of 100 requests beat. It is the wait of the unlucky one in a hundred, and chapter 2.4 showed why that number matters more than the average. **Availability** is the share of time the service works. A **zone** is one separate data centre, so a part spread over two zones survives the loss of one. A **backlog** is the work that has piled up while the part that does it was away.

When you press Run, the board plays all four scenes in order. In scene 2 one CDN turns red. In scene 3 one app server turns red. In scene 4 the workers stop, and jobs pile up in the queue. After the last scene, every check is listed as pass or fail, with the number the board measured, the number it needed, and the reason when it failed.

Before you open the board, look at the table and ask yourself one thing: which of these do you think a first, simple design would fail? There is no wrong guess. Writing it down makes the result more interesting.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the numbers come from, and how far to trust them">

The board is a model, not a cloud. Each part has a capacity that comes from its size and count, and a request waits longer as a part gets busier (the point where waiting climbs, called the utilisation knee in chapter 2.5). Prices are illustrative. Latency is in model milliseconds, so compare one design with another on the board and do not compare the board with a stopwatch.

One thing is very different from a real video service. The board counts requests, never bytes. It charges the CDN for every request, and the real cost of video is the data it sends. So the board gets the shape of the failures right, and the shape of the bill wrong. Chapter 38.3 comes back to this, because it is the most important thing the board cannot show.

</GoDeeper>

  - "An outage is exactly the kind of event a design must expect. Surviving one is a requirement the board tests on purpose."
  - "The CDN only keeps copies for speed. The videos live in object storage. Where data is kept is a design choice, not a kind of requirement."
