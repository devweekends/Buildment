## Your gate answer: report one failure

An incident report is the most useful thing an engineer writes after something breaks, and it has a shape. It says what happened, what people saw, what it cost behind the scenes, and what you would change. In this lab you broke five things on purpose. Pick one of them and write the report about it in about 200 words.

A weak report lists facts: "I stopped Redis. Clicks were lost." A strong report starts from what the person clicking a link saw (here, nothing), says what the system lost without anyone noticing, gives one number from your own run, and compares it with what the board predicted. It then says which design you would rather be on call for, and why. The board and the real stack do not always agree, and the disagreement is the interesting part of the report.

Use the same order every time. First, what people saw. Second, what the system lost, with one number. Third, what the visual lab board said about the same failure, and where it was right or too kind. Fourth, the choice you would make and what it costs, written in the shape you used in module 30: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good cost line has a number or a name, such as "about 2 ms on every redirect to keep the clicks safe" or "a second Redis to hold the queue, one more container to run".

Be careful with three common mistakes. The first is to treat your milliseconds as the truth. They are the truth about your laptop on that day, and the comparison between designs on the same laptop is the part that carries over. The second is to report a failure as a pass because no request failed. A design can serve every request and still lose clicks, or serve them ten times slower. Look for what else was lost. The third is to copy the author's explanation instead of explaining your own result. A mentor can tell, and the report is worth more when it is yours.

Check your report against the learning log above: each answer there should show up in it as a fact, a number or a choice. Last, say what you would try next. Every real investigation ends with a new question. Maybe it is how the design behaves when the database is slow instead of dead, or what happens when two failures overlap. A good next question shows that you understood where the picture is still incomplete.

Write the report in your own words and keep it short, because a short report that names one number is worth more than a long one that names none. A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can pick different failures and both pass, as long as each starts from what people saw, gives a real number, compares it with the board and defends one choice. A mentor may ask a follow-up, such as what would change if the business charged per click, or how you would count clicks without losing them when Redis fails. Answer it by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work.
