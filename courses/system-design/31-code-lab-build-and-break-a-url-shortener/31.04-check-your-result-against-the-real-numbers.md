In the tasks you changed one thing, made a guess and read a number. Now you check that number. The real numbers below were measured on the author's laptop, so yours will differ: a quiet laptop is faster, a busy one is two or three times slower. Compare shapes, not milliseconds. Where a result does not depend on speed (did a request fail, were clicks lost), your number should match closely.

Each beat names the check on the visual lab board that the same result mirrors. The repository's contract file lists these checks with stable ids, so a design built on the board can be replayed here and scored the same way.

## Speed: cache against database

<MermaidDiagram title="Same question, two answers" chart={`flowchart LR
  Q["Open a short link"] --> C["Cache hit<br>about 6 ms"]
  Q --> D["Read the database<br>about 15 ms"]`} />

With 16 requests in flight, a redirect answered by the cache took about 6 ms, and one that read the database took about 15 ms. The slowest 1 in 100 (p99) went from about 17 ms to about 68 ms. On the board this is the speed check on the slowest 1 in 100 redirects.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers and their check ids">

| What was measured | Real stack | Check id in the contract | Board check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical wait (p50), cache hit | 6.0 ms (2.1 ms one at a time) | `redirect_p50_ms` | speed |
| Typical wait (p50), database read | 15.4 ms (3.3 ms one at a time) | `redirect_p50_ms` | speed |
| Slowest 1 in 100 (p99), cache hit | 16.7 ms | `redirect_p99_ms` | Slowest 1 in 100 redirects is fast enough |
| Slowest 1 in 100 (p99), database read | 68.2 ms | `redirect_p99_ms` | same |
| Requests a second, 16 in flight | 1,558 cold, 2,454 warm | `throughput_rps` | Serves the whole peak |

One at a time the cache is only 1.6 times faster. Under load it is 2.6 times faster at the middle and 4 times faster at the slowest 1 in 100. The cache matters most when many people ask at once. Speed targets depend on your machine, so judge these rows by comparing designs on your own laptop. A useful habit is to run each setup three times and write down all three numbers. If the three runs of the same setup differ by more than the gap between the two setups, the gap is noise, not a result. Cold means the cache started empty, and warm means it was already full of the links being opened, so a cold run is always slower. Run the cold case first, then the warm one, and say which you measured.

</GoDeeper>

## A copy of the API dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Losing one of three" chart={`flowchart LR
  G["Gateway"] --> A1["API copy"]
  G --> A2["API copy"]
  G -.->|"stopped"| A3["API copy"]`} />

Stopping one API copy failed none of 4,344 redirects. Stopping two failed none of 4,330. The gateway tried another copy when one did not answer. This mirrors the board check that everyone is still served when one app server dies.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what changed, and what did not">

| What was measured | Real stack | Check id | Board check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Failed redirects, one copy stopped | 0 of 4,344 | `survives_replica_loss` | Everyone is still served when one app server dies |
| Failed redirects, two copies stopped | 0 of 4,330 | `survives_replica_loss` | same |
| Slowest 1 in 100, none, one and two stopped | 8.7 ms, 7.5 ms, 19.6 ms | none | the board's rise from 58 to 77 ms |

Nobody saw an error. What changed was the slowest 1 in 100 once two of three copies were gone, because the one left was busier. This is the shape to look for in your own run: no failures, and a slower tail. If you did see failures, check that the gateway had time to notice the stopped copy before you read the number. The check allows at most 0.1 percent failures, because one request caught in the middle of a stop is noise, not a flaw in the design.

</GoDeeper>

## The cache dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Redis stops" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["API"] -.->|"stopped"| R["Redis"]
  A --> P[("Postgres<br>asked every time")]`} />

With Redis stopped, no redirect failed, and the share answered from the cache fell from 100 percent to 15 percent. The slowest 1 in 100 went from 8.7 ms to 252.7 ms, about 29 times slower. This mirrors the board check that everyone is still served when the cache dies.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the board looked harsher">

| What was measured | Real stack | Check id | Board check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Failed redirects, Redis stopped | 0 of 3,592 | `survives_cache_loss` | Everyone is still served when the cache dies |
| Slowest 1 in 100, Redis stopped | 252.7 ms (8.7 ms before) | `survives_cache_loss` | Slowest 1 in 100 while the cache is down |

On the board, a database with no read-only copy went to 124 percent busy and failed to serve everyone. On your laptop the load is small, so the database kept up, slowly. Both pictures are true: at low load the cache's death is only slow, and near the database's limit it is an outage.

</GoDeeper>

## The database dies

<MermaidDiagram title="Postgres stops" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["API"] --> C["Cache<br>still answers hot links"]
  A -.->|"stopped"| P[("Postgres")]`} />

With Postgres stopped, hot links still redirected from the cache: 5,152 redirects, none failed, all from the cache. Making a new link or opening one nobody had opened failed with a clean error in about 2 seconds, not a hang. The board cannot show this, because it treats a dead database as a full outage.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two checks the board does not model">

| What was measured | Real stack | Check id |
|---|---|---|
| Failed redirects of hot links, Postgres stopped | 0 of 5,152 | `survives_db_loss_reads` |
| Slowest 1 in 100 redirects, Postgres stopped | 6.2 ms | `db_down_redirect_p99_ms` |
| The slowest error for a new link, Postgres stopped | 503 in about 2,005 ms | `db_down_fails_fast` |

The design that counts each click inside the redirect (the `add_cache` preset) behaves differently: each redirect waits for its click to be written, so with the database down every redirect waits for the 2 second timeout. That is the difference between a design that is slow and one that stays fast. When you compare your own runs, look at two numbers: how long a redirect waits when the database is down, and whether the wait is the same for every redirect. A design that fails fast gives a short error and moves on. A design that waits for a timeout holds a connection open for the full 2 seconds, so a burst of 20 requests can pile up behind each other. That pile-up is what a circuit breaker removes, and it is the subject of task T8.

</GoDeeper>

## Clicks

<MermaidDiagram title="Two ways to lose clicks" chart={`flowchart LR
  W["Worker stops"] -->|"clicks wait, then catch up"| OK["All counted"]
  R["Redis stops"] -->|"clicks have nowhere to wait"| LOST["Clicks lost"]`} />

When the worker stopped for 8 seconds, all 7,377 clicks were counted: the waiting clicks peaked at 3,865 and were gone about 4 seconds after the restart. When Redis stopped, about 42 percent of the clicks were lost. This mirrors the board check that no clicks are lost when the worker dies, and shows what the board could not.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the one check that fails on purpose">

| What was measured | Real stack | Check id |
|---|---|---|
| Clicks counted after the worker came back | 7,377 of 7,377 | `worker_loss_clicks_caught_up` |
| Clicks counted while Redis was stopped | 4,106 of 7,055 | `click_loss_redis_down` |
| Clicks counted after a short load, nothing broken | 25,600 of 25,600 | `clicks_counted` |

The `full_design` preset fails `click_loss_redis_down` on purpose, because the queue lives in Redis next to the cache. It is the failure the board drew as two boxes and could not show. The fix is a choice, not a trick: count clicks without a queue and pay in redirect speed, or keep the events somewhere that survives, such as a file or a managed queue. Ask which business you are building. If a click is worth money, a lost click is lost income, and a slower redirect is a fair price. If a click is only a trend line on a chart, losing four in ten during a rare failure may be acceptable, and the fast design wins. The lab cannot choose for you, but it can make the cost visible in a number, and that is what you write down in your report.

</GoDeeper>
