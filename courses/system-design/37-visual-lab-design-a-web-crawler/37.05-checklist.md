## Learning log

1. **Your first run:** Which checks did the starting design fail, and which one surprised you most?
2. **Your busiest part:** In your final design, which part is the busiest at peak, and how busy is it? What would you raise next, and what would it cost?
3. **Your memory:** What does your design do when the seen-URL memory dies? Which part of it makes that survivable?
4. **Your pages:** Did your fetchers save pages themselves or through a queue and parsers? What did you pay for that choice?
5. **Your bill:** Object storage was the biggest cost whatever you chose. Which part of your design could you still trim, and which check would tell you if it went too far?
6. **Your first guess:** In chapter 37.1 you guessed how the wait changes when a till goes from half busy to 90 percent busy. Was your guess right, and where did you see the same thing on the board?

## Your gate answer: defend your design

This is the lab again, in words. You built a design on the board and it told you, check by check, where it was weak. A design review in a real team works the same way. Someone shows a design, someone else asks what happens when a part fails, and the designer answers with a number, not a feeling. Write the answer you would give in that room, about 200 words, about the design that passed or came closest.

A weak answer lists the parts: a load balancer, three fetchers, a memory, a table, a queue and two parsers. It reads like a shopping list, and it could describe any design. A strong answer starts from a number the board showed you and lets that number decide the parts. Say which check was the hardest to pass, what you changed to pass it, and the number before and after. For example, the fetchers at 113% busy with one gone, and 56% after you added a third.

Then pick one trade-off and write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would change it. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a seen-URL memory at $0.15 an hour, so the URL table stays at 4% busy and survives the memory dying", or "two parsers at batch 500, which leave 2,100 pages a second to spare to clear a build-up of waiting pages". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished. A good "when would you change it" line also has a trigger, such as "if the peak doubles" or "if the memory gets a copy".

End with the failure your design is built to survive, and the one it is not. Every design has one. Saying so is not a weakness. It is the part of the answer that shows you looked. The board cannot see politeness, so say how you would keep DalilBot to one request a second for each website (chapter 26.3), and what you would pay for it.

A good order for 200 words is the order of the board. Start with the hardest check and the number that moved. Then the trade-off. Last, the failure you did not fix. If you run short of words, cut the list of parts, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is tuning until the board is green without reading the reasons, and ending with every part at the largest size, which passes everything and fails on cost. The second is treating the seen-URL memory as redundancy. A memory makes questions fast, but it is not a copy that keeps the service up, and the memory scene is there to show it. The third is sizing the parsers for the arrival rate and nothing more, so waiting pages, once they exist, can never be cleared.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two learners can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the choice to a check, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to the pages if the queue itself fails, or why you chose batch 500 and not 50. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.

With one gone, the one left was at 113 percent and only 88.8 percent of fetch jobs were served. I added a third fetcher. With one gone, the two left run at 56 percent, everyone is served, and the slowest 1 in 100 is 65 ms. Trade-off: I kept the seen-URL memory at 95 percent hits and paid for it. I would drop it if I ran two table nodes, or if the load fell to a level one node serves alone.
