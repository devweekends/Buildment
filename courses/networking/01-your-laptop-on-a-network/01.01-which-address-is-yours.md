## Your computer and a website disagree

<MermaidDiagram title="One computer, two numbers" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Your computer says 192.168.1.23"] -->|"your home side"| B["Router, inside: 192.168.1.1"]
  B --- C["Router, outside: 203.0.113.45"]
  C -->|"the internet side"| D["The website sees 203.0.113.45"]
`} legend="The router sits between your home and the internet. It has one number on each side. Your computer uses the number on the home side. The website sees only the number on the internet side." />

Your computer says `192.168.1.23`. The website says `203.0.113.45`. Neither is wrong. They are numbers for two places: one inside your home, one on the internet side of your router. Which one is yours depends on who is asking.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why one device has more than one number">

An address is a fact about a place on a path, not a label stuck on a device. Inside your home, the router and your other devices know your laptop as `192.168.1.23`. Outside, the website never talks to your laptop at all. It talks to the router's outside address, `203.0.113.45`, and the router passes the answer on.

This course is built around one question. All your devices connect to a router, and none of them has an address that the whole internet can reach. So how do they talk to the internet? Module 2 gives a first look at the answer and module 6 builds it with your own hands. Until then, this module only collects numbers and builds a first small network.

<Callout type="info" title="The outside address here is a stand-in">
Numbers such as `203.0.113.45` come from three ranges that are kept for examples, which should not appear on the public internet [S10]. We call them documentation ranges. A real public address is personal, so the course shows a stand-in. Your own numbers will differ from every number printed in this chapter.
</Callout>

</GoDeeper>

## Read your own numbers on your computer

<MermaidDiagram title="Five numbers and where they come from" chart={`flowchart TD
  C["Your computer's settings"] --> A["Address: who you are inside your home"]
  C --> M["Mask: which addresses are on your own network"]
  C --> G["Gateway: the router that takes everything for outside"]
  C --> D["DNS server: turns a name into an address"]
  W["A website"] --> P["Public address: the number the website sees"]
`} legend="Four numbers come from your computer's own settings. The fifth can only come from a website, because it is the number the website sees." />

Five numbers describe where your computer sits. Four come from its settings: the address, the mask, the gateway, which is your router, and the DNS server, which turns a name into an address. The fifth is the public address: the one a website sees. Only a website can tell you that one. Run the commands for your system, then write all five in the log below.

```
# Windows, in PowerShell
ipconfig /all
curl.exe -4 ifconfig.me

# macOS, in Terminal (not yet checked on a Mac)
ifconfig
netstat -rn
scutil --dns
curl -4 ifconfig.me

# Linux, in a terminal
ip addr
ip route
resolvectl status
curl -4 ifconfig.me
```

### Learning log

1. **Your address:** Four numbers with dots, for example 192.168.1.23. Use your own.
2. **Your mask:** Dotted, like 255.255.255.0. On macOS it may print as hex, like 0xffffff00. Write what you see.
3. **Your gateway:** Your router. On a phone it is often called Router.
4. **Your DNS server:** Take the IPv4 one if you see two.
5. **The address a website sees:** What curl printed. If it is long and has colons, write it and say so. This log is private to you.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the lines look like, and when the result is odd">

This is an illustrative example, not a recording. Your numbers will differ. On Windows, `ipconfig /all` prints blocks like this for each adapter, and `curl.exe -4 ifconfig.me` prints one line.

```
   IPv4 Address. . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.23
   Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.255.0
   Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.1
   DNS Servers . . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.1

203.0.113.45
```

The <GlossaryTerm term="gateway" definition="The router your computer hands every packet for outside your own network." /> is the number on the `Default Gateway` line. On many phones the Wi-Fi details screen shows it as `Router`. The <GlossaryTerm term="DNS server" definition="A computer that turns a name such as example.com into an address." /> is on the `DNS Servers` line. The <GlossaryTerm term="public address" definition="The address a website sees when you visit it." /> is the only number you cannot read from your own screen.

Four results are odd but normal. First, if you left out `-4`, `curl` may print a long number with colons, such as `2001:db8:4a1:20::5c` (an example from the documentation prefix [S69]; yours will differ). That is an IPv6 address, which module 9 explains. Write it down, and run the command again with `-4` for the IPv4 one. Second, your provider may share one public address between many customers. You cannot see that from `curl` alone. If your router's status page shows an outside address from `100.64.0.0` to `100.127.255.255`, that is the range providers use for sharing [S11]. It is one sign, not proof. Third, a VPN changes the answer, because the website then sees the VPN's address. Note it, or switch it off for this chapter. Fourth, the lab is a separate machine with its own numbers. Run these commands on your own computer, not in the lab.

`ifconfig.me` is a website run by someone else [S52]. Using it is optional and it is never graded. Your public address is personal, so do not post it. The macOS commands have not yet been checked on a Mac for this course. If a Mac screen looks different, trust your own screen and tell your mentor.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

[S10] RFC 5737: three IPv4 blocks for documentation, which should not appear on the public internet. [S11] RFC 6598: `100.64.0.0/10` is reserved for provider-side shared address space. [S52] ifconfig.me, a public echo service whose operator the page does not name. [S69] RFC 3849: `2001:db8::/32` is reserved for IPv6 documentation examples.
