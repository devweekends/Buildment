This is the gate for module 1. Your evidence is one page: a map of your own home network, drawn or written with your own numbers. You made every number for this page in the last seven chapters, so nothing here is new. The gate only asks you to put it together and to say honestly what you do not understand yet. Your answer saves as you type, so you can stop and come back after a power cut.

A mentor reads the page within three days. You do not have to wait for the review. You can carry on to module 2 as soon as you submit, and the mentor's follow-up questions will reach you while you work.

## What the page must show

The page has four parts, and the mentor looks for each of them. The first part is your numbers. Write your computer's address, your mask, your gateway, your DNS server and the IPv4 address a website sees. Give the mask in both forms, as dotted numbers and as the short prefix that the lab checker printed in chapter 1.3. Copy the numbers from your own `my-numbers.txt` or from your Wi-Fi screen. Numbers copied from this course will not do, because the point is that nobody else could have written yours.

The second part is the map. Show your laptop, your phone and the router, each with its own address. Show the router with two numbers, one on the inside of your home and one on the outside, and name your provider. If you have a TV or another device, add it. The map can be a drawing, a table or a few lines of text. A clear photo of a paper sketch is fine, and so is a link to a labelled diagram.

The third part is the two views. Say which address your computer uses and which address a website sees. Say that they sit on different sides of the router. You do not need to say why the router has two numbers. That is the question this course answers in module 6, and you are not expected to know it yet. What matters is that you can see there are two views of one device, and that you can name them.

The fourth part is three questions. Write three questions about your own home network that you cannot yet answer. A good question comes from your own numbers and can be answered by a later module. A weak question is one that nobody could answer, such as "how does the internet work?". The next section shows the difference.

## A worked example, for a different home

Here is a page for an imagined home. It is made up, and the numbers are stand-ins. Your own numbers will differ. Amal has a laptop, a phone and a TV. Her router has one number inside and one outside.

```
Provider: a made-up provider, home fibre

  Laptop   10.0.0.15   mask 255.255.255.0 (/24)
  Phone    10.0.0.16
  TV       10.0.0.31
      |
  Router   inside 10.0.0.1    outside 203.0.113.77
      |
  The internet

Gateway: 10.0.0.1      DNS server: 10.0.0.1
A website sees: 203.0.113.77
```

Read the example from the bottom. The website sees `203.0.113.77`, which is the outside of the router. The laptop uses `10.0.0.15`, which exists only inside the home. The gateway is the router's inside number, because it is the device that takes everything meant for outside. In this home the DNS server is the router as well, which is common. In your home it may be a different number, and your own page should say so.

Amal's questions might be these. Why does the website see the router's number and not her laptop's? How does her phone find the router's hardware address? What does a gateway do with a packet that is for the internet? Each one starts from her own page, and each one has an answer in a later module. That is what makes a question good: she cannot answer it yet, but she knows where to look.

## If your result was different

Two results from chapter 1.1 change the page a little. Neither one is a mistake, and neither needs a new idea from you.

If the address a website saw was an IPv6 address, write it down, and also run the command again with `-4` to get the IPv4 one. If you have both, put both on the page and say which is which. This is common. About half of the users who reach Google do so over IPv6, 50.10 percent on 28 March 2026 [S33], so your result is normal. The figure is dated and will change.

If the address started with `100.`, write it on your page. Add one sentence: "My provider seems to share addresses between customers." That range is reserved for shared space on provider networks [S11]. It is one sign, not proof. It is common on mobile networks: a study with data from 2014 to 2016 found that more than 92 percent of cellular networks used it [S34]. That number is old, so treat it as a hint about the pattern and not as a measurement of today. Either way, you do not need to explain it. It is a good question to put on the page, because module 6 answers it.
