In 10.4 two networks peered over a direct cable. A big network may peer with hundreds of others. It cannot lay a separate cable to each one. So where do all those cables actually meet? And when your message reaches a friend on another continent, what carries it across the sea?

## Many networks, one shared switch

An <GlossaryTerm term="exchange point" definition="A place where many networks connect to one shared switch, so that they can exchange traffic with each other." /> is a building where many networks connect to one shared switch [S101]. Each network brings one cable, then peers with any network there that agrees. Local traffic stays local: cheaper than sending it abroad, and the route is shorter [S101].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: look up an exchange point near you on PeeringDB">

PeeringDB is a public database of networks, exchange points and the buildings where they meet. Using it means you agree to its Acceptable Use Policy [S97]. On 2026-10-27 its data could be read with no account and no key [S97].

**Your task.** Open `https://www.peeringdb.com` in a browser and search for the name of your city or your country. Pick one exchange point and write down three things: its name, its city, and how many networks it lists. If you prefer the raw data, open this address in your browser. It searches exchange points by name and returns the records as JSON:

```
https://www.peeringdb.com/api/ix?name__contains=AMS-IX
```

Here is one real record, read on 2026-10-27. AMS-IX is an exchange point in Amsterdam. Its shared switch uses the block `80.249.208.0/21` for IPv4 and `2001:7f8:1::/64` for IPv6 [S97]. Those are the addresses the networks' routers get on the shared switch. That is why, in 10.1, a hop with an address in such a block belongs to the exchange in the lookup, even though the router belongs to one of the networks.

You can look up a network the same way. AS3333, RIPE NCC's network from 10.1 and 10.2, lists ports at two exchange points, AMS-IX and NL-ix [S97]:

```
https://www.peeringdb.com/api/net?asn=3333
```

The data is live and changes when networks join or leave. In gate 10.12 you will check whether two networks from your own trace are at the same exchange point.

You may also meet the words route server at an exchange point. RFC 9234 gives it a role of its own, next to customer, provider and peer [S92]. This course does not use one.

</GoDeeper>

## Cables on the sea floor join continents

<MermaidDiagram title="Between continents, mostly under the sea" chart={`flowchart LR
  E["Exchange points and networks<br>on one continent"] ---|"undersea cable<br>more than 600 active and planned"| F["Exchange points and networks<br>on another continent"]
  E -.-|"satellite: a tiny share"| F
`} legend="Traffic between continents travels mostly through cables laid on the sea floor. Satellites carry a very small part. The numbers are from TeleGeography, dated in the text." />

Between continents, traffic travels mostly through cables on the sea floor. TeleGeography confirms the common claim that these cables carry over 99% of intercontinental traffic, with a caveat about missing data [S106]. In 2026 it counted more than 600 active and planned cables [S107].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the 99% comes with a caveat">

The claim "submarine cables carry over 99% of intercontinental data traffic" is quoted everywhere. In a post of 4 May 2023, Alan Mauldin of TeleGeography checked it and wrote that it is true. He also wrote that an exact figure would need data about satellite traffic that does not exist. The only public satellite number he found was from the FCC: satellites carried 0.37% of the international capacity of the United States, in data from 2013 that was released in 2015 [S106]. So "over 99%" is a well-supported estimate, not a measurement, and it is old.

The count of cables is dated too. TeleGeography's answers page tracked "more than 600 active and planned submarine cables" as of 2026 [S107]. "Planned" means some are not built yet. The number grows every year, so always give the year with it.

</GoDeeper>
