On 24 February 2008, for about two hours, traffic to YouTube from networks that took one bad route went to the wrong network. Nobody broke into YouTube. Its servers were fine. A different network announced a block of YouTube's addresses, and routers believed it. This chapter shows how, with the rules you already know.

## A smaller block from the wrong network

<MermaidDiagram title="YouTube, 24 February 2008" chart={`flowchart LR
  Y["YouTube, AS36561<br>announces 208.65.152.0/22"] --> I["Networks on the internet"]
  P["Pakistan Telecom, AS17557<br>announces 208.65.153.0/24<br>from 18:47 UTC"] --> C["PCCW Global, AS3491<br>passes it on"]
  C --> I
`} legend="Two announcements cover the same addresses. YouTube's own /22 holds 1,024 addresses. The /24 from the wrong network sits inside it and holds 256 of them." />

YouTube, AS36561, announced its block `208.65.152.0/22`. At 18:47 UTC, Pakistan Telecom, AS17557, announced `208.65.153.0/24`, a smaller block inside YouTube's. Its provider, PCCW Global, AS3491, passed it on [S105]. A network announcing a block that is not its own is a <GlossaryTerm term="hijack" definition="A network announces a block that is not its own, so traffic for it goes to that network." />.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the timeline, from the case study">

The RIPE NCC wrote a case study from the data of its route collectors, called RIS [S105]. In UTC on 24 February 2008:

| Time | What the collectors saw |
|---|---|
| 18:47 | AS17557 starts to announce `208.65.153.0/24`, and AS3491 passes it on |
| later | YouTube announces `208.65.153.0/24` itself, and then two `/25` blocks inside it |
| 21:01 | AS3491 withdraws the route from AS17557 |

From 18:47 to 21:01 is about 2 hours and 14 minutes. The case study does not say how far the bad route reached. RIS sees only what its collectors collect, so the true reach is unknown [S105]. That is why this chapter says "networks that took the route", not "the whole internet".

Read the AS numbers with 10.3 in mind. A route from Pakistan Telecom through PCCW Global had a path ending in 17557. YouTube's own route had a path ending in 36561. The two origins differ, and nothing in BGP itself says which one is right. A router does not know who should own a block, unless someone tells it. The rest of this chapter is about how someone can.

</GoDeeper>

## The longest prefix won the traffic

<MermaidDiagram title="One router's table during the hijack" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["A packet for 208.65.153.238"] --> T{"Which rows hold it?"}
  T --> A["208.65.152.0/22<br>origin AS36561, YouTube"]
  T --> B["208.65.153.0/24<br>origin AS17557, the wrong network"]
  B --> W["The /24 is longer, so it wins"]
`} legend="A router that heard both announcements has two rows that hold the address. The longer prefix wins, as in 4.3, whatever the AS path says." />

Both rows hold YouTube's addresses, and routers chose the more specific route, "because of the longest prefix match rule" [S105]. It is the rule from 4.3 and step 3 of your `bestRoute`. YouTube fought back with the same rule: it announced its own `/24`, then two `/25` blocks. PCCW withdrew the bad route at 21:01 UTC [S105].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why YouTube's answer worked">

Longest prefix match does not care who announced a block. It cares only about the length. So the only way for YouTube to win back its addresses quickly was to announce blocks at least as specific as the stolen one.

First YouTube announced `208.65.153.0/24` itself. Now routers had two routes to the same `/24`, and they chose between them by policy and path length, as in 10.6: some chose YouTube's, some still chose the other. Then YouTube announced two `/25` blocks, the two halves of that `/24`. A `/25` is longer than the stolen `/24`, so a router that took them sent those addresses back to YouTube [S105].

The bad route itself ended only when the network that passed it on, PCCW Global, withdrew it at 21:01 UTC [S105].

The address in the picture, `208.65.153.238`, is one address inside the stolen `/24`, chosen for the picture.

</GoDeeper>

## A leak: a route sent too far

<MermaidDiagram title="A route leak" chart={`flowchart TD
  P1["Provider A"] -->|"a route, meant for<br>its customer only"| C["Customer network"]
  C -.->|"leaks it up to its other provider"| P2["Provider B"]
  P2 -.->|"passes it on to the world"| W["Other networks send traffic<br>the wrong way, through the customer"]
`} legend="A customer with two providers passes a route from one provider to the other. The 10.4 rules say it must not. The route is real, but it now goes much further than it was meant to." />

A route leak is "the propagation of routing announcement(s) beyond their intended scope" [S91]. The route itself may be true, with the right origin. It just goes where it should not. A customer that passes one provider's routes to another breaks the rule from 10.4, and traffic then squeezes through a small network.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two kinds of leak, and one router that split blocks">

RFC 7908, from June 2016, names six kinds of leak [S91]. Two are enough here. In a hairpin turn, a network with two providers takes a route from one and hands it to the other, exactly the picture above. In another kind, a network takes routes from inside its own network, or more specific blocks it was meant to keep to itself, and lets them out by mistake.

Both break the export rules of 10.4: routes from a provider or a peer go only down to customers [S145, S92].

You may read about an event in June 2019: a router that split blocks into more specific ones, which RPKI origin validation, the next beat, would have dropped [S102]. This chapter uses a cleaner pair: one hijack and one leak.

</GoDeeper>

## Signed records say who may announce

<MermaidDiagram title="RPKI origin validation" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["A signed record, a ROA:<br>block, the AS allowed to announce it,<br>the longest prefix allowed"] --> V{"A router checks a route"}
  V -->|"a ROA matches its block,<br>origin and length"| G["Valid"]
  V -->|"a ROA covers the block,<br>but none matches"| B["Invalid"]
  V -->|"no ROA covers the block"| N["NotFound"]
`} legend="The owner of a block signs a record. A router compares each route with the records and puts it in one of three groups. A router can then drop the Invalid ones." />

With RPKI, the holder of a block signs a record called a ROA. It says which AS may announce the block, and how long a prefix it may use [S89]. A router checks each route: Valid if a record matches, Invalid if records cover the block but none matches, NotFound if no record covers it [S90].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the check looks at">

RPKI follows the same chain as the address registries in 10.2: IANA at the top, the five regional registries below it, then the holders of blocks [S89]. A holder signs a ROA, short for Route Origin Authorisation. It names one AS that may originate the blocks, with an optional maximum length [S89].

Route origin validation, RFC 6811 from January 2013, uses those records [S90]. A route is **Valid** when a ROA matches both its prefix length and its origin AS. It is **Invalid** when at least one ROA covers the prefix but none matches, for example because the origin is wrong, or the prefix is longer than the maximum length. It is **NotFound** when no ROA covers the prefix at all.

The check looks at two things only: the block with its length, and the origin, the last number of the AS path. It does not check the rest of the path. RFC 6811 says so itself: origin validation "does not protect against AS-in-the-middle attacks", and it gives no path validation [S90].

In 10.10 you write a filter by hand that does the same job for one block: it accepts `198.51.100.0/24` only with the origin AS64496. RPKI does that for millions of blocks, from signed records that any network can fetch.

</GoDeeper>

## RPKI caught the hijack, not the leak

<MermaidDiagram title="Cloudflare 1.1.1.1, 27 June 2024" chart={`flowchart TD
  H["AS267613 announces 1.1.1.1/32<br>from 18:51 UTC"] --> HI["Invalid: wrong origin<br>validating routers drop it"]
  L["AS262504 leaks 1.1.1.0/24<br>AS1031 spreads it"] --> LV["Valid: the origin is right<br>origin validation lets it pass"]
`} legend="Two separate events on the same day, both about Cloudflare's resolver at 1.1.1.1. The hijack had the wrong origin. The leak had the right one." />

On 27 June 2024, AS267613 announced `1.1.1.1/32`, and it was RPKI Invalid. Separately, AS262504 leaked `1.1.1.0/24`, and AS1031 spread it. That route was Valid, because its origin was right [S103]. So origin validation stops a wrong origin, or a block longer than the record allows. It cannot see a right route sent too far [S90, S103].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the times, and what would have stopped the leak">

Cloudflare's own report gives the times in UTC [S103]. AS267613 began to announce `1.1.1.1/32` at 18:51, and the hijack was resolved by 22:16. The separate leak of `1.1.1.0/24` by AS262504, spread further by AS1031, ended at 02:28 on 28 June. Users in over 300 networks in 70 countries saw problems [S103].

The `/32` came from the wrong origin, and it was RPKI Invalid [S103], so a network that drops Invalid routes ignored it. The leaked `/24` was Valid, because Cloudflare's AS was still the origin. Cloudflare wrote that path validation was needed for that one: a check of the whole path, called ASPA, not only of the origin [S103]. This course does not teach ASPA.

How common is RPKI? Cloudflare wrote that it "reached a major milestone at 50% deployment" in July 2024, in its own measure of signed blocks [S103]. By that measure, about half the blocks had records in 2024. A hijack of a block with no record is NotFound, not Invalid.

There is also a list of good practice for networks, MANRS. It asks for four actions: filtering, anti-spoofing, coordination, and global validation with registry data or RPKI [S109]. The filter you write in 10.10 is the first of those four.

</GoDeeper>

## Facebook 2021: a withdrawal, not a theft

<MermaidDiagram title="Back to 7.4: routes withdrawn, servers fine" chart={`flowchart LR
  B["Backbone links down<br>after a maintenance command"] --> D["DNS servers cannot reach<br>the data centres"]
  D --> W["They withdraw their own<br>BGP announcements"]
  W --> X["No route to the DNS servers:<br>running, but unreachable"]
`} legend="The outage from 7.4, now read with this module's words. Nobody announced a wrong route. The right routes were withdrawn." />

In 7.4 you met Facebook's outage of 4 October 2021. Now you can name it. The DNS servers lost their links to the data centres, so they withdrew their own BGP announcements [S48]. That is part 2 of challenge 7: a withdrawal. The servers ran, but no network knew a way to them.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three ways a route goes wrong">

Put the three cases side by side:

| Case | What was announced | Origin | RPKI origin validation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hijack, YouTube 2008 | A more specific block, by the wrong network | Wrong | Would mark it Invalid, if the owner had a record |
| Leak, Cloudflare 2024 | The right block, sent too far | Right | Valid: it passes |
| Withdrawal, Facebook 2021 | The right routes, taken back | Right | Nothing to check: no route left |

The YouTube row is a thought experiment: RPKI origin validation was not yet a standard in 2008. RFC 6811 is from 2013 [S90]. With a record for its `/22` that allows only `/22`, the stolen `/24` would have failed on both the origin and the length.

The Facebook row is the one from 7.4. Facebook's own account says a maintenance command meant to assess backbone capacity took down all the backbone links, and a bug in an audit tool let it run. The DNS servers then withdrew their announcements, because they could not reach the data centres. Remote access failed too, and engineers had to go on site [S48]. No RPKI record and no filter helps against that. The routes were right. There were simply none.

</GoDeeper>
