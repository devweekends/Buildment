In 1.1 you wrote down the public address a website sees when you visit it. In 4.1 you ran a trace and marked a dozen hops as private, public or unknown. Your provider owns the first few. Who owns the rest? Every public address belongs to some network, and that network has a number and a name you can look up in a minute, in your browser, with no account.

## One address, one block, one network

<MermaidDiagram title="From your address to a network" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Your public address<br>from 1.1"] --> B["The block it is in<br>for example a /21"]
  B --> C["The network that announces the block<br>its AS number"]
  C --> D["The name of that network<br>its holder"]
`} legend="Left to right: an address sits inside a block of addresses, one network announces that block to the rest of the internet, and the registry knows that network's name. A lookup tool walks the three steps for you." />

Your address sits inside a block, and one network tells the internet that it can reach that block. That network has a number, its AS number, and a holder's name. Paste your address from 1.1 into RIPEstat or bgp.tools in the browser. Then do the same for each public hop of your trace.

| Your computer | How to look it up |
|---|---|
| Windows | In the browser: RIPEstat or bgp.tools. Or `whois -p 43` in the lab, which has it installed |
| macOS | The browser, or `whois` in Terminal |
| Linux | The browser, or `whois` (Ubuntu package `whois`, 0.3 MiB) |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: four ways to look up an address, all without an account">

**RIPEstat** has a public data interface with addresses of this shape: `https://stat.ripe.net/data/<call>/data.json?resource=<what>` [S93]. Three calls do this chapter's job, and each one worked on 2026-10-27 with no key [S94]:

- `whats-my-ip` tells you the address the service sees, the same idea as the page in 1.1.
- `prefix-overview?resource=<your address>` gives the block the address is in and the AS number that announces it.
- `as-overview?resource=AS<number>` gives the holder's name.

Here is a real lookup, documented on 2026-10-27, so you can practise even if your own trace shows nothing useful. `prefix-overview?resource=193.0.0.1` answers that the address is in `193.0.0.0/21`, announced by AS3333, and `as-overview?resource=AS3333` gives the holder RIPE-NCC-AS [S94]. That is RIPE NCC's own network. The data is live, so the answer may change one day.

```
https://stat.ripe.net/data/prefix-overview/data.json?resource=193.0.0.1
https://stat.ripe.net/data/as-overview/data.json?resource=AS3333
```

RIPEstat states no hard request limit, but it asks for registration above 1,000 requests a day and allows 8 requests at once from one address [S93]. A few lookups by hand are nowhere near that.

**bgp.tools** looks up an AS number, a block or a name, and showed its page for AS3333 with no login [S95]. It sells scripting and an interface for programs, so use it to look, not to script.

**whois** asks a registry's server directly. It is a client for the old directory protocol of RFC 3912, and it guesses which server to ask unless you name one with `-h` [S138]. The same registries also answer RDAP, the newer form over HTTPS, at addresses such as `https://rdap.db.ripe.net/autnum/3333` [S99, S100].

**Team Cymru** runs a free service that maps an address to its AS number, from BGP data refreshed every 4 hours. Heavy use can get your address blocked, and one block can have more than one origin network [S98]. Ask it like this, on macOS or Linux:

```
whois -h whois.cymru.com " -v 193.0.0.1"
```

In the lab, add `-p 43`, the whois port. The lab image has no list of port names, so without it `whois` stops with `Servname not supported for ai_socktype`:

```
whois -p 43 -h whois.cymru.com " -v 193.0.0.1"
```

On macOS and Linux, `mtr -z` shows an AS number beside each hop of a trace [S110], and `traceroute -A` prints AS lookups after each address [S31]. Run them on your own computer, as in 4.1, not in the lab: the lab hides the real path.

If you are behind carrier-grade NAT (6.4), the address a website sees is your provider's, not your router's. That is fine here: the lookup then names your provider's network, which is the answer you want.

</GoDeeper>

## A hop's address is a good guess

<MermaidDiagram title="Whose address is on the link?" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Your provider<br>AS 64500"] -- "a cable between two networks<br>numbered from the neighbour's block" --- N["The next network<br>AS 64501"]
  N --- X["An exchange point<br>its own block for many networks"]
`} legend="Two made-up networks and an exchange point. A cable between two networks needs addresses from one of them, so a router's reply can carry the neighbour's address. At an exchange point, many networks share one block that belongs to the exchange." />

A cable between two networks needs addresses, and they come from one side's block. So a router in your provider can answer from an address that the next network owns. At an exchange point, many networks share the exchange's own block. So "this hop is in AS X" is a good guess. It is not proof.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to read your trace with care">

Each line of a trace is a reply from one router, sent from one of its own addresses (4.4). That address sits on a cable that joins two machines, and someone had to number the cable. When the cable joins two networks, its addresses usually come from one of the two blocks. So the lookup for that hop names one of the two networks, and you cannot tell which from the number alone. This is reasoning, not a rule from a standard. The services in the first fold give a hint of their own: Team Cymru says that a block with more than one origin network is normal [S98].

An exchange point is a place where many networks connect to one shared switch (10.5). The exchange has its own block for that switch. For example, the exchange AMS-IX in Amsterdam lists `80.249.208.0/21` as its peering block in PeeringDB [S97]. A hop with an address in such a block belongs to some network at the exchange, but the lookup names the exchange.

Mark each public hop with a network and a question mark when you are not sure. Two hops in a row with the same AS number are probably inside one network. A change of number is where your packet crossed from one network to the next, give or take one hop.

**If your trace shows only stars or private hops**, for example on a mobile hotspot or behind carrier-grade NAT, use the sample trace in `challenges/challenge-7-bgp/README.md` in your repository, and practise the lookup on `193.0.0.1` from the first fold. The sample is a real public trace: RIPE Atlas measurement 18399116, from Amsterdam to Tokyo on 27 August 2026 [S148]. The README also lists what RIPEstat and PeeringDB said about each hop on 27 October 2026 [S149, S150]. If you cannot open your repository, for example on a phone, gate 10.12 prints the same trace and its lookups in a fold. The trace from 4.1 in this course uses rewritten addresses from the documentation ranges, so a lookup finds nothing for them.

<Callout type="warning" title="Your public hops are personal">
Your public address and the hops after it tell a reader which provider you use and roughly where you live. Keep the lookups in your own notes. Gate 10.12 asks for them, and only your mentor sees that answer.
</Callout>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Your trace shows hop 5 and hop 6 both in AS 64500, your provider, and hop 7 in AS 64501. What can you say about where your packet left your provider's network?"
options:
  - "Near hop 6 or hop 7, give or take a hop"
  - "Exactly at hop 7, the first new number"
  - "At hop 5, where AS 64500 first shows"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. The number changes between hop 6 and hop 7, but the cable there may be numbered from either network, so the crossing is near that point, not exactly on it."
  - "Hop 7's address could come from a cable numbered by AS 64501 while the router still belongs to your provider, or the other way round. The lookup is a good guess, not proof."
  - "Hop 5 is still inside your provider's network, and so is hop 6. The packet crosses where the number changes, not where it first appears."
```
