Routers do not tell anyone that they forwarded your packet. So how does `traceroute` learn their addresses?

## TTL is a countdown in every packet

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m4-ttl-countdown" />

Every IP packet carries a number called <GlossaryTerm term="TTL" definition="Time to live: a counter in the packet that each router lowers by one." />. A Linux computer starts it at 64 [S28]. Each router lowers it by one. In the picture the server sees 62 after two routers. At zero the packet is discarded. Press Next to watch the countdown.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the rule, and what the lab showed">

RFC 1812 says that a router must lower the TTL by at least one, and that at zero it must discard the packet [S30]. "At least one" means that some routers may lower it by more, but one is the usual step. Linux starts its own packets at 64, because the setting `ip_default_ttl` is 64 by default [S28].

The lab agrees with the picture. In challenge 2 the laptop's echo request had `ttl 64` on the inside link and `ttl 63` on the outside link, after one router [R-router]. In another test, `tcpdump -v` on the far side of two routers showed `ttl 62`, which is 64 minus 2 [D5.8]. So the count of routers is 64 minus the TTL that arrives, when the sender started at 64.

IPv6 has the same idea under another name, the hop limit. A router discards the packet at zero [S2]. The RFC text we read does not say that an error message is sent, so this chapter makes no claim about IPv6 errors.

</GoDeeper>

## At zero the router reports back

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m4-time-exceeded" />

When a router discards a packet at zero, it must tell the sender, with an <GlossaryTerm term="ICMP" definition="The message protocol that computers and routers use for errors and tests. Ping uses it too." /> message called Time Exceeded [S30]. The message comes from the router's own address. Press Next, and pick who the router tells.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: who sends it, and why RFC 1812">

For a unicast packet, a router must send ICMP Time Exceeded, with code 0, when it discards the packet because the TTL reached zero [S30]. RFC 792 defines the message as type 11 [S29]. But RFC 792 only says that the gateway "may" notify the sender. So the duty of the router comes from RFC 1812, and that is the one this course cites.

The message goes back to the source address of the packet that died. For a probe from the laptop, that is the laptop. The source address of the message is the router's own address. This is the key fact for the next beat, because the laptop learns who the router is by reading that source.

You already met ICMP in 1.5: ping sends an echo request, and the other computer sends an echo reply. Time Exceeded is another kind of ICMP message, sent for another reason. Both are carried inside IP packets.

</GoDeeper>

## Traceroute sends TTL 1, 2, 3

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m4-traceroute-probes" />

Traceroute makes each router speak. It sends probes with TTL 1, then 2, then 3. Probe 1 dies at the first <GlossaryTerm term="hop" definition="One router that passes your packet on." />, probe 2 at the second, and probe 3 reaches the server. Each reply names the device that stopped it, so the replies draw the path.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what traceroute really sends">

On Linux, `traceroute` sends UDP probes by default, to ports that start at 33434. The option `-I` uses ICMP echo instead, and `-T` uses TCP SYN packets, to port 80 by default. The TTL starts at 1 and rises, and the default stops at 30 hops [S31].

The picture sends one probe per line to keep the story clear. Your own trace may show more on each line, such as times. In the lab, `traceroute -n` across one router printed two lines: the router, then the server [R-router]. Across two routers it printed three: both routers, then the host [D5.8].

<MustRead title="traceroute(8), the Linux manual page" url="https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man8/traceroute.8.html" source="man7.org" description="The manual page for traceroute on Linux. Read it to answer one question: which kind of packet does traceroute send by default?" readMinutes={8} />

</GoDeeper>

## The reply comes from who stopped it

<MermaidDiagram title="Three probes and their replies" chart={`sequenceDiagram
  participant L as Laptop
  participant R1 as R1 192.168.1.1
  participant R2 as R2 198.51.100.1
  participant S as Server 203.0.113.80
  L->>R1: probe 1, TTL 1
  R1-->>L: Time Exceeded from 192.168.1.1
  L->>R2: probe 2, TTL 2, passes R1
  R2-->>L: Time Exceeded from 198.51.100.1
  L->>S: probe 3, TTL 3, passes R1 and R2
  S-->>L: answer from 203.0.113.80
`} legend="Three probes go out, with TTL 1, 2 and 3. Each reply is signed by the device that stopped the probe, or by the server when the probe arrived." />

Read the diagram from the top. The address on line N of a trace belongs to the device that stopped probe N. If the probe arrived, that device is the destination. The routers never announced themselves. Traceroute made each one answer by ending a packet there.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a trace as a list of stoppers">

Every line of a trace answers one question: who stopped the probe with this TTL? Line 1 is the first router, line 2 is the second, and so on. A trace to a server that is two routers away has three lines when every device answers: two routers and the server.

Now go back to your own trace from 4.1. Line 1 was your home router, the gateway from 4.2. The path from the laptop to that router was one link, so the probe with TTL 1 died at the first device it met. That is why line 1 of any trace is the first router, not the destination.

Notice what the trace does not show. It shows the address that each router used to answer. It does not show how a router chose the next hop. For that, you need the router's table, which a trace never prints. That is chapter 4.3.

</GoDeeper>

## A silent router shows * * *

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m4-silent-router" />

R2 is set to stay silent. Probe 2 still dies there, but no reply comes back, so line 2 shows three stars. Probe 3 passes through R2 as usual, so line 3 still answers. A silent router forwards packets. It only does not report.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a router stays silent">

A router can fail to answer in two ways. It may be set not to send the reply. Or a filter may drop the probe on the way in, or the reply on the way back. In both cases the sender waits, finds nothing, and prints three stars for that line.

The picture shows the first case, a router set to stay silent. It does not draw rate limits, which some routers use to reduce how many replies they send. Chapter 4.1 told you that an all-star trace is normal on some networks. Now you can say why: every router on the way, or a filter in front of them, chose not to reply to these probes.

The silence does not stop the traffic. Probe 3 went through R2 and the server answered. This is also why you cannot judge a path from one line. A line of stars needs the lines after it to be read.

</GoDeeper>

## Stars do not mean the path is broken

<MermaidDiagram title="What a line of stars can mean" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["A line shows * * *"] --> B{"Do later lines answer?"}
  B -->|"yes"| C["That router is silent, or a filter dropped its reply. The path works."]
  B -->|"no"| D["Stars alone cannot say why. Look at the probe type and the other lines."]
`} legend="A line of stars means no answer came back. If later lines answer, the path is not broken at that hop." />

Stars mean that no answer came back. The router may be set to stay silent, or a filter may have dropped the probe or the reply. If later lines answer, the path is not broken at that hop. Stars give you a question to ask, not a verdict.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to do with stars">

First, read the lines after the stars. If one answers, the packets got through. Second, try another kind of probe. In our lab test the default UDP probes showed stars after hop 1, while `-I` and `-T` reached the destination [D5.5]. That lab result came from Docker Desktop's own networking, not from a filter. It still shows that the kind of probe can change what you see.

Third, write down what you do not know. In the gate chapter 4.10 you will explain your own trace. A star line is a good place for an honest open question: is this router silent, or does a filter sit here, and what would I check?

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not claim more than you know">
A trace cannot tell you why a line is stars. It can only tell you that no answer came back for that TTL.
</Callout>

</GoDeeper>
