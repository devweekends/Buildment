You read the evidence of three different problems and say what is wrong, what fixes it, and what cannot be fixed. The network lab, where you type commands against a broken router, is not available yet, so this lab is a graded quiz on one fixed home, and an interactive version comes later.

The skill you practise is the one that matters most when something does not work. Do not guess. Collect two or three facts, each from a different place, and let the facts point to the cause. Every level below gives you the facts. Your job is to read them.

## The home, and the three levels

Mina's home is the one from challenge 4. Her laptop is `192.168.1.23`, and it runs a web page on port 80. Her phone is `192.168.1.24`. Her router has `192.168.1.1` on the inside and `203.0.113.1` on the outside, and it masquerades everything that leaves. A friend on the internet, simulated by a server at `203.0.113.80`, tries to open her page.

Use the same four questions each time. Does the packet reach the router? Does a row or a rule match it? Does the target answer? Does the reply find its way back? You do not need to answer all four every time, because the facts tell you which question to stop at.

## Level 1: the rule that points at nobody

Mina wrote one forwarding rule: what arrives at `203.0.113.1:8080` goes to `192.168.1.32:80`. She asked her friend to open `http://203.0.113.1:8080/`. The friend's browser waits, and then gives up. She did not see an error message. The page works fine on her own network when she visits the laptop directly.

She collects four facts. Think about what each one rules out.

| What she tried | What she saw |
|---|---|
| From the router, a ping to `192.168.1.23` | Answers come back |
| From the router, a ping to `192.168.1.32` | No answer at all, two sent and none received |
| From the friend's server, a request to `203.0.113.1:22` | Refused at once |
| From the friend's server, a request to `203.0.113.1:8080` | The request waits, then gives up |

Start with the third fact. A request to a port with no rule is refused at once. That tells you the router is up and can be reached from outside. The router answers. The fourth fact shows that port 8080 is different: the router accepts the packet and sends it on, and then nothing comes back. So the packet leaves the router, and it never finds a device that answers. The second fact explains why. Pinging `192.168.1.32` from the router gets no answer, while pinging `192.168.1.23` works. Nobody at home owns `.32`. This is the silent loss you met in module 1, when a ping to an address that nobody owns got no answer.

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "From the router, a ping to the rule's target, 192.168.1.32, gets no answer, but a ping to the laptop at 192.168.1.23 works. What is the fault, and what is the fix?"
options:
  - "Forwarding is off in the router: switch it on"
  - "The rule's target is wrong: use 192.168.1.23"
  - "Masquerade is missing: add a rule on the outside plug"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "Nothing in the facts points at forwarding. The router answers the friend on port 22, and its own ping to the laptop works. Only the address 192.168.1.32 gets no answer."
  - "Right. The rule sends port 8080 to an address nobody owns, so the packet disappears. Change the rule's target to the laptop, 192.168.1.23, and test from outside again."
  - "Masquerade rewrites the source of packets that leave. The request here never gets as far as needing it, because it is sent to an address that nobody owns."
```

## Level 2: two servers, one port

Mina fixed her rule, and her laptop's page now opens for her friend. Now her brother wants to share a photo page that runs on his phone, `192.168.1.24`, also on port 80. Mina's first idea is to forward port 80 of the router to the phone as well, so both pages live at `203.0.113.1:80`.

Think about what one outside address and one port can name. A packet that arrives at the router carries a destination address and a destination port. The router must pick exactly one device for it. If two rules ask for the same outside address and the same outside port, there is no way for the router to know which of the two the friend meant. In the order of the rules, the first match wins, so the second rule would never be used.

She collects three facts.

| What she tried | What she saw |
|---|---|
| Rule A: `203.0.113.1:80` to the laptop, port 80 | The friend opens the laptop's page |
| Rule B added: `203.0.113.1:80` to the phone, port 80 | The friend still gets the laptop's page, never the phone's |
| Both devices, checked from inside | Each page opens fine on its own port 80 |

The facts say both web servers are healthy, and the clash is in the one outside address and port. The inside ports do not have to change, since the laptop and the phone are different devices and each can listen on its own port 80. The part that has to change is the outside side of the rules. Each page needs its own outside port. The same trick that you saw in 6.3, with 8080 outside and 80 inside, solves it.

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "The laptop and the phone both run a web page on port 80. How can a friend reach each one through the single outside address?"
options:
  - "Forward outside port 80 to both devices at once"
  - "Give the phone the router's outside address"
  - "Forward 8080 to the laptop, 8081 to the phone"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "One outside address and one port can name only one destination. With two rules for the same address and port, the first match wins, and the other device is never reached."
  - "Two devices cannot both hold the router's address. The phone keeps its private address, and the router decides where outside packets go."
  - "Right. Each page gets its own outside port, so one outside address plus one port names one destination. The inside ports can both stay at 80."
```

## Level 3: the provider adds a NAT

A month later Mina's provider changes her connection. Her router's own page now says its outside address is `100.72.14.9`, and a website says it sees `203.0.113.45`. You know what this means from 6.4: there is a second NAT, run by the provider, between her router and the internet. Her forwarding rule is exactly as it was and her home devices work as before, but her friend can no longer open her page, however he tries.

She collects four facts.

| What she tried | What she saw |
|---|---|
| Her router's page | Outside address `100.72.14.9`, which is in the shared block |
| A website's view of her | `203.0.113.45` |
| From the laptop, a request to a website | Works, as always |
| From the friend, a request to `203.0.113.45:8080` | Never arrives, however long he waits |

Think about the second NAT as a router with its own table. Mina's laptop opens a connection to a website, so that connection starts from inside. The provider's NAT sees the packet go out and makes a row, and the reply finds its way home. But the friend's packet is different. It starts from outside, and it arrives at the provider's NAT, which has no row for it and no rule from Mina. Her forward rule lives in her own router, which is behind the provider's. The packet never gets that far. And Mina cannot write a rule on the provider's router.

What still works? Everything that starts from inside. That is why a chat app or a call app, which opens a connection from the phone, keeps working behind a provider NAT. A relay, as in 6.5, sits at a place both sides can reach from inside, and it passes the traffic between them. There are other ways around the problem, such as a tunnel, and IPv6, which has enough addresses that NAT is less needed. Those come later in the course and are not graded here.

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Mina's forward rule is correct, but a provider NAT now sits in front of her router. Why does the friend's request fail, and what still works?"
options:
  - "Provider NAT has no row for outside packets; her own requests still work"
  - "Provider NAT deletes her forward rule; her own connections stop working too"
  - "Provider NAT blocks only port 8080; any other outside port would reach her"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. The provider's NAT has no row for a packet that starts outside, and Mina cannot add a rule there. Connections that start from inside still create rows, so a relay can carry the traffic."
  - "The provider's NAT cannot touch the rules inside her router, and her laptop's own requests to websites still work. The failure is about packets that start outside."
  - "The reason is not the port number. The provider's NAT has no row and no rule for any packet that starts outside, so changing the port would not help."
```

## What to take away

You solved three different problems with the same method. In level 1 you used a fact from inside the home, the router's ping, to find a target that nobody owned. In level 2 you saw that one outside address and port can name only one destination, and you gave each page its own outside port. In level 3 you saw the limit of what Mina can do. A forwarding rule only works if the packet can reach her router, and a provider NAT in front of it stops that. What still works is every connection she starts herself.

Notice what the three levels share. In each, the symptom was a friend who waits and gets nothing. But the cause was in a different place each time: a wrong target, a clash of ports, and a router that Mina does not control. The first question is always the same: where does the packet stop? If you want to check your reasoning, read the explanation of every option, not only the right one. The wrong options are the mistakes people really make, and each explanation says why it fails. Level 1 answers it with a ping from the router. Level 2 answers it by looking at which page opened. Level 3 answers it by comparing the router's page with what a website sees.
