This is the gate for module 6, and it asks the question that the whole course is built around. All your devices are connected to your router, and none of them has a public IPv4 address. So how do they talk to the internet? You have already built the answer. Your evidence is a short explanation, in your own words, that uses one line from your own capture and one line from your own conntrack output. You have met every piece in the last ten chapters, so nothing here is new. The gate asks you to put the pieces together. Your answer saves as you type, so you can stop after a power cut and come back.

## Go back to your first numbers

Open the log you wrote in 1.1. On your computer you wrote an address like `192.168.1.23`, and a website showed you a different number, like `203.0.113.45`. At that time you could not say why. Now you can. The first number is the one your device uses inside your home. The second is the router's outside address, which the website sees. In between, your router rewrote the source of every packet. Your explanation should start from your own two numbers. Use the ones from your log, not the ones in this course.

## What your explanation must cover

Imagine a friend who knows what an address is, and nothing more. Five things make an explanation they can follow.

The first is the rewrite. Say that the router changes the source address of each packet that leaves, and say what it changes it to. Then say what happens to the port. Take care here: the port is kept unless two conversations would clash, and then the router gives the second one a new port. That is how the Linux router in your lab behaves. Other routers may differ.

The second is the table. Say that the router keeps one row for each conversation, and that the row is what sends a reply to the right device. Without the row, the router would not know who the reply is for.

The third is your evidence. Quote one line from your capture on the inside cable and one on the outside cable, and say which field changed between them. Then quote your conntrack line for the phone and point at the new port. Use your own lines. A line copied from this course proves nothing, because nobody else could have written yours.

The fourth is the other direction. Say why someone on the internet cannot start a conversation with your laptop. There are two reasons: the internet has no route to private addresses, and a packet to your router's outside address has no row and no rule, so your lab router refuses it. Then say how a forwarding rule changes that for exactly one port.

The fifth is one sentence about carrier-grade NAT. Say that a provider can run its own NAT in front of yours, and what that does to forwarding. About 250 words are enough, or about three minutes if you speak. A voice note is as good as text.

## A worked example, for a different question

Here is the same shape of answer for a different question: why did the guest in challenge 4 reach the server but time out when it asked for the laptop's page? It uses a different part of your work, so it shows the shape without giving away your answer.

A weak answer says: "The guest is blocked because of the firewall." It is not wrong in spirit, but it names nothing. Your friend cannot repeat it, and cannot check it. It also hides a mistake, because the router has no firewall until someone writes a rule.

A strong answer says: "The router has two kinds of rule. Masquerade rewrites the source of packets that leave, so the guest reaches the server, which sees the router's address. A rule in the forward chain drops packets from the guest network to the home network. A drop gives no answer, so the guest's request waits three seconds and times out. That is different from port 22, which is refused at once because nothing listens there." Look at what the strong answer does. It names the two rules, it says what each one does, and it ends with a result your friend can test. Your answer has the same three moves for the course question: the parts, what each does, and a result you can check.

## Weak answers to look out for

Three weak answers are common. The first is "NAT hides my devices". It sounds right, but it explains nothing: hides how, and from whom? The second is "my router has a firewall that blocks the internet". That mixes up two jobs. NAT rewrites, and a filter blocks, and in your lab the router had no filter until you added one. The third is "port forwarding is how my devices get internet". It is the other way round. Devices reach out without any forwarding. Forwarding is only for letting a friend in.

A strong answer avoids all three. It names the rewrite, the table and the port rule, and it uses your own lines.

## If your result was different

Two results change the answer a little. Neither one is a mistake, and neither needs a new idea.

If your public address in 1.1 was a long number with colons, it was an IPv6 address. This module is about IPv4, so run `curl -4 ifconfig.me` on your own computer, and use the IPv4 answer. IPv6 comes in module 9. Do not post the number, because a public address is personal.

If your router's page and a website show different public addresses, or your router's address starts with `100.`, the connection probably has a second NAT in front of your home. That is the case in 6.4. Say so in your answer, and use the private hops in your own trace from 6.4 as your evidence. If you use a phone as a hotspot, the trace is all you need.
