The gate asks you to put the pieces together in your own words. Your answer saves as you type, so you can stop after a power cut and come back.

A mentor reads your answer within three days. You do not have to wait. This is the last module of the course, so there is no next module to unlock, and if the mentor has a question, it reaches you while you work. You answer it by editing and sending your answer again.

## What your explanation must cover

Your friend knows that a phone has an address, and that is all. Four things make an explanation they can follow.

The first is one reason IPv6 helps. Tie it to a number or to something you saw. You can use the size: an IPv6 address has 128 bits, where an IPv4 address has 32 [S2]. You can use the subnet: one `/64` holds 2^64 addresses, so every device can have its own global address and nobody has to share. Or you can use your own phone from chapter 9.1. Say what the reason changes in a home, for example that NAT is no longer needed to make addresses last.

The second is one reason IPv4 and NAT will last. Tie this one to a number too. The free pools ran out, or reached their last block, on four dates: IANA on 3 February 2011 [S13], APNIC on 15 April 2011 [S14], ARIN on 24 September 2015 [S16] and RIPE NCC on 25 November 2019 [S15]. Yet IPv4 still works, because sharing keeps it working. A provider can put many customers behind one address, and the range `100.64.0.0/10` is kept for that [S11]. A study with data from 2014 to 2016 found that carrier NAT was used in over 92 percent of cellular networks [S34]. That study is old, so say that its data is dated. And Google counted 50.10 percent of its users on IPv6 on 28 March 2026 [S33]. So about half of its users did not reach it over IPv6 that day.

The third is care with words. "IPv4 is dead" is false, and so is "IPv6 will replace it next year". Both run side by side on the same devices, which is called dual stack [S74]. Say what is true: the shortage is real, and sharing has covered it so far.

The fourth is clarity. Write for your friend, not for a mentor. Use no word that you have not explained in the same sentence. About 200 words are enough, or about two minutes if you speak. A voice note is as good as text.

One more thing about privacy. Your real global address can point at your network. If you mention it, use a stand-in from the documentation prefix `2001:db8::/32` [S69], such as `2001:db8:4a1:20::5c`. Do not post your real one in a public place.

## A worked example, for a different question

Here is the same shape of answer for a different question: is NAT a firewall? It uses a different topic from the one you will write about, so it shows the shape without giving away your answer.

A weak answer says: "NAT hides your devices, so it keeps hackers out." It sounds right, but your friend cannot check it, and it is not what the lab showed.

A strong answer says: "In our lab, the home router only did masquerade. A server outside added one route to the private network and then reached the laptop at once. The internet normally has no route to private ranges, and that is what keeps strangers out, not the NAT. So NAT changes the source address, but it does not decide who may connect. A filter does that, and with IPv6 the filter is all that is left to do the job." This answer states what happened in the lab. It gives the reason. And it ends with a consequence the friend can follow.

Your answer has the same three moves, for a different question: the fact or number, the reason, and what it means for your friend. The weak answer has none of them.

## If your result was different

Two results change the answer a little. Neither one is a mistake, and neither needs a new idea.

If your phone and computer showed only a link-local address, your network does not offer IPv6. That is normal. Use that as your observation: your own network is one of the networks that still depends on IPv4, and that supports your second reason. You can still give your first reason from the numbers of chapter 9.2.

If you are on mobile data, your phone's IPv4 address may be in `100.64.0.0/10`, which runs from `100.64` to `100.127`. That range is shared space [S11]. It is a sign that your provider shares addresses between customers, but it is not proof. You can say this in your second reason, as long as you say that it is a sign and not proof.
