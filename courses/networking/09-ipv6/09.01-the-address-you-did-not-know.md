Open your phone's Wi-Fi details. Next to the familiar `192.168.x.x` there is often a long address with colons, such as `2001:db8:4a1:20::5c`. Where did a second address come from? Your devices very likely hold one already, and this chapter shows where to look.

## Your phone has two addresses

<MermaidDiagram title="A phone's Wi-Fi details, with example numbers" chart={`flowchart TD
  W["Phone: Wi-Fi details (example numbers)"] --> A["IP address: 192.168.1.24 (IPv4)"]
  W --> L["IPv6 address: fe80::1c2b:3aff:fe4d:5e6f (link-local)"]
  W --> G["IPv6 address: 2001:db8:4a1:20::5c (global)"]
`} legend="One phone, three lines. The first is the IPv4 address from module 1. The other two are IPv6 addresses. The numbers are made up for this course." />

Many Wi-Fi screens show more than one address. The short one with dots is IPv4. The long ones with colons are IPv6. This phone has two: one starts with `fe80` and stays on your own link [S3], and one is a global address.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a phone needs a second address">

Module 2 showed that the free pools of IPv4 addresses ran out. IPv6 is the long-term answer. An IPv6 address has 128 bits, where an IPv4 address has 32 bits [S2]. Chapter 9.2 shows how to read one.

Your phone does not have to choose between the two. It can hold both at the same time. A site that has an IPv6 address can be reached over IPv6. A site that has only an IPv4 address is reached over IPv4. Chapter 9.3 calls this dual stack.

The two IPv6 lines in the picture are two different kinds of address. A <GlossaryTerm term="link-local address" definition="An IPv6 address that starts with fe80. It works only on your own link and is never forwarded off it." /> is for talking to the neighbours on your own link, and routers never forward it off that link [S3]. A <GlossaryTerm term="global address" definition="An IPv6 address that can be reached from the internet. It is the one other networks see." /> is the one other networks see. Your phone shows both because both are real, and both are in use.

## Find your own IPv6 addresses

<MermaidDiagram title="Which line is which" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Run the command for your system"] --> Q{"Any address with colons?"}
  Q -->|"starts with fe80"| L["Link-local: stays on your link"]
  Q -->|"another long address, not ::1"| G["Global: your network offers IPv6"]
  Q -->|"only fe80 lines"| N["No global address offered: normal"]
`} legend="Read each address with colons and sort it into one of three boxes. The last box is a normal result, not a fault." />

Run the command for your system and look for addresses with colons. One that starts with `fe80` is link-local. Another long address, apart from `::1` [S3], is probably global. If you see only `fe80`, nothing is broken: your network does not offer IPv6.

```
# Windows, in PowerShell
ipconfig

# macOS, in Terminal (not yet checked on a Mac)
ifconfig

# Linux, in a terminal
ip addr
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the lines look like, and what to keep private">

Phone screens differ. If your Wi-Fi details show no long address, use your computer instead. Run the command above, and read the lines for the network adapter you are really using. A computer often has several adapters, and some of them are virtual.

The lab you built in module 1 shows how a Linux line looks. This is from the course lab, not from your computer, so your own lines will differ. The new interface has an IPv4 address and, next to it, a link-local address with `/64` and the words `scope link`:

```
    inet 192.168.1.10/24 scope global veth-laptop
...
    inet6 fe80::9084:33ff:fe2d:7d3f/64 scope link tentative
```

The words at the end of the second line are a status note. You can ignore them for now. The part to read is the address. It starts with `fe80`, so it is link-local, and the lab never gave it a global address. That is the normal result for a network that offers no IPv6.

If you do find a global address, write it down for the gate in 9.5. Do not paste it into a public place, such as a public repository or a forum post. A global address can point at your network. When you share a screenshot, use a stand-in from the documentation prefix `2001:db8::/32` [S69]. This course always does the same.

<Callout type="info" title="A missing global address is not a fault">
On a network without IPv6, a device has only the link-local address. Your computer and phone still work. This chapter's checklist accepts "none was offered" as a complete answer.
</Callout>
