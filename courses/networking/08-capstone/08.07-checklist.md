This is the gate for the capstone. You built a small internet from namespaces. It has a home subnet and a guest subnet behind a router that does NAT. It has one forwarded port, DHCP and DNS, a provider router and a public web server. You broke it with a seeded script and repaired it from evidence. Now you show it and explain it. Nothing here is new. The gate asks you to put what you built into your own words and point at your own proof.

Your evidence is three things: the repository with your four parts, the fault write-up, and a recorded demo of five minutes or less. A mentor reads them within three days. You do not have to wait. When the mentor has questions, they arrive as feedback, and you answer by editing and sending your answer again. Plan about 20 of the 60 minutes for that. Your answer saves as you type, so you can stop after a power cut and come back.

## What your demo must show

Record your screen with your voice. Any recorder is fine, and you do not have to download anything new. The template file `demo-template.md` is in your repository. It sets five parts and their times. Do not read it out loud. Show your own network, and say what you see.

| Time | Show | Say |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:40 | The address plan table and `ip addr` for the home router | Each subnet, why that size, and where the pool sits |
| 0:40 to 1:30 | A device getting its lease: the `udhcpc` output and the `lease applied` line | What the lease contained, and where the name comes from |
| 1:30 to 3:30 | One `curl` from `home1` to `203.0.113.80:8080`, with a capture on both sides of the router and the `conntrack -L` line | The source address inside, the source address outside, whether the port changed, and why |
| 3:30 to 4:40 | One injected fault: the seed, the symptom, the command that found it, the fix | Which layer it was, and why you looked there first |
| 4:40 to 5:00 | Your answer to the course question | Why home devices reach the internet without public addresses |

Label every command **On your computer** or **In the lab**, out loud or on the screen. The lab cannot show the real path to the internet. In the lab container, a trace to a real address hides the routers in between, because Docker's own network stands in the way [D5.5]. So never run a real-internet command in the lab and call it part of the demo. Run those on your computer, and say so.

Keep the video small. Five minutes at a low resolution is enough, if the text on the screen is easy to read. Close other windows first, so that no private message or name is on the screen. Use a link that only people with the link can open.

## What a strong explanation sounds like

Here is the same shape of answer for a question that is not on the list: why does the server see TTL 62, and not 64? It uses a different fact from the one you will explain, so it shows the shape and does not give away your answer.

A weak answer says: "The TTL goes down on the way, so it is lower." It is not wrong, but it explains nothing. Your listener cannot repeat it, and cannot check it.

A strong answer says: "A Linux computer starts its packets at TTL 64 unless that setting is changed, and the lab does not change it [S28]. Each router lowers the TTL by at least one [S30]. My packet crossed two routers, `hrouter` and `prouter`, so it arrives with 62. A lab capture across two routers showed the same number [D5.8]. If I added a third router, I would expect 61." This answer gives the starting value, the rule, the count from the picture, and a consequence that can be tested.

Look at what the strong answer does. It states a fact that can be checked. It says why that fact leads to the number. And it ends with something the listener can test. Your probe answers have the same three moves, and they point at your own capture.

## The five questions

A mentor picks three of these five. You answer in writing, in a voice note or in a short video. A good answer is short, and it points at a line of your own output.

1. Why does the server see only one address?
2. When does the source port change?
3. What breaks if the guest subnet is a `/30`?
4. Why can the server reach your forwarded port but not any other port on a home device?
5. Which test would catch a missing default route on your router?

Think about them before you record. Question 3 needs the arithmetic you did in modules 2 and 3. Question 5 needs the layer order from part 4. The rest need your captures from part 2. Do not learn a speech. If your answer does not match your own capture, the mentor will see it.

## How the mentor reads your work

The mentor has under 30 minutes, so the review is short. About 3 minutes go on your address plan, 6 on the network, 5 on the captures, 6 on the diagnosis and 8 on your explanation. The table shows what the mentor looks for in each part.

| Criterion | Meets looks like | Not yet looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Address plan | Every link has its own subnet with no overlap, sizes are justified, pools sit inside their subnets and leave room for static addresses, public-looking addresses come from the documentation ranges, and the plan matches `ip addr` | Overlapping ranges, an unexplained size, or a plan that differs from the running network |
| Working network | The test sets pass from a clean lab (build, test, teardown); each part has a must-fail test that fails for the right reason | Tests pass only after manual fixes, or a must-fail test fails for a different reason |
| Captures as evidence | Captures from both sides of the router are committed, labelled with where they ran, with a one-line reading and the `conntrack -L` line | Captures without labels, one side only, or output pasted but not read |
| Diagnosis | The write-up gives the seed and, for each fault, the symptom, the finder command with output, the fix and a passing re-run, in layer order | A fix with no evidence of how it was found, or guesses |
| Explanation | In the demo and answers you explain why the server sees one address and when the port changes, pointing at your own capture without notes | Restating the lecture, or answers that do not match your own capture |

## Capstone checklist

- [ ] All four tests pass from a clean lab: parts 1, 2 and 3, and the `test.sh` of part 4
- [ ] The scripts build from nothing and tear down cleanly
- [ ] `results.md` and the captures from both sides of the router are committed for each part
- [ ] `fault-writeup.md` has the seed and three faults, each with a symptom, a finder and a fix
- [ ] Every command in the write-up is one I ran
- [ ] My demo is five minutes or under, and every command is labelled
- [ ] I can say why the server sees one address, and point at my capture

## Learning log

1. **The hardest fault:** Which of your three faults was hardest to find? What made it easier, and what would you check first next time?
2. **The surprise:** Which field of the packet changed at the router when you did not expect it, or stayed the same? Write one sentence.
3. **Your plan:** If you built the address plan again, what is one number you would change, and why?

## If your result was different

Two things can differ, and neither is a mistake. If you used `restore.sh` or your mentor's help for one part, say so in your demo and in the write-up, and say which part. The mentor wants to know what you built yourself. And if your recorder only makes a large file, record a shorter demo that covers the same five parts, with less detail in each. Time inside the five minutes matters more than polish.
