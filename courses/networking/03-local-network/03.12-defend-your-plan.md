This is the gate for module 3. Your evidence is the school plan you wrote in 3.10, defended against one change. A fourth computer lab of 40 devices arrives. Where does it go? What changes? And what would you do if no space were free? You have met every piece in the last eleven chapters, so nothing here is new. The gate asks you to put the pieces together, in your own words and with your own numbers. Your answer saves as you type, so you can stop after a power cut and come back.

A mentor reads your answer within three days. You do not have to wait. You can carry on to module 4 as soon as you submit, and if the mentor has a question, it reaches you while you work. You answer it by editing and sending your answer again. Your tests from 3.7 and 3.8 and your commit from challenge 1 part 2 are part of the evidence as well, so check that they pass before you submit.

## What your answer must cover

Imagine that you are explaining the change to the school's network manager. She has your table in front of her and she wants to know whether she can say yes. Four things make an answer she can use.

The first is the place. Name the block for the new lab: its prefix, its first address and its last address. Say why that prefix, using the need and the 20% margin. Then show that the block is on its own boundary, and that it overlaps nothing in your table. A manager cannot approve "somewhere near the end". She can approve a block with a start and an end that she can check.

The second is the cost. Say what changes. A strong answer shows that nothing changes, and no device gets a new address. A plan that leaves space on purpose can take a new network without touching the old ones. If your answer moves other blocks, say which ones and how many devices must change address, because every changed address means a visit to every device.

The third is the case with no space. Imagine that the free space in your plan is not there. Say what you would do. You may shrink a block, ask for more addresses, or move a group. Whatever you choose, say what it costs. Shrinking a block removes its margin or changes its addresses. Be honest about that cost, because a choice with no cost named is not yet a decision.

The fourth is clarity. Write for the manager, not for a mentor. Use no word you have not explained in the same sentence. Use the real numbers from your own table. About 200 words are enough, or about two minutes if you speak. A voice note is as good as text.

## A worked example, for a different change

Here is the same shape of answer for a different school change. It uses the clinic from 3.10. The clinic has the block `10.50.0.0/23`. Its plan is guest `10.50.0.0/24`, staff `10.50.1.0/26` and instruments `10.50.1.64/27`. The free space is `10.50.1.96` to `10.50.1.255`. Now a pharmacy with 60 devices arrives. It uses the clinic numbers from 3.10, so you can compare shapes without copying your own answer.

A weak answer says: "Put the pharmacy after the instruments, at 10.50.1.96, as a /25." It sounds reasonable, but it fails the check. A `/25` must start where its last number is 0 or 128. The address `10.50.1.96` is neither, so a `/25` written there is really the block `10.50.1.0/25`. That block holds the staff and the instruments, so the answer creates an overlap. The writer never did the AND.

A strong answer says: "Sixty devices plus 20% is 72, so the pharmacy needs a /25, which has 126 usable addresses. Guests use the first half of the /23, so a /25 can start only at 10.50.1.0 or 10.50.1.128. The first is taken by staff. The second is free, so the pharmacy gets 10.50.1.128/25, from 10.50.1.128 to 10.50.1.255. No other block moves and no device changes address. What is left is 10.50.1.96 to 10.50.1.127, which is 32 addresses, enough for one small /27 later. If the pharmacy needed a /24, the free space would be 160 addresses, but there is no aligned /24 free. I would then ask for a second block, because shrinking the guest network would remove its margin and change every guest address."

Look at what the strong answer does. It states the need and the prefix with a reason. It does the boundary check and names the one start that works. It says what changes, which is nothing, and it names the leftover space. And for the case with no space, it makes a choice and names the cost. There is one more lesson hidden in it. A calculator plans from nothing. If you gave `splitBlock` all four needs at once, it would sort them and put the pharmacy before staff. Staff and instruments would move to new addresses. A real network is already running, so the better answer fits the new block into the free space and leaves everything else alone.

## If your plan was different

Your table may not look like the one in the worked example, and that is fine. If your free space is split into several small pieces, say so, and say which piece you would use. If you cannot find an aligned block for the fourth lab in your own plan, that is not a failure. It is a finding, and it is exactly the case in the third part of the question. Answer that part carefully, with the cost of your choice named. Use your own numbers, and do not copy the numbers from the example above.

If you did not finish the plan in 3.10, go back and finish it first. The gate defends a plan, and without a table there is nothing to defend.
