In 11.1 the internet dropped your packet for `10.20.0.5`, because it carries only public addresses. Yet with a VPN the same ping works. If the internet only routes public addresses, how can a packet to `10.20.0.5` cross it?

## Wrap the private packet in a public one

<MermaidDiagram title="One packet inside another" chart={`flowchart TD

  end
  A --> B --> C --> D --> E
`} legend="Top to bottom, the layers of one outer packet. The outer IPv4 and UDP headers use public addresses, so the internet can carry it. Inside is a WireGuard message, and inside that, locked, is the ping to the private address." />

The trick is old and simple. Your real packet, the ping to `10.20.0.5`, becomes the cargo of a second packet. The second packet goes between two public addresses: the café's and the office's. Routers on the way read only the outer header, as you saw in 1.6. This wrapping is called a <GlossaryTerm term="tunnel" definition="A way to carry one packet as the cargo of another packet." />.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the oldest form, and WireGuard's form">

RFC 2003, from October 1996, describes the plainest tunnel: an IP packet carried as the payload of another IP packet [S120]. The outer packet has its own source and destination, and the routers on the way only look at those. The RFC also names the obvious cost: the wrapped packet is bigger than the original [S120].

WireGuard, the VPN you build in this module, uses the same idea with two changes. First, it puts the inner packet inside UDP, so every WireGuard packet on the internet is a UDP packet [S112]. It cannot run over TCP [S115]. UDP fits, because a lost packet is the inner program's problem, as you saw in 5.5. Second, it locks the inner packet: it is encrypted, and a 16-byte check value at the end lets the other side prove that nobody changed it [S112].

The addresses in the picture are the ones in challenge 8. The café's public address is `198.51.100.2`, and the office's is `203.0.113.2`. The laptop has a second address, `10.99.0.2`, that exists only inside the tunnel. Chapter 11.3 explains where it comes from.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the sizes and the timing still tell">

The capture above is from the recorded run of challenge 8, part 3. The café router's outside plug carried 16 packets while the laptop pinged `10.20.0.5` three times and fetched a page. All 16 were UDP between port 51820 on both ends.

Look at the pattern, not the content. Six packets of `length 128`, in pairs, about one second apart. That is the shape of three pings and three answers, even though the café cannot open them. The page fetch shows as packets of other sizes, 96, 112, 160 and one of 304, in a burst. So a tunnel hides **what** you send, but the sizes and the timing stay visible. Chapter 11.7 returns to this.

The café also learns one more thing: `203.0.113.2` on port 51820. Port 51820 is only a habit, not a rule, as 11.3 explains. But a watcher who sees UDP on 51820 can guess that this is a WireGuard tunnel. WireGuard does not aim to hide that it is WireGuard [S115].

## Inside the VPN box: the ping again

<MermaidDiagram title="The VPN box opens the outer packet" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Outer packet arrives<br>UDP 198.51.100.2 to 203.0.113.2"] --> B["Office router forwards UDP 51820<br>to the VPN box, 10.20.0.2"]
  B --> C["VPN box checks and unlocks it"]
  C --> D["wg0 shows the inner packet<br>10.99.0.2 to 10.20.0.5, ICMP echo request"]
  D --> E["Passed on to the test server"]
`} legend="Left to right: the outer packet reaches the office, is forwarded to the VPN box, and is checked and unlocked there. On the VPN box's tunnel interface, wg0, the inner ping is plain again." />

At the other end, the VPN box unlocks the cargo. Before you read its capture, say which two addresses and which protocol you expect. Its tunnel interface, `wg0`, shows the inner packet as a normal ping. Same moment, two views: outside, UDP between public addresses; inside, ICMP from `10.99.0.2` to `10.20.0.5`. The office sees everything the café could not.

```
listening on wg0, link-type RAW (Raw IP), snapshot length 262144 bytes
16:16:38.604418 IP 10.99.0.2 > 10.20.0.5: ICMP echo request, id 879, seq 1, length 64
16:16:38.604475 IP 10.20.0.5 > 10.99.0.2: ICMP echo reply, id 879, seq 1, length 64
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why wg0 has no hardware addresses">

The capture says `link-type RAW (Raw IP)`. In 1.4 every capture had hardware addresses, because a cable carries Ethernet frames. `wg0` is not a cable. Packets that go into it come out at the other end of the tunnel, so there is no neighbour to address on a link. tcpdump shows the bare IP packet.

The ping lines look just like the ones from 1.4. `length 64` is the ICMP part: 56 bytes of data and an 8-byte ICMP header. With the 20-byte IP header, the inner packet is 84 bytes. That is the `56(84)` that `ping` prints on its first line. The next beat starts from those 84 bytes.

The office is the other end of the tunnel, so the office can read every inner packet. That is fine for a company network: you trust your own office. With a paid VPN app, the other end is a company you pay, and it sees the same. Chapter 11.7 is about exactly that.

</GoDeeper>

## 84 bytes in, 156 bytes out

<MermaidDiagram title="The outer size, one layer at a time" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Inner ping: 84 bytes"] --> B["Pad to a multiple of 16: 96"]
  B --> C["Add 32 of WireGuard: 128<br>the UDP length in the capture"]
  C --> D["Add 8 of UDP header: 136"]
  D --> E["Add 20 of IPv4 header: 156<br>the outer length in the capture"]
`} legend="Top to bottom, the running total. Each box adds one layer. Two of the totals appear in the capture: 128 as the UDP length, and 156 as the length of the whole outer packet." />

84 is not a multiple of 16, so WireGuard pads it to 96. Its own 32 bytes make 128: that is the `UDP, length 128` in the café's capture. UDP adds 8, and IPv4 adds 20, so the whole outer packet is 156 bytes. A capture with `-v` prints both numbers. Your sum must match it.

```
inner 84, padded 96, outer 156
16:16:40.725329 IP (tos 0x0, ttl 63, id 12214, offset 0, flags [none], proto UDP (17), length 156)
    198.51.100.2.51820 > 203.0.113.2.51820: UDP, length 128
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the 32 bytes come from">

A WireGuard data message has four fields before the cargo: a type of 1 byte, 3 reserved bytes, a receiver index of 4 bytes and a counter of 8 bytes [S112]. That is 16 bytes of header.

Then comes the inner packet, locked, and padded with zeros to a multiple of 16 bytes [S112, S111]. At the end is a 16-byte check value, often called a tag, which proves the message was not changed [S112]. So WireGuard adds 16 + 16 = 32 bytes, plus the padding.

To round up to a multiple of 16 by hand: divide by 16, round up, multiply by 16. 84 / 16 is 5.25, which rounds up to 6, and 6 x 16 = 96. The lab script does the same with `(INNER + 15) / 16 * 16` in whole numbers. Another check from the course's research test: a ping with `-s 100` has 100 + 8 + 20 = 128 bytes inside. 128 is already a multiple of 16, so there is no padding, and the capture showed a UDP payload of 160, which is 128 + 32 [S142].

</GoDeeper>

## Room for the wrapping: MTU 1420

<MermaidDiagram title="Why wg0 says mtu 1420" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["The link carries up to 1500 bytes"] --> B["The worst case outer wrapping:<br>IPv6 40 + UDP 8 + WireGuard 32 = 80"]
  B --> C["Room left for the inner packet:<br>1500 - 80 = 1420"]
`} legend="Left to right: the biggest packet the link carries, minus the most the wrapping can take, gives the biggest inner packet that still fits." />

A link carries packets up to a size, called the <GlossaryTerm term="MTU" definition="The biggest packet a link or interface carries in one piece, in bytes." />. In 1.4 your cable said `mtu 1500`, the usual size for Ethernet. A tunnel must leave room for its extra bytes, so WireGuard's `wg0` says `mtu 1420`: 1500 minus 80, enough even for an IPv6 outer header.

```
2: wg0: <POINTOPOINT,NOARP,UP,LOWER_UP> mtu 1420 qdisc noqueue state UNKNOWN mode DEFAULT group default qlen 1000
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what happens to a packet that is too big">

The 80 is arithmetic, not a number any WireGuard page states as a rule. WireGuard adds 32 bytes, UDP adds 8, and the outer IP header is 20 bytes for IPv4 or 40 for IPv6. That is 60 or 80 bytes. 1500 - 80 = 1420. The WireGuard whitepaper only shows `mtu 1420` in an example listing [S111], and the lab's `wg0` showed the same. The tool `wg-quick`, which reads a config file, picks the MTU from the endpoint addresses or the default route when you do not set one [S118].

Why does it matter? A packet bigger than the link's MTU cannot cross it in one piece. The sender must cut it into pieces, or the network drops it and tells the sender to send smaller ones. RFC 2003 says that when the inner packet says "do not cut me into pieces", the outer packet must say so too, so that this size feedback works [S120]. Here is a made-up example of what can go wrong. If that size feedback is blocked somewhere on the path, small packets get through and big ones vanish: a ping works, but a big download hangs. We found no real outage report to cite for this, so treat it as a risk to know, not as a story that happened.

With `mtu 1420` on `wg0`, the laptop never builds an inner packet bigger than 1420 bytes. After the wrapping, it still fits in 1500.

</GoDeeper>
