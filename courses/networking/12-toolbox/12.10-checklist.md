This is the gate for module 12. In the last eight chapters you met one tool at a time, and each chapter told you which fault to look for. Real problems do not come with a chapter name. A friend says "it is slow" or "it does not load", and you choose the tool. So this gate has three parts: your round 1 evidence, a blind round where nobody tells you what is broken, and a cheat sheet you keep for later. Nothing here is new. Your answers save as you type, so you can stop after a power cut and come back.

A mentor reads your hand-in within three days. You do not have to wait. You can carry on as soon as you submit, and if the mentor has a question, it reaches you while you work. You answer it by editing and sending your answer again.

## Part 1: your round 1 evidence

Open `challenges/challenge-9-toolbox/part-0-the-broken-network/faults.md`. Parts 3 to 6 each filled in one fault. Check that all four entries are there, each with four lines: the tool, the exact command you ran, the one output line that proves the fault, and the command that cleared it. Every line must be one you really ran and copied from your own screen, inside backticks.

The tests of parts 3 to 6 already checked each entry once. The gate test in part 2 below checks all four again, and matches each output line to the fault your seed planted in round 1. For fault 1 it wants an mtr row of hop 2 or hop 3 with loss, not hop 1. For fault 2 it wants a line with the wrong address the campus resolver gave for your name. For fault 3 it wants your app port with `filtered`. For fault 4 it wants an iperf3 receiver line near your speed limit. If an entry fails, the test says why, in words you now know: "loss at one hop only can be the router limiting its own replies", or "read the receiver line, not the sender line".

## Part 2: the blind round

When parts 3 to 6 pass, run the blind round. **In the lab**, from the challenge 9 folder:

```
cd /work/challenges/challenge-9-toolbox
bash part-0-the-broken-network/faults.sh --round 2
```

It builds the network again and plants **two** of the four kinds of fault, with new numbers from your seed. It does not say which two, and it prints no complaints. The service sheet is printed again, and read it carefully: the app has moved to a new port. Your round 1 answers do not carry over either. A wrong name, if there is one, may be another of the five names on the sheet, and any loss or limit has a new size.

Now work without the fault matrix and without the chapter names. Start from the four questions that ping and the trace could not answer in 12.1. Is anything lost, and where? Does any of the five names give a wrong address? Does the app's new port stay silent? Is any direction of the path slow? Each question has a tool in your toolbox, and each tool takes under a minute. Run every check before you decide, because a clean result is evidence too: it rules a fault out. One rule from 12.7 still holds. If you find loss, clear it before you measure speed, or the two faults mix in iperf3. Apart from that, you do not have to clear the round 2 faults. The gate test reads only `faults.md`.

Write both findings at the end of `faults.md`, under **Finding A** and **Finding B**. The template already has both headings, each with four lines: what it is (loss, a wrong DNS answer, a filtered port or a speed limit), the tool, the command and the output line. The command and the line must come from a run after `faults.sh --round 2`. Leave your round 1 entries as they are: the test checks them against your round 1 numbers. Then run the gate test, **in the lab**:

```
bash part-0-the-broken-network/test.sh --round 2
```

It checks all of `faults.md`: the four round 1 entries against your round 1 numbers, and the two findings against the two faults that round 2 really planted, with their new numbers. A finding fails if its line does not prove a fault that round 2 planted, with round 2's numbers, or if both findings prove the same fault. When it passes, commit `faults.md`.

## Part 3: your cheat sheet

Make one page, `cheat-sheet.md`, in the `challenge-9-toolbox` folder. For each of the seven tools, paste one real output from your own runs, cut to the lines that matter, and write one line on how to read it. For Wireshark, paste the display filter you used and what it showed, or the first lines of your followed stream. Keep it to one page. This is the page you open the next time a friend says "the internet is slow", so write it for yourself, in your own words. Commit it.

## A worked example, for a round 1 finding

Here is the shape of good evidence, for fault 3 from round 1 of the recorded run. It is not a round 2 fault, so it shows the shape without giving away your answer.

A weak finding says: "Tool: nmap. Output line: the port is blocked." It names a tool, but it quotes no line, and "blocked" could mean closed or filtered.

A strong finding says: "Tool: nmap. Command: `ip netns exec laptop nmap -n -p 80,81,7630 203.0.113.80`. Output line: `7630/tcp filtered hawk`." And in the explanation: "Port 81 in the same scan says `closed`, so the server does answer with a reset when nothing listens. 7630 got no answer at all. `ss -ltn` on the server shows the app listening on 7630. So the packets are dropped on the way, and the rule was in r2." The strong finding quotes one real line, says what it means, and uses a second line to rule out the other explanation.

Look at the last move. Faults 1 and 4 both make a copy slow. A strong finding for either one says why it is this fault and not the other: the retransmits, and whether mtr is clean.
