## The free pool emptied region by region

<MermaidDiagram title="Four free pools, four dates" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["3 February 2011: the free pool at IANA is empty"] --> B["15 April 2011: APNIC takes its final /8 block"]
  B --> C["24 September 2015: the free pool at ARIN reaches zero"]
  C --> D["25 November 2019: RIPE NCC runs out"]
`} legend="Time runs from the top to the bottom. IANA is the top of the system. APNIC, ARIN and RIPE NCC each give addresses to providers in one part of the world." />

New addresses come from a free pool. <GlossaryTerm term="IANA" definition="The group at the top that holds all addresses and gives big blocks to the registries." /> ran out first. The <GlossaryTerm term="regional registries" definition="Five organisations that give addresses to providers, each in one part of the world." /> followed: APNIC in April 2011, ARIN in 2015, RIPE NCC in 2019.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: who gives out addresses, and where 4.3 billion comes from">

An IPv4 address is 32 bits long [S1]. Chapter 2.2 shows what that means. Two to the power 32 is 4,294,967,296, which is the 4.3 billion in this chapter's title. RFC 791 fixed the size in 1981 [S1]. In 1981 that looked like more than anyone could use. Today billions of phones, laptops and servers want one address each.

Addresses go out in steps. IANA holds the whole space and gives big blocks to the five regional registries. The block size it used was a `/8`, which is one 256th of the space: 4,294,967,296 divided by 256 is 16,777,216 addresses [S13]. Chapter 2.3 explains the `/8` notation. In 2009 a global policy said that the last five `/8` blocks would be split equally among the five registries [S13]. So when IANA's pool was empty on 3 February 2011, every registry already held some blocks.

Each registry then kept giving out the blocks it held, and that is why the dates differ. APNIC took its final `/8` on 15 April 2011 [S14]. ARIN's free pool reached zero on 24 September 2015 [S16]. RIPE NCC announced that it had run out of IPv4 addresses on 25 November 2019 [S15].

## Ran out means the free pool is empty

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m2-scene-free-pool" />

The bar is the free pool: addresses that nobody uses yet. It drains to zero, and the web keeps working, because an address already in use stays in use. This is called <GlossaryTerm term="exhaustion" definition="The free pool of addresses is empty." />. Newcomers join a waiting list, or receive a block from a holder who transfers it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what changed, and what did not">

"Ran out" describes the free pool only. A website that had an address in 2010 still has it. Your phone's address this morning came from a block that your router or your provider already held. Nobody needed a new block for it.

For a new provider the world did change. At ARIN, requests continue through a waiting list or through transfers between holders [S16]. At RIPE NCC a waiting list gives an eligible member a single `/24` [S15]. A `/24` block holds 256 addresses, so one block is small. Chapter 2.3 shows how block sizes are written.

Two ideas cover the gap. The first is sharing. Many networks use the same private numbers inside (chapter 2.4), and one router can share one outside address among many devices (module 6). The second is a longer address. IPv6 has 128 bits instead of 32 [S2], and module 9 covers it. IPv4 is still everywhere, which is why this module spends its time on it.

One sentence to avoid: "the internet ran out of addresses". It is wrong. The free pool is empty. The internet still works, and addresses are now traded and shared.

</GoDeeper>
