## Your product sense toolkit

These are the habits you keep. Read each line and ask yourself whether you could do it now, without opening the chapter. If you could not, go back to that chapter once.

- Start from the problem: ask about the last time it happened, not whether someone would use a feature (1.2)
- Write the job as "When I ..., I want to ..., so that ..." (1.3)
- Set the time first, cut the scope to fit it, and say no with a reason (1.4)
- Pick one goal and one signal you can watch a person do, in a check with three to five people (1.5)
- Squint at the screen: one thing first, and no more than three sizes (2.2)
- Body text of 16 or more with a line height of about 1.5, and less space inside a group than between groups (2.3, 2.4)
- Contrast of 4.5 to 1 for normal text and 3 to 1 for large text and meaningful parts, and never colour alone (2.5)
- Design the four states of a screen: empty, loading, error and full (3.2)
- Keep labels visible, put errors next to their cause in words, aim for targets of 44 to 48, and move things only to show a change (3.3 to 3.5)
- Show the valuable thing first, and teach inside the screen (3.6)
- Judge with criteria and evidence, compare products on the same task, and check a popular rule before you repeat it (4.2 to 4.5)
- Choose your defaults on purpose, cut sentences a hundred products could say, brief an AI tool and check its critique (5.2 to 6.3)
- Show the result first, in a demo that works muted and a page that passes a skim, and open it in one click with a fallback (7.2 to 7.4)

## What changed in how you think

At the start of this course, a screen was either fine or ugly, and you could not say why. A feature was a good idea if you liked it. A project was finished when it ran. By now you should have a different set of questions. What problem does this solve, and for whom? What does a person see first? What happens when it is empty, slow or broken? What did the maker choose, and what did they leave as a default?

The biggest change is probably the way you give reasons. Taste, in this course, was never a private feeling. It was a rule, a piece of evidence and a choice, written down so that another person could argue with it. That is why the critique log has a column for what you gave up. A change with no cost is usually a change nobody examined.

The second change is the reader. You now picture a person who is busy, not an expert and often on a phone with the sound off. You build the first screen, the video and the link for that person. You do not need to be more famous or more skilled for this to work. You need to make the good part easy to reach.

The third change is how you use AI tools. They are fast and they have habits. They give you a first draft, and they give you opinions that are sometimes wrong and always confident. Your job is to ask for questions, check each point and decide with a reason.

## Your defence

You made a set of changes and wrote a reason for each. The last step of the course is to defend them. You have two ways, and the second one always completes the gate. The first way is the AI interviewer, which is optional. The second way is a written reply, which is required.

The AI interviewer is a text chat. It uses your own OpenRouter key, which you set in Settings, under AI interviews. Using it may cost a little credit on your own account. Check OpenRouter's terms, including its age rule, before you use it (checked 2026-10-25). It sends the text you type to the AI provider, so never type a client's data, a private detail about another person or anything secret.

Before you start, paste a short description of your redesign: the four parts of the capstone in a few lines. The interviewer asks what was wrong, what you changed and what you gave up. It follows with "why" questions, and it may ask where a number came from. The best answer to a question you cannot answer is the honest one: "I did not test that." An invented number is worse than a gap.

```aiinterview
title: Defend your redesign
opening: "Pick one change you made in your redesign. What was wrong before, what did you change, and what did you give up?"
focus: [case-study, best-technical]
context: project
maxTurns: 8
whyDepth: 2
required: false
rubric: "A strong defence names one real change and gives the problem in the person's words, not in design words. It gives the rule or the evidence behind the change, such as a contrast ratio, a state, a label rule or a result from a small check. It shows the change. It names the trade-off: what was given up and who it might affect. When the learner is asked for a number, they say where it came from, or they say honestly that they did not test it. A proxy result measured on the design is fine when it is labelled. Probe vague claims such as 'it looks better' or 'users liked it', and ask how the learner knows. Do not reward long answers. Probe any AI opinion the learner used: did they check it against a rule or a person, and did they reject any point?"
```

Whether or not you use the interviewer, write your reply below. It is a reply to a question from a reviewer, so write it in your own voice and keep it short.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor looks first for three real changes, not three ways of saying the same thing. They then check that each has a reason that someone else could test, such as a rule, a standard or a result from a small check. Finally they look for the trade-off. A change that gave up nothing makes a mentor ask what you did not look at.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three changes | Three different changes, each shown with what was wrong before and what it is now | Three changes, but two are nearly the same, or one is not shown clearly | Fewer than three |
| The reason | Each change has a rule, a standard or a result from a check | Two of the three have one | Reasons such as "it looks better" |
| What you gave up | Each change names a cost and who it may affect | Two of the three | No cost is named |
| Honesty | Every number has a source or a label, or you say you did not test it | One number has no source | An invented result |

**Pass rule:** your reply passes when no part is Not yet and at most one part is Partly.

The common weak reply is a list of three improvements, each followed by "because it looks better" and no cost. The next most common is a number with no source. Your mentor will usually ask one follow-up question about which change you would undo if a reviewer said it hurt them. If your answer needs more work, one or two short questions come back, and you answer them in writing.
