A busy reader sees mostly the <GlossaryTerm term="first screen" definition="The part of a page you see before you scroll. People also call it the part above the fold." /> of a page, so that is where the answer must be.

## Ten seconds on the old page

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m7-skim-before" />

Press Start and look for ten seconds. The page is honest and well written. It tells you who Maryam is and how many features she built. It does not tell you whether the redesign worked. The result is in the last section, far below this screen.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the evidence says about the first screen">

Two findings support putting the result first. An NN/g analysis from 2011 looked at 205,873 pages. Users often left within 10 to 20 seconds. Pages that made their value clear in the first 10 seconds could hold people for minutes. The data came from one browser plug-in [B58]. In an eye-tracking study with 120 people and more than 130,000 fixations, 57% of viewing time fell above the fold, and 74% in the first two screens. People did scroll when they had a reason [B59]. Both are research, and both are about web pages in general, not about case studies.

So "a ten-second skim" is the course's own test, not a fact about how reviewers behave. The ten-second figure in the first study is about pages, not portfolios. We use it as a target that is easy to check.

Here is the check. Show the first screen on a phone to someone who has never seen your project. Hide it after ten seconds. Ask three questions: what is it, who is it for, and did it work? A similar test, the five-second test, rests mostly on anecdote and fits simple screens, and for complex pages ten seconds may work better [A71]. Like the five-person check from module 1, it finds problems. It does not prove that the page is good.

</GoDeeper>

## Ten seconds on the new page

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m7-skim-after" />

Same button, same ten seconds, a different page. Now the biggest thing on the screen is the result: 4 of 5 joined, up from 2 of 5. The job and Maryam's role fit in the same screen. A reviewer can say what changed before the time is up.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the first screen is not a summary of everything">

This is practice. The new first screen does not try to say everything. It has one big number, one sentence about the job, two small tags and one button. The rest of the page waits below for readers who stay. That is the trade-off: the top is short, and the depth is below it.

Notice what the first screen leaves out. It does not list the 14 features, the tools Maryam used or the story of her weekends. Those may be true and interesting, but none of them answers "did it work?". A good test for any line on the first screen is whether a stranger would miss it if it were gone.

No source we found gives a right length for a case study. A set number of words has no support. The rule is simpler: short enough that the top can be skimmed in about ten seconds, with the detail below.

The words of your resume and profile, including how to write numbers about your work, belong to course 16. This chapter is about the shape of the page.

</GoDeeper>

## Result first, then the story behind it

<MermaidDiagram title="The order of a case study" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["1 Result: what changed, by how much"] --> P["2 Problem and job"]
  P --> O["3 Your role"]
  O --> C["4 Choices and what you gave up"]
  C --> B["5 Before and after, and how you know"]
`} legend="The result goes first because most viewing time falls near the top. The story behind it follows." />

Put the sections in this order. The result comes first. Then the problem and the job, your role, your choices and what you gave up, and last the before and after with how you know. Readers who stay get the depth. Readers who leave still got the point.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: one sentence for each section of Halaqa's page">

This is practice, built from what hiring managers say they want. Here is one sentence for each section of Maryam's page.

The result: "4 of 5 classmates joined a study group in their first visit, in about 2 minutes. Before: 2 of 5." Under it, in small print, goes how she knows: "a check with 5 classmates".

The problem and the job: "Students with an exam coming up often study alone because they cannot find classmates." Then the job, in the course's template: "When an exam is a few days away and I am stuck on a topic, I want to find two or three classmates to revise with tonight or tomorrow, so that I do not study alone."

Her role: "I designed and built it alone, in 12 hours over two weeks, with an AI coding tool." This line is honest about her part and about the tool.

Choices and what she gave up: "I kept one job. I gave up the features my cut list marked Not now. A student who wants those will not find them yet." This is the line reviewers rarely see, and it shows judgment.

Before and after: screenshots with numbered changes and the two results, with how she knows.

NN/g surveyed 204 hiring professionals in UX design. They said they want evidence of value delivered and the messy process, not only polished screens, and they prefer a few detailed case studies that are easy to scan [B60]. That survey is about designers, so we treat it as a strong hint for engineers, not a rule.

</GoDeeper>

## Write for a reader outside design

<MermaidDiagram title="Different readers, one need" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Recruiter new to design"] --> N["What is it, who is it for, did it work?"]
  B["Product manager"] --> N
  C["Hiring manager"] --> N
`} legend="Different readers need the same three answers, and they need them fast." />

Reviewers are often not designers. A recruiter new to design or a product manager may read your page. They need three answers fast: what is it, who is it for, and did it work? Skip words only a specialist knows, or explain them in a few words.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: writing and building for a busy reader">

NN/g reports that portfolios are often reviewed by recruiters or managers who are not designers [B61]. Its advice comes from interviews and surveys about UX designers, not engineers. Applying it to an engineering project is an inference by this course, so check it against the people who will really read your page.

In practice, write the first screen without field words. Say "I put one thing first on each screen" in place of "I improved the visual hierarchy", unless you explain the term in a few words. Give each screenshot a short text alternative, as you learned in 3.4, so the page works for a reader who cannot see it.

Most readers will open your page on a phone, perhaps on weak data. A heavy page may never finish loading. Decide a weight budget for the page, write it down and check it. The median mobile home page weighed about 2.3 MB in October 2024 [A48]. That is context, not a target for you.

Finally, test the page with someone who has never seen the project. If you explain it to them, the test is over. Only what is on the screen counts.

</GoDeeper>

## A before and after needs a result

<MermaidDiagram title="From old screens to a result" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Save the old screens first"] --> C["Change the design"]
  C --> T["Run the same check again"]
  T --> W["Write what changed and how you know"]
`} legend="Without the first step, there is no before to compare with." />

Two screenshots side by side do not say whether the change worked. Save the old screens before you start. Run the same small check after. Then write what changed and how you know: 2 of 5 before and 4 of 5 after, in a check with five classmates. If you did not test something, say so.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a result you can defend">

NN/g advises that a before and after is not enough. State whether the change worked and by how much, and capture the starting state before you redesign [B62]. This is practice from portfolio reviewers.

Maryam saved her old screens before she changed them. Her first check had 5 classmates, and 2 of them joined a group in their first visit. After the redesign, 4 of 5 did, in about 2 minutes. She compares ratios, not raw counts. A ratio still makes sense if the number of people changes [A30].

Be honest about what the check can show. Five people find most big problems for one group per round, and then you fix and test again. The check finds problems. It does not prove the design is good [A20]. Write that limit in one short line.

If you have no check, do not invent a number. Write "I have not tested this with users yet", and add the check you would run. An honest gap is better than a made-up result, and a reader can tell the difference. The AI interviewer in 7.7 may ask where each number came from.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Sufian wrote a case study for Rihla, a bus timetable app for a university (fictional). Four facts: 1) The first screen is a headline, 'Hi, I am Sufian', and three paragraphs about why he loves coding. 2) The result, '9 of 10 students found their bus in under a minute, in a check with 10 students', is in the last section. 3) The 'Try it' link is in the footer. 4) A recruiter who is new to design will open the page on a phone. What should he change first?"
options:
  - "Add a paragraph that explains his tools in more depth"
  - "Rewrite the page with design words for expert readers"
  - "Keep the order and add a second Try it link in the footer"
  - "Put the result and a Try it button on the first screen"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Fact 1 shows that the first screen already holds only paragraphs about him. Another paragraph pushes the result even further down."
  - "Fact 4 says the reader is a recruiter who is new to design. Design words would make the page harder for that reader to follow."
  - "Facts 2 and 3 show the problem is the order. A second link in the footer leaves the result in the last section and the link below the first screen."
  - "Right. Facts 2 and 3 put both the result and the link out of sight. A reader on a phone sees the first screen, so both belong there."
```
