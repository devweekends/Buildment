Maryam's first case study told the story of how she built Halaqa. It never answered the question Dalia had: did it work? A busy reader sees mostly the <GlossaryTerm term="first screen" definition="The part of a page you see before you scroll. People also call it the part above the fold." /> of a page, so that is where the answer must be. Short pages can still fail, as the next question shows. Maryam wants to write a page that a stranger can skim on a phone and still understand.

## Ten seconds on the old page

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m7-skim-before" />

Choose 10 or 20 seconds, then press Start. The screen is short, about 36 words, so you can read all of it. It tells you who Maryam is and what she built. It does not tell you whether the redesign worked. The result is in the last section, far below this screen.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the evidence says about the first screen">

Two findings support putting the result first. An analysis by NN/g in 2011 looked at 205,873 pages. Users often left within 10 to 20 seconds. Pages that made their value clear in the first 10 seconds could hold people for minutes. The data came from one browser plug-in. In an eye-tracking study with 120 people and more than 130,000 fixations, 57% of viewing time fell above the fold, and 74% in the first two screens. People did scroll when they had a reason. Both are research, and both are about web pages in general, not about case studies.

So "a ten-second skim" is the course's own test, not a fact about how reviewers behave. The ten-second figure in the first study is about pages, not portfolios. We use it as a target that is easy to check. If you read in a second language, take 20 seconds. The test still shows what the page puts first.

Here is the check. Show the first screen on a phone to someone who has never seen your project. Hide it after ten seconds. Ask three questions: what is it, who is it for, and did it work? A similar test, the five-second test, rests mostly on anecdote and fits simple screens. For complex pages, ten seconds may work better. Like the five-person check from module 1, it finds problems. It does not prove that the page is good.

</GoDeeper>

## Ten seconds on the new page

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m7-skim-after" />

Same button, same question, a different page. Now the biggest thing on the screen is the result: 4 of 5 joined, up from 2 of 5. The job and Maryam's role fit in the same screen. A reviewer can say what changed before the time is up, even at the slower setting. Compare this with the first page: the same space, a different first thing.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the first screen is not a summary of everything">

This is practice. The new first screen does not try to say everything. It has one big number, one sentence about the job, two small tags and one button. The rest of the page waits below for readers who stay. That is the trade-off: the top is short, and the depth is below it.

Notice what the first screen leaves out. It does not list the 14 features, the tools Maryam used or the story of her weekends. Those may be true and interesting, but none of them answers "did it work?". A good test for any line on the first screen is whether a stranger would miss it if it were gone.

No source we found gives a right length for a case study. A set number of words has no support. The rule is simpler: short enough that the top can be skimmed in about ten seconds, with the detail below.

The words of your resume and profile, including how to write numbers about your work, belong to course 16. This chapter is about the shape of the page.

</GoDeeper>

## Result first, then the story behind it

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m7-page-order" />

The same five sections sit in two orders, and the line shows where a phone's first screen ends. In story order, the first screen holds Maryam and her tools. With the result first, it holds the result and the problem. Readers who stay get the depth. Readers who leave still got the point. Many reviewers are not designers, so use plain words.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: one sentence for each section, and who reads them">

This is practice, built from what hiring managers say they want. Here is one sentence for each section of Maryam's page.

The result: "4 of 5 classmates joined a study group in their first visit, in about 2 minutes. Before: 2 of 5." Under it, in small print, goes how she knows: "a check with 5 classmates".

The problem and the job: students with an exam coming up often study alone, because they cannot find classmates. The job, in one sentence: a student with an exam coming up wants two or three classmates to revise with tonight, so that she does not study alone.

Her role: "I designed and built it alone, in 12 hours over two weeks, with an AI coding tool." This line is honest about her part and about the tool.

Choices and what she gave up: "I kept one job. I gave up the features my cut list marked Not now. A student who wants those will not find them yet." This is the line reviewers rarely see, and it shows judgment.

Before and after: screenshots with numbered changes and the two results, with how she knows.

NN/g surveyed 204 hiring professionals in UX design. They said they want evidence of value delivered and the messy process, not only polished screens. They prefer a few detailed case studies that are easy to scan. That survey is about designers, so we treat it as a strong hint for engineers, not a rule.

NN/g also reports that portfolios are often reviewed by recruiters or managers who are not designers. Applying this to an engineering project is an inference by this course, so check it against the people who will read your page. In practice, write the first screen without field words. Say "I put one thing first on each screen" in place of "I improved the visual hierarchy", unless you explain the term in a few words. Give each screenshot a short text alternative, as you learned in 3.4.

Most readers will open your page on a phone, perhaps on weak data. A heavy page may never finish loading. Decide a weight budget for the page, write it down and check it. The median mobile home page weighed about 2.3 MB in October 2024. That is context, not a target for you.

</GoDeeper>

## A before and after needs a result

<MermaidDiagram title="Which result can you show?" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Save the old screens first"] --> C["Change the design"]
  C --> Q{"Can 3 to 5 people try it?"}
  Q -->|"Yes"| A["Run the same check again"]
  Q -->|"No"| P["Measure the design itself"]
  A --> W["Write what changed, how you know and the limit"]
  P --> L["Label it: measured on the design"]
  L --> W
`} legend="A number measured on the design is honest when its label sits beside it." />

Save the old screens before you change anything, then run the same small check again. If nobody can try your design, measure the design itself: steps to the first action, contrast, or the squint test, before and after. Put the label next to the number, and never invent a result.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a result you can defend">

NN/g advises that a before and after is not enough. State whether the change worked and by how much, and capture the starting state before you redesign. This is practice from portfolio reviewers.

Maryam saved her old screens before she changed them. Her first check had 5 classmates, and 2 of them joined a group in their first visit. After the redesign, 4 of 5 did, in about 2 minutes. She compares ratios, not raw counts. A ratio still makes sense if the number of people changes.

Be honest about what the check can show. Five people find most big problems for one group per round, and then you fix and test again. The check finds problems. It does not prove the design is good. Write that limit in one short line.

This part is the course's own habit, not research. Suppose nobody can try your design yet. Then a proxy result, measured on the design, is a fair answer to "did it work?". Four kinds are easy. Count the steps from opening the app to the first useful action, before and after. Compute the contrast ratio of the main text, before and after. Run the squint test from 2.2 on both versions and write what came first. If you can, ask three to five people to do the main task. Write the label beside the number. An example is 7 steps to the first booking before and 3 after, measured on the design, not with users.

A proxy result does not say that people liked the design. It says that the design changed in a way you can check. For a first screen, that is enough to pass the skim, because the reader sees a result and an honest label. If you have nothing to measure, do not invent a number. Write "I have not tested this with users yet", and add the check you would run. The AI interviewer in 7.8 may ask where each number came from.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Sufian built Rihla, a bus timetable app for a university (fictional). His case study opens on the headline 'Hi, I am Sufian' and three paragraphs about why he loves coding. The result, '9 of 10 students found their bus in under a minute, in a check with 10 students', is in the last section. The Try it link is in the footer. A recruiter who is new to design will open the page on a phone. What should he change first?"
options:
  - "Add a longer list of the tools that he used to build it"
  - "Fill the intro with design words that sound expert"
  - "Add a second Try it link to the footer of the page"
  - "Move the result and a Try it button to the top"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "A tools list is a common first section, but it does not answer whether the app worked. It would also push the result further down."
  - "Specialist words make the page harder for a recruiter who is new to design. Plain words serve this reader better."
  - "A second link in the footer sits below the first screen, just like the first one. The reader still has to scroll to find it."
  - "Right. The result and the link are the two things a recruiter needs, and the first screen is what a phone shows. Everything else can wait below."
```
