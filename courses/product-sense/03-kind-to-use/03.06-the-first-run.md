## Three first screens for one new student

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-first-run" />

Switch through the three first screens and ask what Tariq can do on each one. The tour delays Halaqa's point by four steps. The empty screen gives him nothing to do. On the third, the first thing he sees is tonight's groups, with one Join button and a hint beside it. The valuable moment is one tap away.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the evidence says about tours">

NN/g's article on onboarding tutorials says they frequently do not perform well with real users in its studies. People skip them, they interrupt the task, and people forget them. A tutorial can help when the interaction is new, such as an unusual gesture. For most apps, NN/g recommends help that is triggered when the person needs it, with the details shown a step at a time and easy to dismiss [A62].

Be careful with the strength of this. The article reports no sample sizes, so it is expert guidance, a heuristic, and the research on first-run flows in general is thin [A62]. That is why this chapter says "tours are a weak default" and not "tours never work".

NN/g's guidance on empty states points the same way: prefer learning cues and a direct way to start over a forced tutorial [A22]. Halaqa's tour screen lists groups, chat, files, polls and voice rooms. It repeats the mistake of module 1, which was to show fourteen features and no reason to come back. The student has no group to see, and no seat to take.

The second screen shows what an empty first run looks like without 3.2 applied. "Nothing here" is true, but it says no reason, and offers no start. Compare the empty state in 3.2: it said why, what a group is and how to start one.

</GoDeeper>

The diagram shows both paths from the same start. Count the steps. On the tour path, Tariq passes four tour steps, an empty home and a search before he can join. On the value path he sees the groups and taps Join. Every step between a person and the valuable moment is a place where they can stop.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: counting the path, and what the third screen needs">

The count is simple arithmetic on the course's example. Tour path: open, four tour steps, the empty home, the search, then the join: 1 + 4 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 8 steps. Value path: open, tonight's groups, join: 3 steps. The numbers belong to this example. They show how steps add up, and are not a measurement of real apps.

The value path asks more of the designer. Halaqa must have something to show on the first screen: tonight's groups at the student's university. That is real content, not a placeholder. If there are no groups tonight, the empty state from 3.2 still applies: say why, say what a group is and offer a button to start one. NN/g also lists demo data as a way to start. If you use it, label it as an example, so that nobody tries to join a group that does not exist [A22].

Reviewers meet the same problem when they open your project. A reviewer's first run is the first screen of your link. If it is a tour, a login or a blank screen, they see no value in the first minute. Module 7 comes back to this with the login wall.

</GoDeeper>

## Help appears next to the thing it explains

<MermaidDiagram title="Help in place" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["The person reaches a control"] --> H["A short hint appears beside it"]
  H --> D["The person acts, or dismisses the hint"]
`} legend="Help is triggered by need, kept short and easy to dismiss." />

NN/g calls this a pull revelation: help that shows up when the person needs it, such as a short hint beside a button. It is short, easy to dismiss and tied to one control. On the third screen, "Tap Join to hold a seat" sits beside Join, so nothing is taught before Tariq needs it. The hint is plain text, so it needs no hover.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: writing help that does not block the person">

NN/g recommends pull revelations, which are help triggered when the person needs it, such as <GlossaryTerm term="tooltips" definition="Small boxes of help text that appear next to a control." /> or <GlossaryTerm term="coach marks" definition="Short pointers drawn over a screen that point at one control and explain it." />, with progressive disclosure and easy dismissal [A62]. Progressive disclosure means showing the next detail only when it matters. The first screen shows tonight's groups. The seats and the Join button come with each group. Details about chat or files wait until the student is in a group.

Three habits keep help short. Write one sentence that says what the control does and what happens next, as in "Tap Join to hold a seat. You can leave any time." Put it beside the control it explains, not on a separate screen. Let the person dismiss it, and do not show it again once they have used the control.

Make the hint plain visible text, as in the picture, so it works on a phone without a hover and for a keyboard user. This is course practice. The sources do not test it.

The same idea works for an empty state. A line that says "A group has a time, a place and seats" is a learning cue [A22]. It teaches the idea in the place where the student is looking.

</GoDeeper>

## Aha is a goal you pick

<MermaidDiagram title="From a first-run goal to a check" chart={`flowchart LR
  G["Goal: a student joins a group"] --> S["Signal: joins in the first visit"]
  S --> C["Check: 5 classmates, count joins and time"]
`} legend="Maryam's goal, signal and check from module 1, now pointed at the first run." />

Teams talk about an "aha moment": the point where a new person sees the value. It is a goal a team picks, not a finding. Maryam picked hers in 1.5: a student joins a group. Her signal is how many of five classmates join in their first visit, and how long it takes. Her first check found 2 of 5.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: aha numbers, goals and signals">

A famous story says a social network found that people who added 7 friends in 10 days tended to stay. An analytics writer wrote in 2015 that such numbers should not be read as scientific tipping points. They are often round numbers picked inside a range. No primary source for the original claim was found [A73]. So the lesson is not "find your number". It is "choose a goal you can check, and then check it".

In module 1 you used a scaled-down habit adapted from the Goals, Signals, Metrics process. It has one goal, one signal you can watch a person do, and a check with three to five people [A30]. For Halaqa's first run, the signals are: does the student join in the first visit, how long does it take, and where do the others stop? The last one is a failure signal. Failure is sometimes easier to see than success, such as giving up or going back [A30].

Use ratios, not raw counts. "4 of 5 joined" says more than "4 joined", because the count of five is part of the claim. Remember what the check can and cannot say. Five people find most of the big problems of one group in a round. Then you fix and test again. The check finds problems, but it does not prove the design is good [A20].

The check is a way to learn, not a grade. If the third screen gets 3 of 5, look at the two who did not join and ask where they stopped. The answer tells you what to fix next.

</GoDeeper>
