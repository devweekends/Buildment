Maryam drew Halaqa's study groups screen once, with groups on it. Bashar's blank screen showed her that the same screen has other faces. A screen that shows data has four states: <GlossaryTerm term="empty state" definition="The screen a person sees when there is nothing to show yet, for example a new account with no groups." />, loading, error and full. The four-state frame is the course's own. The rules for each state come from NN/g's guidance.

## Four states of one screen

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-four-states" />

Tap through the four states. Empty says why there is nothing and offers a way to start, loading shows that the app is working, and error names the cause and gives a step. Full is the state most apps draw, so draw the first three first, because people meet them whenever something is wrong.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each state must do">

Never leave a container fully blank. A blank can mean any of the other three states, and the person cannot tell which. Each state has a short job, and the table below gives it. The rules are heuristics (the word from 2.2): expert guidance from NN/g with examples, not controlled tests.

| State | What it must do | Kind of rule |
|---|---|---|
| Empty | Say why it is empty, what the feature is for, and give a direct way to start | Heuristic |
| Loading | Show that the app is working, and say how long if the wait is long | Heuristic |
| Error | Say what failed, near its cause, in plain words, with a next step | Heuristic |
| Full | Show the content | The state teams already design |

An empty state has a second job: it teaches. The first time a student opens a screen, the empty state is a good place to say what the screen is for. Halaqa's empty state says that a group has a time, a place and seats. That line is a learning cue. The button is the direct way to start. NN/g also lists demo data as a way to start, which lets a person see the screen with something on it.

Why draw the unhappy states first? Because every real person meets them. The full state is the one you see on your own fast connection with your own test data. The other three happen on a phone in a library, in the first minute of a new account, or when the server fails. A reviewer who opens your project link will meet at least one of them.

## The longer the wait, the more to say

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-wait-ladder" />

Drag along the wait and compare the two columns. The left column is something real apps ship: a spinner that only flashes at 0.4 seconds, a blank at 3 seconds, a bare spinner at 12. Bashar waited 12 seconds, so he needed a time, not a spinner alone. An upload gets a bar with a percentage.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the three limits, spinners and skeleton screens">

NN/g's guidance goes back to three limits of waiting. About 0.1 seconds feels instant. About 1 second is still short enough for the person to stay on the task. About 10 seconds is the most that keeps attention on the task. Beyond that, the person needs to know when it will finish. These limits come from older work cited in a 1993 book. They are guidance from perception research, not new measurements, so say "NN/g's guidance", not "proven".

For long waits, the advice is as follows. Over about 10 seconds, show a percentage done. If you do not know the total, show running progress, such as the item being processed. A spinner with no words is a last resort. For 2 to 10 seconds, a busy sign and a small changing count can be enough. The sources give no firm rule between 1 and 2 seconds.

A <GlossaryTerm term="skeleton screen" definition="A grey outline of the page, with boxes where text and pictures will appear, shown while the real content loads." /> is a common way to fill a short wait. NN/g suggests skeleton screens for full-page loads under about 10 seconds. It suggests spinners for one part of a page in about 2 to 10 seconds, and progress bars over 10 seconds and for uploads and downloads. It advises against skeletons that draw only the frame. It also warns that animated shimmer, a glint that moves across the grey boxes, can distract some people or cause accessibility problems.

Do skeleton screens make pages faster? No. NN/g says they create the illusion of a shorter wait. Its article presents no tests of its own, so this is an argued claim. Treat a skeleton as a way to set expectations, never as a speed fix.

Words do not shorten a wait. If Halaqa's list needs 12 seconds on weak data, the first fix is to send less. The page weight budget is the course's own habit: decide how many kilobytes a screen may weigh, and check it. For context only, the median mobile home page weighed about 2,311 KB in October 2024. That is the middle of the crawled web, not a target.

</GoDeeper>

## Check loading first, then error, then empty

<MermaidDiagram title="The order of checks on one screen" chart={`flowchart TD
  L{"Still loading?"} -->|"yes"| LS["Show loading"]
  L -->|"no"| F{"Did it fail?"}
  F -->|"yes"| ES["Show error"]
  F -->|"no"| N{"Any items?"}
  N -->|"no"| EM["Show empty"]
  N -->|"yes"| FU["Show the list"]
`} />

Read the diagram from the top, where the first yes ends the questions. If the screen asked about an empty list first, a list that is still loading would look empty, and the screen would say "No groups yet" while the groups are on the way. Empty is the right answer only at the bottom, after loading is over and nothing has failed.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the same order in code">

In a component, the order is a few lines. A list that starts as an empty array looks empty before the request has even begun, so the starting status must be loading, not idle.

```js
function GroupsScreen({ status, groups, error }) {
  if (status === "loading") return <LoadingState />;
  if (status === "error") return <ErrorState message={error} />;
  if (groups.length === 0) return <EmptyState />;
  return <GroupList groups={groups} />;
}
```

Reading the order of the checks is a quick review of any screen, including one that an AI tool wrote. Look for three more failures. The starting status is "idle", so the empty state flashes first. The error state clears the list the person was reading. A refresh replaces the whole list with a spinner. For a refresh, keep the old list and show a small sign that it is updating.

Empty can also mean different things: a new account with nothing yet, a search with no match, or a list the person has finished. The wording should fit the reason. This is course practice, not a sourced rule. "No groups for Data structures yet" fits a search. "You have no groups yet" fits a new account.

</GoDeeper>

## An error names the cause and a step

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-error-words" />

Switch between the two messages after the same failed send. The first has no cause and no step, and it empties the form. The second sits next to the button, names the cause, offers a step and keeps what the person typed. A good message has four parts: near the cause, plain words, a next step and the input kept.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: error messages worth reading">

NN/g's guidance for error messages has seven parts. Place the message near the cause. Do not rely on colour or animation alone. Use plain language and describe the specific problem. Offer next steps. Do not blame the person. Keep what the person typed. It adds one more: do not show an error too early, because leaving a field empty is exploratory, not a mistake. The next chapter uses that last point for forms.

Two standards sit behind this. WCAG 3.3.1 (level A) says that when an input error is found automatically, the item in error is identified and the error is described in text. Colour may help but cannot replace the text. The GOV.UK Design System (checked 2026-10-25) gives templates for the words, such as "Enter [what it is]" and "[What it is] must be [number] characters or less". Both ask for specific words, not "Invalid input".

A rewrite shows the difference. Before: "Something went wrong." After: "Your group was not saved. The connection dropped. Everything you typed is kept." The second version has 13 words in three short sentences. It names the cause, keeps the input and blames nobody. A Try again button supplies the step.

Errors cost more on weak data, because a failed request often has to be repeated. Keeping the person's input matters most there. A student who typed a long note and lost it to a dropped connection will not type it a second time.

</GoDeeper>
