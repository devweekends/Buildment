Maryam watches Samir, a classmate, try to start a study group in Halaqa. He fills two fields, stops at the third and puts his phone down. The form has four fields in this order: Course, When, Where and Seats. Her first version has four problems, and each one takes away something a person needs to finish. One of them is a <GlossaryTerm term="placeholder" definition="Grey example text inside an empty field. It disappears as soon as the person types." />, which some forms use instead of a label.

## Four problems on one form

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-form-spot" />

Tap the four parts that make people give up: "Invalid input" names no field, placeholders used as labels vanish, a red border alone has no words, and a small "Submit" does not say what happens. Then switch to the fixed form. It has labels above, hints, the error in words next to its field, and a button that names the result.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where each fix comes from">

Each fix has a source, and the sources are of different kinds. Keep them apart when you explain a choice. The GOV.UK Design System and NN/g pages named here are current at the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), so check them again before you quote them.

| Fix | Kind of rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Keep labels visible, outside the field | Heuristic and practice | NN/g, GOV.UK |
| A label closer to its own field than to others | Heuristic | NN/g |
| Single column, labels above on mobile, fewer fields | Heuristic | NN/g |
| Describe the error in text, not colour alone | Standard, level A | WCAG 3.3.1 |
| Provide labels or instructions | Standard, level A | WCAG 3.3.2 |
| Check when the person leaves the field | Practice from usability testing | A research firm |

WCAG does not ban placeholders. Its rule 3.3.2 says that labels or instructions must be provided and that labels should reach all users, but the page never mentions placeholders. The case against them comes from NN/g, from GOV.UK and from contrast rules. So say "placeholders as labels cause problems", never "WCAG forbids placeholders".

A <GlossaryTerm term="hint" definition="A short line of extra help under a label, such as the format to use or an example." /> is not a label. The label says what the field is. The hint helps the person fill it in. Under When the hint reads "For example, tomorrow 5 pm". Under Where it reads "Library, online or cafe". GOV.UK asks for labels above the input, in most cases visible, and says not to use placeholder text in place of a label, or for hints or examples.

Cutting fields matters as much as styling them. Every field is a chance to stop. Halaqa's create-group form asks for four things: a course, a time, a place and seats. A long description would be a fifth chance to stop, so Maryam leaves it out. NN/g's guidance is one column and fewer fields. It cites a study with success figures, but this course did not check that study, so it repeats no numbers.

</GoDeeper>

## A placeholder is gone when you type

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-label-vanish" />

Switch from the empty form to the typed one. "Course", "When", "Where" and "Seats" disappear as Samir types, and the last field now holds only a 3. Is that seats, a table, hours? He would have to delete his answer to find out, but in the third form the label stays above the field.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why placeholders cause trouble">

NN/g gives several reasons. A placeholder vanishes when typing starts, so the person cannot check the field later. It makes checking and fixing work hard. It can be mistaken for text that is already filled in. And placeholder grey often has poor contrast. NN/g cites user testing and eye tracking, but gives no methods or sample sizes, so label this a heuristic.

GOV.UK adds a technical reason. Not all screen readers read placeholder text. Default placeholder styles often fail the contrast rule 1.4.3 at level AA, which asks for 4.5 to 1 for normal text (see 2.5). The practical result is the same: the person loses the field name, either by typing or by not seeing it.

Floating labels start inside the field and move above it when the person types. NN/g says they are better than a placeholder alone, but labels outside the field are best when there is space. On a phone there is room for a label above a field. One line of label text costs little height, and it leaves the field as wide as the screen.

</GoDeeper>

## Check when the person leaves the field

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-when-to-check" />

Switch through the three moments. In the first, an error appears while Samir is still typing "DS2", which is too early. In the second, he has left the field, and one error says what is missing. In the third, he fixes the code and the error clears as he types, but a check on leaving the field does not replace the check when the form is sent.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the timing of checks">

<GlossaryTerm term="validation" definition="Checking that what a person typed fits the rules, for example a group code of two letters and three digits." /> can run at four moments: on every key, when the person leaves the field, when the fixed length is reached, and when the form is sent. The research firm's advice is to check when the person leaves the field by default. For inputs of fixed length, such as a postal code, you can check as soon as the right length is typed. Once an error shows, update it on each key, so that it clears as soon as the input is valid.

The evidence is practice from usability testing and benchmark observations. The article gives no participant count and no test that compares different timings. It also says that checking while a person fills in a form does not replace handling errors when the form is sent. So keep the server check, and keep the submit-time message.

NN/g says the same from the other side: do not show an error too early, because leaving a field empty is exploratory. A person may tap into a field only to read it. A required-field error at that moment is noise.

Two mistakes are easy to make in code. One is checking on every key from the first letter, which produces the red text in the first version of the picture. The other is leaving the error up after the fix, which keeps the person looking at a problem they already solved. Both are about timing, not about the rule itself.

</GoDeeper>

## Error words follow a pattern

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-error-templates" />

The GOV.UK Design System writes its error words from a few templates. Switch between them. Each message names the field and says what to do, and sits right under its field, so the person does not have to look elsewhere. Compare these with "Invalid input", which names nothing and fixes nothing.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: writing the words, and where to put them">

An error in words has three jobs: say which field, say what is wrong and say what to do. The templates in the picture do all three in one short line: "Enter [what it is]", "[What it is] must be [rule]" and "[What it is] must be [number] characters or less". NN/g adds that the message belongs near the cause, in plain language, without blame, and must not rely on colour alone. WCAG 3.3.1 (level A) asks the same for the text: when an error is found automatically, the item in error is identified and described in text.

A red border can stay as an extra cue, as long as the words are there. It also needs enough contrast against its background. Rule 1.4.11 at level AA asks 3 to 1 for the parts of a control that a person needs to identify it (see 2.5).

Compare the first and the fixed form of the first picture. In the first, "Invalid input" sits at the top. A person must look at the red border, work out which field it belongs to, and guess the rule. In the fixed form, the message sits under Seats and says "Seats must be 1 or more. Others need room to join." The second sentence gives the reason for the rule.

Last, keep the person's input. If sending fails, the form should still hold everything they typed. On weak data a send can fail, and an emptied form means typing it all again. Chapter 3.2 listed this as the fourth part of a good error.

</GoDeeper>

explanations:
  - "Right. A label above the field is still there after the person types. Extra help, such as a format, goes in a hint under it. NN/g and GOV.UK both advise this."

  - "A tooltip hides the help behind a tap, and the field is still unlabelled once the person types."
