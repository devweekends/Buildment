This is the gate for module 3. The work itself was in the practice chapter, 3.7, where you wrote the unhappy paths of one flow. This gate asks something different: how do you think about a screen now? Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

## Module 3 checklist

- [ ] I can say what a screen shows when it is empty, loading and broken, in exact words, and why a screen checks loading before it checks empty
- [ ] I put a label above every field, write errors in words next to their cause, and check a field after the person leaves it
- [ ] I size targets for fingers at about 44 to 48, read the Tab order, keep focus visible, give meaningful images words, and mirror a card for Arabic
- [ ] I use motion only to show a change, and I say that change in words when the reader asks for reduced motion
- [ ] I put the valuable moment on the first screen and teach inside the screen, not in a tour
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 3.2 to 3.6, finished the practice in 3.7 and the quiz in 3.8

## Learning log

1. **The state you forget:** Think of an app you use or one you built. Which of the four states does it forget most, and what do you see on your screen when it happens?
2. **The words you will change:** Which words in an error, a label or a button in your own project would you rewrite first? What would they say now?
3. **The rule that changed your mind:** Which rule in this module was different from what you believed before: placeholders, checking on every key, a welcome tour, reduced motion, or another? What changed your mind?
4. **The path you will never skip:** In your next project, which unhappy path will you design first, and at what point in the work will you do it?

## What changed

In module 2 you learned what a person sees first on a happy screen: one thing first, readable type, space that groups, colour with a job. Module 3 asks what that person sees when the screen is not happy. The data has not arrived. The list is empty. The request failed. The form was filled in wrongly. The button is too small for the finger. Or the reader has asked for less movement.

A screen built quickly, with or without an AI tool, can show only the happy case, because that is the one the builder sees on a fast connection. The change this gate looks for is in your first question. Before, the first question about a screen was "does it work?". Now it is "what does a person read here when it does not?".

That question is cheap to ask, and it finds problems that a polished happy screen hides. It also gives you words to write, such as "Groups did not load. The connection dropped before the list arrived.", and exact words are something a mentor can check.

A habit helps here: before you call a screen finished, cause its unhappy cases yourself. Switch to flight mode, go where the signal is weak, clear the data, type a bad value, read the Tab order, zoom the text, turn on reduced motion. Each takes a minute, and together they show most of what this module taught. Notice also what the module did not ask you to do. It did not ask for a new look. Most of the fixes cost words, an order of checks, some padding or a few lines of CSS.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your answer for the habit, not for the exact list. They want to see that you look at a screen from the side of the person who meets the unhappy case. That person may be on weak data, on a small phone, using a keyboard, or using reduced motion.

They read your fixes for exact words and numbers. "Add a loader" is advice. "Finding rooms..." with a line about the wait is a fix. They also read your first choice. There is no single right order, but there must be a reason that names the person and what they lose.

The common weak answer lists the problems it can name and fixes each with a general sentence, such as "show better errors". It sounds right and could be written without looking at the page. A strong answer writes the words and the numbers, and ties each moment to a rule from the module.

After you submit, the mentor usually asks one follow-up question. For example: "You put the cancel icon first. What would the student see at second 3 if you had put the loading state first? Who would that hurt more?" A good reply points to the page and gives a reason you can show.

## The study-room page

A fellow built a page where students book a study room in the university library. It is a fictional app. Here are six facts about it. First, on library wifi the page shows a title and white space for 8 seconds, and then the rooms appear. Second, when no room is free, the list says "No results".

Third, the booking form has four fields. Each shows only a placeholder: "Room", "Date", "Start" and "Hours". When something is wrong, the top of the form says "Invalid input". Fourth, the "Book" button pulses all the time. Fifth, the first time a student opens the page, a four-step tour explains rooms, hours, rules and fees before any room is shown. Sixth, the icon that cancels a booking is 16 by 16 pixels and sits next to the Edit icon with no space between them.

Your task is a timeline of one student's evening, in five moments. You can use a screen of your own project instead and choose five moments of your own. The rubric below is for the study-room page.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moments covered | At least four of the five moments, each with the words on screen now and the words you would put there | Three moments, or four without the new words | Two or fewer moments |
| Words and rules | Every fix is exact words or numbers a developer could use, and each names a rule from the module | Most fixes are exact, but some are only advice or have no rule | The fixes are advice only, such as "add a loader" |
| First fix and reason | One moment chosen first, with a reason that names the student and what they lose | A moment chosen first, but no reason | No order or no reason |

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet, and at most one part is Partly.
