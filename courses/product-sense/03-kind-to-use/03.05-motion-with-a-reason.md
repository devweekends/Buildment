Maryam's AI tool added motion to Halaqa without being asked: a bouncing badge, a pulsing button, a spinning star. She shows the page to Dana, a classmate who says that moving things on a screen make her dizzy. Dana has turned on reduced motion in her phone's settings. Maryam asks one question about each movement: what is it for? Anything that has no answer is a candidate to cut, and the rest of this chapter shows how to decide.

## Loops everywhere, or one that helps

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-motion-with-a-job" />

Switch between three pages and watch which parts move. On the first, four things loop and none has a job, so they compete with the time and the seats. On the second, one spinner turns while the join is in progress and nothing else moves. On the third, Dana's setting is on: nothing moves, and words say the same thing.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why decoration costs the reader">

The loops in the first page are not harmless. The standards name the risk. WCAG 2.3.3 says that decorative movement, for example during scrolling, can cause dizziness and headaches for some people. The web platform documentation adds that scaling and panning of large objects are common triggers for <GlossaryTerm term="vestibular" definition="Related to the inner ear and the sense of balance. Some people feel dizzy or unwell when parts of a screen move." /> problems. Dana is one example of a reader who feels this.

Decoration also has a plain cost for everyone. Each loop competes with the facts for attention. A student who came to read "Tonight 8 pm, library room 2" and "2 seats left" has to read past a bouncing badge. This point is the course's own observation, not a measured result. NN/g makes a related point about one kind of motion: animated shimmer on a skeleton screen can distract some people or cause accessibility problems.

The loops in the picture repeat so you can see them. A real motion that shows a change plays once and stops. A looping spinner is the exception, because it has a job for as long as the wait lasts, and it stops when the wait ends.

Check generated pages too. For each moving part, ask the question in the next beat.

</GoDeeper>

## Four jobs earn motion; skip the rest

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-motion-sorter" />

Sort the eight movements. Motion has a job when it shows a change: work in progress, something new, something moved, or a value changed. Looks do not decide it, because the same fade is decoration on a scrolling card and a job after a join. If the honest answer is "it looks nice", cut it first.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how long, and how to write it">

Keep transitions short. At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), Material Design 3 lists default durations from 200 to 500 milliseconds. Times are longer when something enters than when it leaves, and shorter for changes over a small area. Easing is how a movement speeds up and slows down. Material also says that this easing and duration system is no longer maintained and has been succeeded by a spring-based system. Use the range as a starting point, not a rule. The test is simple: the person should not have to wait for the motion to finish before they can act.

The safest way to write motion is to put it inside the no-preference case. Then a person who asks for less never gets it, and an old browser that ignores the query also gets none.

```css
.row-new {
  opacity: 1;
}

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference) {
  .row-new {
    animation: slide-in 250ms ease-out;
  }
}
```

That is practice, not a standard. Both values of the setting exist: `no-preference` and `reduce`.

In Halaqa, the four jobs map to four moments. A spinner shows that a join is in progress. A new group row slides in when someone starts a group. A joined group moves into "My groups". The seats count ticks from 2 to 1 after a join. Nothing else needs to move. The star, the pulsing button and the bouncing badge have no job.

</GoDeeper>

## Reduced motion still says what changed

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m3-reduced-motion" />

Dana's phone has a setting for less motion, and the browser passes it to your page. Take the pulse away from the first page and nothing is left. The second page says "You joined" and "1 seat left", so it needs no pulse. Reduced motion means less movement, not less information, so the page drops the movement and keeps the news.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading the setting, and what counts as essential">

The web platform documentation describes `prefers-reduced-motion` as a way to detect whether the person has asked the system to minimise non-essential motion. The values are `no-preference` and `reduce`. It works across browsers and has been widely available since January 2020 (checked 2026-10-25). In CSS you read it with a <GlossaryTerm term="media query" definition="A CSS rule that applies only when a condition is true, such as a setting the person has chosen." />. In JavaScript you read it like this:

```js
const reduce = window.matchMedia("(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)").matches;
```

Do not read "reduced" as "none". The documentation's own example replaces a scaling pulse with a gentler fade, so it lowers the risk of vestibular problems and keeps the cue. That is what the second page in the picture does with words.

What is essential? Motion that is the information itself. A spinner shows that work is in progress. Under reduced motion you can keep a still indicator and add words, such as "Joining the group..." and a line that says what will appear when it is done. The goal is that nobody has to guess whether the app is working. That echoes 3.2: a screen that says nothing is the problem, whether it is blank or still.

Also remember that the setting is a request from the person. Respect it everywhere: page transitions, scroll effects, auto-playing carousels and loading shimmer.

</GoDeeper>

## Which rule applies to which motion

<MermaidDiagram title="Two WCAG rules for moving content" chart={`flowchart TD
  M{"Does it move by itself?"} -->|"yes"| A{"More than 5 seconds, beside other content?"}
  A -->|"yes"| R1["2.2.2, level A: a way to pause, stop or hide"]
  A -->|"no"| R2["2.2.2 asks for no control"]
  M -->|"no, a tap or scroll starts it"| R3["2.3.3, level AAA: let the person turn it off"]
`} />

A spinner that lasts a few seconds needs no control, but a banner of offers that scrolls beside the groups does. The first rule is level A. The second is level AAA, a step beyond the course's AA bar, so treat it as extra, and honouring the reduced-motion setting is one way to meet it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the two rules in detail">

WCAG 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (level A) covers moving, blinking or scrolling content. It applies when the content starts automatically, lasts more than five seconds and sits beside other content. Then the person needs a way to pause, stop or hide it. Content that updates by itself has the same rule without the five-second exemption. A scrolling banner of offers beside a menu is the usual example. A spinner that lasts a few seconds is not.

WCAG 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (level AAA): motion animation triggered by an interaction can be disabled, unless it is essential. Do not say 2.3.3 is required. It is a level above this course's bar. It is still good practice, and honouring the reduced-motion setting is one way to meet it.

Put the two together for Halaqa. If a banner scrolls by itself beside the groups, it needs a pause control. If a card scales when tapped, reduced motion should turn that scaling off, which is one way to meet 2.3.3. Better still, do not add the banner, because the facts the student needs are already on the page.

Evidence labels for this chapter: 2.2.2 and 2.3.3 are standards, at levels A and AAA. The `prefers-reduced-motion` feature is a web platform specification. The duration range is design-system guidance. The four jobs and the idea that decoration competes for attention are the course's own.

</GoDeeper>
