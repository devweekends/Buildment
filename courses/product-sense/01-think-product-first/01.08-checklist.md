This is the gate for module 1. The work itself was in the brief in chapter 1.6. This gate asks something different: how do you think about a request now? Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

## Module 1 checklist

- [ ] I can turn a feature request into a problem by asking about the last time it happened
- [ ] I can write a job as "When I..., I want to..., so that..." without naming a feature
- [ ] I set my time in hours first, then sort features into must have, nice to have and not now
- [ ] I can say no with four parts: the item, who it affects, what happens without it, and what I do instead
- [ ] I choose one goal and one signal I can watch a person do, written as x of y
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 1.2 to 1.5, finished the practice in 1.6 and the quiz in 1.7

## Learning log

1. **Your last request:** Think of the last time someone asked you for a feature, or you asked yourself for one. Was it a problem, a feature or a guess? What would you ask now?
2. **Your job:** Take the app out of your job sentence. Does it still make sense? Would a friend in the same situation agree that it is their job too?
3. **Your cut:** What is the one thing in your project that you most want to keep and have the weakest reason for? What would the four-part no say about it?
4. **Your signal:** Which number do you look at today that only counts? What could you watch a person do instead?

## What changed in how you choose

Before this module, most builders start from the tool. They open an AI coding tool, describe an app, and watch features appear. Each feature works, so each one feels like progress. Maryam did exactly this, and she ended with fourteen features and three users. The tool was not the problem. The problem was that she never wrote down who the app was for.

After this module, the first move is different. You start from the last time something went wrong for a real person. You write the job with a situation, an action and a result. You set the time before the design, and you cut the scope to fit it. You say no with a reason that the other person can check. You choose a signal that you can watch, and a small group of people to watch it with.

None of these steps needs a laptop. All of them can be done with a pen and three classmates. That is why they come first in the course. The chapters that follow teach you to make screens clear and kind to use, but a clear screen for the wrong job is still the wrong thing. If the answers in this gate feel obvious to you now, that is the sign that the habit has started.

## Your gate task: one new request

Take one request that you could receive, from your own project or from Wasel. For Wasel, use this one. Sami says to Karam: "Customers keep asking me for online payment. Let us add it." Karam has 6 hours this week, and the job is the one from chapter 1.6.

Write what you would do, in order. First, the problem: who you would ask about the last time, what you would ask, and what problems you expect to hear. Second, one smaller fix that might answer the problem before you build the request. Third, where the request goes in a 6-hour week, and a four-part no if it goes to not now. Fourth, the signal that would tell you the change worked, written as x of y.

Here is the shape of a strong answer, told about a fictional fellow. Ghadeer studies at a coding bootcamp and built a class app. A classmate asks her to add a leaderboard. She does not start with the leaderboard. She asks two learners about the last time they felt stuck in the app. Both say the same thing: they open it and do not know which lesson to do next. She writes the problem in one sentence: learners cannot tell which lesson comes next.

Her smaller fix is to highlight the next lesson on the home screen, which is far less work than a leaderboard. She has 5 hours. The highlight takes 2 and she keeps 1 hour for a check. The leaderboard goes to not now, with four parts. The item is the leaderboard. It affects learners who like to compete. Without it, the next lesson still gets found. Instead, she builds the highlight and looks again after the check. Her signal is how many of five learners start the next lesson within 30 seconds of opening the app. She writes "x of 5", and one failure signal: opening the lesson list and scrolling.

Look at the shape of her answer. The problem is a stuck moment, not the feature. The smaller fix is real and costs less than the request. The no has all four parts. The signal can be watched, and it ties to the goal. Your answer does not need her topic. It needs the same four moves.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads the problem first. They ask whether it happened to a person, at a time, and whether it can be checked. If your problem is "students need online payment", it restates the feature and tells the mentor nothing. If it is "a student arrived at the counter without cash and could not collect the order", it is a moment, and the mentor can ask what to do about it.

The common weak answer builds the request and then tries to justify it. It says yes to online payment because a customer asked, and the signal is "number of payments". Both choices skip the last time, and the signal is a count that grows by itself. A second weak answer says no, but only because the job seems hard. It never says what happens without the feature or who it affects.

The follow-up question mentors often ask is: "Who would you ask, and what would make you change your mind?" A good reply names real people and a result that would move the decision. For example: "If three of five students say they were stuck at the counter, I would look at payment again." You can answer by voice with your phone's microphone.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The problem | A moment that happened to a person, with who you would ask about the last time | A problem stated, but it restates the request or has no one to ask | Only the feature, or a guess about what people want |
| The smaller fix | One fix that costs less than the request and answers the problem | A fix is named, but it is not smaller or does not answer the problem | No alternative to building the request |
| The cut | A place in the time budget and a four-part no or a reason tied to the job | A place and a reason, but one of the four parts is missing | No place, or the reason is only that it is hard |
| The signal | An x of y signal you can watch, with a time or a failure signal | A signal you can watch, but no ratio | Only a count, or only what people say |

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet, and at most one part is Partly.
