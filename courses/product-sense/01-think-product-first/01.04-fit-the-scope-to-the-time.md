Maryam knows the job. Halaqa has fourteen features, and she has 12 hours over two weeks, because she also studies on her own and takes freelance work. She must cut the <GlossaryTerm term="scope" definition="Everything the work includes: its features, screens and details." /> to fit the time.

## Decide the time first, then shape the idea

<MermaidDiagram title="Two ways to plan" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Time: 12 hours over two weeks"] --> B["Shape the idea to fit"]
  B --> C["Finish what fits"]
  D["Idea: all 14 features"] --> E["Estimate the hours"]
  E --> F["The deadline moves"]
`} legend="Top: the time is fixed and the scope changes. Bottom: the idea is fixed and the date changes." />

An <GlossaryTerm term="appetite" definition="The time an idea deserves, set before the design starts." /> is a fixed time budget. Maryam's is 12 hours over two weeks. The usual way starts with the idea and asks how long it takes, and then the date moves. An appetite works the other way: the time is fixed, and the scope changes to fit it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: appetite, estimate and the hours you really have">

Shape Up, a free book from Basecamp, separates two things. An estimate starts from a design and gives a number. An appetite starts from a number and shapes the design to fit it. The book's phrase for this is "fixed time, variable scope" [A27]. It is a method from a software company, so it is practice and not a study.

The book also gives sizes for teams: a small batch is one to two weeks for a small team, and a big batch is a full six-week cycle [A27]. For this course, your own time budget is your appetite. That mapping is the course's own, not the book's.

Count the hours you will really have. Maryam's 12 hours over two weeks is 6 hours a week, or about 2 hours on each of three evenings. Study, work, a weak connection and a power cut all take time, so write the number you can keep and not the number you hope for.

When the work runs late, move the scope and not the time. If Maryam has used 9 hours and the home screen is half done, she cuts something. She does not take a third week. Without a fixed time, the scope grows to fill whatever time exists, and the project never ends.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask the same questions of every feature

<MermaidDiagram title="The questions for each feature" chart={`flowchart TD
  F["A feature on the list"] --> Q1["Does the job fail without it?"]
  Q1 --> Q2["How many people does it affect?"]
  Q2 --> Q3["What do they do today?"]
  Q3 --> R["Must, nice or not now"]
`} legend="Each feature goes through the same questions. The answers decide where it goes." />

Shape Up calls this <GlossaryTerm term="scope hammering" definition="Asking hard questions about each item until only what the job needs is left." />. For each feature, ask what happens without it, how many people it affects, and what the person does today. Must haves stay. Nice to haves get a tilde (~) and usually are not built. Everything else waits.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the questions in detail">

The questions come from Shape Up's chapter on scope hammering [A76]. In this course they work like this.

**Does the job fail without it?** For Halaqa, the job is to find two or three classmates to revise with tonight or tomorrow. Group search and create group pass this test. A student with neither cannot do the job.

**What happens without it, and to how many people?** Take notifications. Without them, a student who forgets can still open the app. The job works, so notifications are not a must have. They help many students a little, so they are a nice to have.

**What does the person do today?** Shape Up calls this comparing to what the person has now. Students already message each other in apps they use. So chat in Halaqa does not fix what is failing today. What fails is finding a group.

A nice to have is marked with a tilde in the brief, for example "~ Notifications". Marked items are usually not built. If hours remain after the must haves and the check, build the cheapest one first.

A small budget example. Maryam estimates 8 hours for the must haves and the reshaped screens, and 2 hours for the check in chapter 1.5. Eight plus 2 is 10, which leaves 2 of her 12 hours. That is enough for one small nice to have, such as calendar sync. It is not enough for voice rooms. This is her own estimate for the case.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort nine of the fourteen features

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-scope-hammer" />

Sort nine of Maryam's fourteen features for her job and her 12 hours. Voice rooms and chat are not bad. Students already have a way to talk, and the job fails at finding a group, not at talking. The other five features are in the fold.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the full sort of all fourteen">

This table sorts every feature for the same job and the same 12 hours. Another job, or another time budget, would give another table.

| Feature | Bucket | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Group search | Must have | The job is to find a group |
| Create group | Must have | If no group fits, the student is stuck |
| Notifications | Nice to have (~) | The job works without them |
| Calendar sync | Nice to have (~) | A student can still join and go |
| Chat | Not now | Students already message in other apps |
| Voice rooms | Not now | An online group can meet on a call link |
| Streaks | Not now | They serve the weekly habit job |
| Badges | Not now | They serve the weekly habit job |
| Leaderboard | Not now | It does not help find a group |
| Profile themes | Not now | They do not help the job |
| Dark mode | Not now | It does not help this job yet |
| Polls | Not now | A group can agree on a time in the app it already uses |
| File sharing | Not now | Files come after a group exists |
| AI summary of notes | Not now | It serves studying alone, a different job |

Count them: 2 must have, 2 nice to have and 10 not now. That is 14. Not now does not mean never. Dark mode, for example, may matter to a student who revises at night in a dark room. It waits because it does not serve tonight's job, and a later check may show that it matters.

</GoDeeper>

## Say no with a reason

<MermaidDiagram title="A no with four parts" chart={`flowchart TD
  I["The item: leaderboard"] --> W["Who it affects: no one finding a group"]
  W --> N["Without it: nothing breaks"]
  N --> S["Instead: it stays on the later list"]
`} legend="A no names the item, who it affects, what happens without it and what happens instead." />

A no needs a reason, or it feels like a refusal. The course's template has four parts: the item, who it affects, what happens without it, and what you do instead. Maryam tells Rawan: no leaderboard now. It does not help anyone find a group tonight. Nothing breaks without it. It stays on the later list.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: saying no to a person, and to a tool">

Scope hammering and the "Do less" principle in GOV.UK's design principles give tools for saying no [A76, A75]. No source in this course's research says there is one right way to say it, so the four-part template is the course's own, built on the scope hammering questions.

Here is Maryam's reply to Adel, who asks for streaks again. The item: streaks. Who it affects: students who study every week, such as Adel and Rawan. Without it: the exam-night job still works. Instead: streaks stay on the later list, and she will look at them after the check. The reply is short and it names a next step.

Keep the tone kind. Say not now, not never. Do not blame the person who asked; their request holds a real wish. And do not say yes to be polite. A "later" with no condition turns into a promise.

You may also need to say no to a tool. An AI coding tool can add features you did not ask for. Read what the tool changed, and ask the same questions of each addition. If the answer is no, remove it and note why.

</GoDeeper>

## Cut features wait on a list

<MermaidDiagram title="What happens to the cut list" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Not now list"] --> B["Check with 3 to 5 people"]
  B --> C["Add back only what the check asks for"]
`} legend="Cut features wait on a list. A check with real people decides whether any come back." />

Shape Up argues that choosing what to build does not leave holes in the product, and can make it different. That is the book's argument, not a study. Maryam keeps her not now list and looks at it again after her check in chapter 1.5. She adds back only what students ask for.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: cutting scope and keeping quality">

A common fear is that a smaller scope means a worse product. Shape Up argues the opposite: when you choose what to execute, the product has no holes, and the choice can set it apart [A76]. This is practice from one company's experience. It is a reason to try the method, not a guarantee.

Cutting scope does not allow poor work. The features that stay must work well. The reason to cut is to give the ones that stay your full attention: a clear screen, a form that handles errors, a first run that reaches the result. Modules 2 and 3 are about that quality.

The risk of cutting is that you cut something people need. The check in chapter 1.5 is how you find out, and it is cheap. A feature on the not now list can come back in an evening. A feature built too early can be hard to remove, because people have started to use it.

</GoDeeper>
