Maryam knows the job. Halaqa has fourteen features, and she has 12 hours over two weeks, because she also studies on her own and takes freelance work. She has built them all, so she must now choose. Fourteen features in twelve hours is not a plan. She must cut the <GlossaryTerm term="scope" definition="Everything the work includes: its features, screens and details." /> to fit the time.

## Time first, then the idea

<MermaidDiagram title="Two ways to plan the same idea" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Appetite: 12 hours over two weeks"] --> B["Shape the idea to fit"]
  B --> C["Finish what fits"]
  D["Estimate: all 14 features"] --> E["Count the hours"]
  E --> F["The deadline moves"]
`} legend="Top: appetite. Bottom: estimate." />

An <GlossaryTerm term="appetite" definition="The time an idea deserves, set before the design starts." /> is a fixed time budget. Maryam's is 12 hours over two weeks. The usual way starts with the idea and asks how long it takes, and then the date moves. An appetite works the other way: the time is fixed, and the scope changes to fit it. The check in chapter 1.5 also needs hours.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: appetite, estimate and the hours you really have">

Shape Up, a free book from Basecamp, separates two things. An estimate starts from a design and gives a number. An appetite starts from a number and shapes the design to fit it. The book's phrase for this is "fixed time, variable scope" [A27]. It is a method from a software company, so it is practice and not a study.

The book also gives sizes for teams: a small batch is one to two weeks for a small team, and a big batch is a full six-week cycle [A27]. For this course, your own time budget is your appetite. That mapping is the course's own, not the book's.

Count the hours you will really have. Maryam's 12 hours over two weeks is 6 hours a week, or about 2 hours on each of three evenings. Study, work, a weak connection and a power cut all take time, so write the number you can keep and not the number you hope for.

When the work runs late, move the scope and not the time. If Maryam has used 9 hours and the home screen is half done, she cuts something. She does not take a third week. Without a fixed time, the scope grows to fill whatever time exists, and the project never ends.

</GoDeeper>

## Three questions put a feature in a bucket

<MermaidDiagram title="Where each feature goes" chart={`flowchart TD
  F["A feature on the list"] --> Q1{"Does the job fail without it?"}
  Q1 -->|"Yes"| M["Must have"]
  Q1 -->|"No"| Q2{"Does it ease this job, and is it small?"}
  Q2 -->|"Yes"| N["Nice to have, marked ~"]
  Q2 -->|"No"| L["Not now"]
`} legend="Diamond: a question." />

Shape Up calls this <GlossaryTerm term="scope hammering" definition="Asking hard questions about each item until only what the job needs is left." />. For each feature, ask what happens without it, how many people it affects, and what the person does today. Must haves stay. Nice to haves get a ~ (tilde) and usually are not built. Everything else waits for later. Read the diamonds from the top.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the questions in detail">

The questions come from Shape Up's chapter on scope hammering [A76]. In this course they work like this.

**Does the job fail without it?** For Halaqa, the job is to find two or three classmates to revise with tonight or tomorrow. Group search and create group pass this test. A student with neither cannot do the job.

**What happens without it, and to how many people?** Take notifications. Without them, a student who forgets can still open the app. The job works, so notifications are not a must have. They ease the job for many students a little, so they are a nice to have.

**What does the person do today?** Shape Up calls this comparing to what the person has now. Students already message each other in apps they use. So chat in Halaqa does not fix what is failing today. What fails is finding a group.

A nice to have is marked with a tilde in the brief, for example "~ Notifications". Marked items are usually not built. If hours remain after the must haves and the check, build the cheapest one first.

A small budget example. Maryam estimates 8 hours for the must haves and the reshaped screens, and 2 hours for the check in chapter 1.5. Eight plus 2 is 10, which leaves 2 of her 12 hours. That is enough for one small nice to have, such as calendar sync. It is not enough for voice rooms. This is her own estimate for the case.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort nine of the fourteen features

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-scope-hammer" />

Sort nine of Maryam's fourteen features for her job and her 12 hours. Voice rooms and chat are not bad: the job fails at finding a group, not at talking, and students already have ways to talk. Profile themes are small, but small is not enough to be a nice to have. The other five features are in the fold. Try to say each reason in your own words.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the full sort of all fourteen, and the later list">

This table sorts every feature for the same job and the same 12 hours. Another job, or another time budget, would give another table.

| Feature | Bucket | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Group search | Must have | The job is to find a group |
| Create group | Must have | If no group fits, the student is stuck |
| Notifications | Nice to have (~) | The job works without them, and they ease it |
| Calendar sync | Nice to have (~) | A student can still join and go, and it eases the job |
| Chat | Not now | Students already message in other apps |
| Voice rooms | Not now | An online group can meet on a call link |
| Streaks | Not now | They serve the weekly habit job |
| Badges | Not now | They serve the weekly habit job |
| Leaderboard | Not now | It does not help find a group |
| Profile themes | Not now | They do not ease the job, whatever their size |
| Dark mode | Not now | It does not ease this job yet |
| Polls | Not now | A group can agree on a time in the app it already uses |
| File sharing | Not now | Files come after a group exists |
| AI summary of notes | Not now | It serves studying alone, a different job |

Count them: 2 must have, 2 nice to have and 10 not now. That is 14. Not now does not mean never. Dark mode, for example, may matter to a student who revises at night in a dark room. It waits because it does not ease tonight's job, and a later check may show that it matters.

Cut features wait on a list. Maryam keeps her not now list and looks at it again after her check in chapter 1.5. She adds back only what students ask for. Shape Up argues that choosing what to build does not leave holes in the product, and can make it different [A76]. That is the book's argument, not a study.

</GoDeeper>

## Say no with a reason

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-say-no" />

A no needs a reason, or it feels like a refusal. The course's template has four parts: the item, who it affects, what happens without it, and what you do instead. Read the three replies to Rawan. Only the third gives her something she can check or answer, and it stays kind. A clear no also tells Rawan what to expect next.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: saying no, and keeping quality">

Scope hammering and the "Do less" principle in GOV.UK's design principles give tools for saying no [A76, A75]. No source in this course's research says there is one right way to say it, so the four-part template is the course's own, built on the scope hammering questions.

Here is Maryam's reply to Adel, who asks for streaks again. The item: streaks. Who it affects: students who study every week, such as Adel and Rawan. Without it: the exam-night job still works. Instead: streaks stay on the later list, and she will look at them after the check. The reply is short and it names a next step.

Keep the tone kind. Say not now, not never. Do not blame the person who asked; their request holds a real wish. And do not say yes to be polite. A "later" with no condition turns into a promise.

You may also need to say no to a tool. An AI coding tool can add features you did not ask for. Read what the tool changed, and ask the same questions of each addition. If the answer is no, remove it and note why.

A common fear is that a smaller scope means a worse product. Cutting scope does not allow poor work. The features that stay must work well. The reason to cut is to give them your full attention: a clear screen, a form that handles errors, a first run that reaches the result. Modules 2 and 3 are about that quality. The risk of cutting is that you cut something people need. The check in chapter 1.5 is how you find out, and it is cheap. A feature on the not now list can come back in an evening, while a feature built too early can be hard to remove.

</GoDeeper>
