Maryam studies software on her own and does a little freelance work. With an AI coding tool, she built Halaqa in two weekends. Halaqa is Arabic for "study circle", and the app helps university students find or start a small study group for a course (a fictional app).

## One feature became fourteen

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-scene" />

Step through three screens. On "First", Halaqa had one feature: a search for a study group. On "Built", it has fourteen, from chat and polls to streaks, badges and an AI summary of notes, all the same size. On "Used", only three people open the app each week: Maryam and her friends Adel and Rawan.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the rest of Maryam's story">

Maryam did not plan fourteen features. She started with one: a student searches for a study group. The AI tool wrote it in an evening, and the next idea took only an evening too. Create a group. Then chat, then file sharing, then polls. Each one worked when she tried it, so each one felt like progress.

She showed Halaqa to Adel and Rawan. Both said it looked great and both agreed to use it. Three weeks later, still only the three of them opened it in a week. Nobody had told her it was bad. Nobody had a reason to come back, either.

A mentor then asked her two plain questions. Who is this app for? What is that person trying to do when they open it? Maryam could not answer in one sentence. She could only list what the app can do. A list of what an app can do is not an answer to what it is for.

In this module, Maryam answers those questions and a few more. She finds the problem under a request. She writes down the job a student wants done. She decides how much time Halaqa deserves and cuts the features that do not fit. Last, she picks one thing she can watch a student do, to know that it worked. She comes back to Halaqa in modules 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7, so you will see the same app change.

</GoDeeper>

## Each feature felt like progress

<MermaidDiagram title="Why the features kept coming" chart={`flowchart TD

  B --> C["It works when she tries it"]

Every turn of the loop felt like progress, because each feature worked. The question in the diamond was never asked. When building is cheap, the hard question is not how to build it. It is what to build.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why this matters for your work">

Today, a fellow can build a working app in a weekend. That is good news, and it also changes what a reader notices. A client, a mentor or a reviewer can open your link and see that it works. What they cannot see is whether you chose it for a reason.

Apps built quickly can look alike, and they can lack a clear reason to exist. This course helps you avoid both. The first step is in this module. Before you build, say who the app is for and what they are trying to do. Say how much time it deserves, and how you will know it worked. Those four things are yours. A tool cannot choose them for you, and a reviewer may notice when they are missing.

Later modules build on this one. Modules 2 and 3 make the screens clear and kind to use. Module 4 teaches how to judge quality with reasons. Modules 5 and 6 are about the generic look that AI tools produce and how to use those tools under your own judgment. Module 7 shows how to present the result so that a busy person understands its value fast. The capstone asks you to redesign your own project, or a provided one, and defend your changes.
