Maryam has a job, a cut list and 12 hours. One question is left: how will she know that the new Halaqa works? A big number will not tell her. She picks one goal and one thing she can watch a student do.

## One goal, one signal, one small check

<MermaidDiagram title="Goal, signal, check" chart={`flowchart LR
  G["Goal: join a group for an exam"] --> S["Signal: how many of 5 join, and how fast"]
  S --> C["Check: watch 5 classmates"]
`} legend="The goal says what should happen. The signal is what you can watch. The check is where you watch it." />

A goal says what should happen. A <GlossaryTerm term="signal" definition="Something you can see a person do that shows the goal was met or missed." /> is something you can watch a person do. Maryam's goal: a student joins a study group for an upcoming exam. Her signal: how many of five classmates join a group on their first visit, and how long it takes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where this habit comes from">

The habit is adapted from a paper by researchers at Google (CHI 2010) on measuring user experience at large scale [A30]. The paper names five categories: Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention and Task success, called HEART. It also gives a process called Goals, Signals, Metrics: state the goal, find signals in behaviour or attitude, and then build metrics.

HEART was made for web products with large logs and surveys. The paper itself says it is not always right to use every category. For example, Engagement may not mean much for a tool people must use at work [A30]. A project built by one person has no dashboard, so the course keeps only the shape of the idea: one goal, one signal, and a small check.

Of the five categories, the one a small check can see is task success: did the person finish, how long did it take, and what went wrong [A30]. That is why Maryam's signal is "joined a group" and "how long".

The paper also says that metrics should be combined with usability studies and field studies, and are not a substitute for early work with users [A30]. So the small check below is not a weaker version of the real thing. It is the part of the real thing that suits a project of one.

This course names HEART only as the source of the habit. It does not ask you to track five numbers.

</GoDeeper>

## A signal you can watch beats a count

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-signal-or-count" />

Sort eight lines Maryam could write down. A signal comes from watching a person. A count, such as page views, can rise while nobody joins a group. After a friend shared the link in a faculty group, Maryam's counter showed 1,000 page views. Her weekly users stayed at 3.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: counts, ratios and failure signals">

The Goals, Signals, Metrics paper gives three tips that apply to a small project [A30].

First, raw counts rise with the number of users, so use ratios or averages per user. "1,000 page views" grows when more people click a link, even if none of them joins a group.

Second, choose signals that are sensitive and specific to the goal. A page view is not specific: it does not tell you whether the goal was met. "Joined a group on the first visit" is specific.

Third, failure is sometimes easier to see than success. The paper gives abandonment and undo as examples [A30]. In a small check, pressing Back twice and closing the app is a failure signal. You can see it, and it points at a screen.

A count can still be useful context. It can show that people arrive. It cannot say whether they succeed.

Two traps. A signal you cannot watch, such as "students feel supported", cannot be checked in an afternoon. And a signal that people can produce without reaching the goal, such as "tapped Join", can look good while the goal is missed. Tie the signal to the goal itself.

</GoDeeper>

## Use ratios, not raw counts

<MermaidDiagram title="The count rose, the share fell" chart={`flowchart TD
  M1["Month 1: 10 visited, 5 joined"] --> R1["5 of 10: half joined"]
  M2["Month 2: 100 visited, 8 joined"] --> R2["8 of 100: 8 in every 100 joined"]
`} legend="Joined went up from 5 to 8. The share that joined went down from half to 8 in 100." />

The count went up from 5 to 8. The share went down from 5 of 10, which is half, to 8 of 100. A raw count grows when more people come. A ratio asks how many of the people who tried succeeded. Choose ratios, or averages per person.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the arithmetic, and how to report five people">

Check the numbers. Five out of 10 is 5 divided by 10, which is 0.5, or 50 percent. Eight out of 100 is 8 divided by 100, which is 0.08, or 8 percent. The count rose by 3. The share fell by 42 percentage points.

With five people in a check, each person is one fifth, which is 20 percentage points. So 2 of 5 is 40 percent and 4 of 5 is 80 percent. A single person changes the result by 20 points. For that reason, write "4 of 5" and not "80%". The first is honest about how small the check was. The second sounds more exact than it is.

Always write the ratio as "x of y", with the number who tried as the second number. A reader can then see both parts. "Four joined" hides how many tried.

Add the time when it matters. "About 2 minutes" is a measure you can take with the clock on your phone. Use the average of the people who finished, and say how many that was.

</GoDeeper>

## Watch three to five people try it

<MermaidDiagram title="One small check, repeated" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Pick one task"] --> B["Watch 3 to 5 people, no help"]
  B --> C["Count who finished and how long"]
  C --> D["Fix the biggest problem"]
  D --> E["Check again with new people"]
`} legend="Each round finds the biggest problems. Fix them and check again." />

Maryam gives each classmate one task: join a study group for Thursday's exam. She watches and says nothing. She writes down who finished, how long it took and where people got stuck. In her first check of the current app, 2 of 5 joined a group. She fixes the biggest problem, then checks again.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to run the check">

NN/g's guide to usability testing describes the parts: a facilitator, a task, and a participant who thinks aloud. It suggests five participants for a single group of users [A80]. A software blogger, writing in 2000, describes a free version: ask the next person who passes to try what you built [A70]. That is practice from one writer, not a study.

Steps for Maryam's check:

1. Write the task in one sentence: "Find a group for Thursday's exam and join it."
2. Ask three to five students from the group you built for: people who are revising for an exam. Friends are fine to begin with, but they are kind, so add one stranger.
3. Ask permission. Say you are testing the app, not them.
4. Use their phone or yours. If it is yours, close private chats first. If it is theirs, do not look at anything except the app.
5. Say "please say what you are thinking" and then do not help. If they give up, write that down.
6. Write for each person: finished or not, how long, where they stopped, and what they said.
7. Thank them. Do not share their names.

Another cheap check is the five-second test. You show a screen for five seconds, hide it, and ask what the person remembers. It tests a first impression on simple screens, not how the app is used, and its five-second figure rests mostly on anecdote [A71].

</GoDeeper>

## A check finds problems, not proof

<MermaidDiagram title="What a good result means" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["4 of 5 joined"] --> B["A good sign from five people"]
  B --> C["Not proof: fix, then check again"]
`} legend="A good result from five people is a sign. It is not proof that the design is good." />

After the redesign, Maryam's second check shows 4 of 5 joining, in about 2 minutes. That is a good sign. It does not prove the design is good. Five people from one group can show many of the biggest problems. Other groups of students may meet other problems. Fix, and check again.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what five people can and cannot tell you">

NN/g's 2000 article "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users" gives a model. The share of problems found grows with each person. In the authors' projects, one person found about 31 percent of the problems. On that basis, five people find about 85 percent of the problems in a round [A20]. This is a heuristic, an expert rule from experience, and not a law. Read it carefully. It is the share found by one kind of user in one round, in those projects. It is not a promise that five people find everything.

The article adds limits. A fix may not work, so run another round. One person can be unlike the rest, so use at least three. Different groups of users need their own people: three or four per group when there are two groups, and three per group when there are more [A20]. It advises several small rounds, about three rounds of five, over one large round.

For Halaqa this matters. Students who revise at home and students who revise in the library may meet different problems. If Maryam wants both, she needs people from both.

A small check finds problems. It does not show that a design is good. A pleasing design can also hide problems, because people who like how a thing looks may judge it easier to use [A58]. So watch what people do, not only what they say.

</GoDeeper>

```quiz
  - "The number of booked visits went up, and so did the number of patients"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "A visit to the page is not a booking. Visits can rise while no one books, so this is a count and not a signal."
  - "Right. It is a ratio of people who were watched, it matches the goal (a booking), and it has a time. It also says how many tried."
  - "What people say about how a page looks is not the same as watching them book. The goal is a booking, not a compliment."
  - "Both counts rose together. It does not say how many patients tried and failed, so it cannot show that the page works."
```
