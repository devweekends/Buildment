## What you make, and why it matters

You will write one page that answers the questions of this module for a real project. The page holds the problem you heard, the job as one sentence, and your time in hours. It also holds a cut list with a reason for each cut. It holds one goal with one signal you can watch, and a plan for the first check with three to five people. Use your own project, or use Wasel, the provided case further down. Both routes can be done on a phone, with a pen or a notes app.

This page is the first part of your capstone in chapters 7.6 and 7.7. When you build the case study page for your redesign, the problem and the job in this brief become its opening. The signal becomes the way you show that something changed. It is much cheaper to find that you are building the wrong thing on paper than after a week of work.

If your project belongs to a client or an employer, do not put their screens or data into an AI tool, and do not show them in public, without permission. Course 13 teaches that habit in full. The diagram shows the order of the steps, and how the brief feeds the capstone.

## The steps

1. Ask two or three people about the last time the problem happened, and write the problem in one sentence.
2. Write the job: "When I ..., I want to ..., so that ...", with no feature in it.
3. Set your time in hours, and say how many hours a week that is.
4. List every feature or idea, and sort each into must have, nice to have (marked with a tilde) or not now.
5. For at least the three cuts that are hardest to accept, write a four-part no: the item, who it affects, what happens without it, and what you do instead.
6. Write one goal, one signal as "x of y" with a time, and one failure signal.
7. Plan the check: the task in one sentence, three to five people, how you will ask permission, what you will write down, and the date.

Step 1 comes first because everything else depends on it. If the problem is a guess, the job will be a guess, and the cut list will cut the wrong things. Listen for a day, a place and what the person tried. If you cannot ask anyone this week, write the problem as a story from your own experience and mark it as unchecked.

Step 2 makes the problem small enough to build for. Take the app out of the sentence. If it still makes sense, it is a job. If not, you wrote a feature or a persona.

Step 3 comes before the cut list on purpose, because time is the one thing you cannot add. Count the hours you really have, and make the number hold in a bad week.

Step 4 is where the time becomes real. Use the one rule from chapter 1.4: a must have is something the job fails without. A nice to have is small and eases the same job, but the job works without it. Everything else is not now. If you have more must haves than hours, move something down.

Step 5 teaches you to say no, including to yourself. A cut with no reason will come back as an argument. A cut with four parts is short, kind and easy to check. You may write more than three, but three is the least. Choose the cuts that tempt you most, because those are the ones you will have to defend.

Step 6 turns the brief into something you can test. Write the signal as a share such as "4 of 5", because a raw count grows by itself. Add one failure signal, because failure is often easier to see than success.

Step 7 is the part most people skip. Without a plan, the check never happens. Put a date on it.

## Worked example: Maryam's brief for Halaqa

Maryam wrote her brief in one evening, after she had asked classmates about the last time they were stuck before an exam. Her problem line says that students with an exam close and a topic they do not understand cannot find classmates to revise with. Adel could not tell how to join an online group. Rawan searched for her course and found no group. The stories were different, but the moment was the same.

Her job is the sentence from chapter 1.3. Her time is 12 hours over two weeks, which is 6 hours a week. For the cut list, she asked the questions of each of the fourteen features. Group search and create group are must haves, and the call link for online groups is part of create group. Notifications and calendar sync are nice to haves, marked with a tilde. The other ten are not now. Her estimate is 8 hours for the must haves and the reshaped screens, and 2 hours for the check. Eight plus two is ten, so 2 of the 12 hours are spare, enough for one small nice to have and not for voice rooms.

Her goal is that a student joins a study group for an upcoming exam. Her signal is how many of five classmates join a group on their first visit, and how long it takes. Her failure signal is pressing Back twice on the first screen and closing the app. In her first check of the current app, 2 of 5 joined. She writes her target as 4 of 5 in about two minutes, and she writes that a target is a hope and not a promise.

Her plan for the check: the task is "Find a group for Thursday's exam and join it." The people are five new classmates who are revising for an exam. They did not take part in her first check. She will ask permission, use her own phone with private chats closed, write down who finished and how long, and not help. The date is the Thursday after the two weeks end.

Here is the choice she got wrong first. In her first draft, the entry for voice rooms said: "Not now: too hard to build." A classmate asked a fair question. Hard for whom? The reason was about Maryam, not about the students. She rewrote it with the four parts. The item: voice rooms. Who it affects: students in online groups. What happens without it: they can still meet, because Adel's real problem was a missing call link. What she does instead: add a call link to create group, and look again only if students still get stuck.

## The provided case: Wasel

If you have no project of your own, write the brief for Wasel. Everything you need is in this section. Wasel is a print and copy shop near the university gate, owned by Sami. It is open from 8 am to 6 pm. Black and white pages cost 0.50 shekels a page, colour pages cost 2 shekels a page, and binding costs 5 shekels. An order sent before noon is ready by 4 pm. In exam weeks Wasel gets about 30 to 40 orders a day. Most of its customers are students printing assignments.

Sami's nephew Karam built an order app for Wasel in one evening, with an AI tool. The app has a landing page, an order form with the fields File, Copies, Colour and Pickup time, and an order status page. This module is not about how those screens look. Later modules are. Here you decide what the app is for and what it should not do.

The job has three parts. When: an assignment is due tomorrow morning and I have no printer. I want to: send the file from my phone and collect it printed on my way to class. So that: I do not wait in line or miss the deadline.

Sami and Karam have ten ideas on the table. Karam has 6 hours this week, in the evenings, to improve the app. Sort the ideas with the rule from step 4, and give a reason for each.

1. Upload a file from the phone
2. Choose copies, colour or black and white, and binding
3. Pick a pickup time
4. See the status of an order
5. Show the price total before the student sends the order
6. Pay online
7. Loyalty points for regular students
8. A chat with Sami
9. An AI helper that suggests print settings
10. Dark mode

Wasel's count of 30 to 40 orders a day is useful to Sami, but it cannot tell him whether the app helped. A student could have walked in and ordered at the counter. A signal for Wasel must be something you can watch a student do with the app. For example: how many of five students send a complete order from their phone on their first visit, and how long it takes.

## Rubric

Your mentor reads your brief with this table. Each part is Met, Partly or Not yet.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The job | Situation, action and result are written, and no feature is named | All three parts, but a feature, a tool or a kind of person slips in | A part is missing, or it reads like a persona or a feature list |
| Time and cut list | Hours stated, six or more items sorted by the rule in step 4, and a four-part no for at least the three hardest cuts | Hours stated and items sorted, but fewer than three nos have all four parts | No hours, no cut list, or every item is a must have |
| Goal and signal | One goal, a signal written as x of y with a time, and one failure signal | A signal you can watch, but the share, the time or the failure signal is missing | Only a count, such as page views, or only an opinion |
| The check | A one-sentence task, three to five people, permission, notes and a date | A task and people, but permission, notes or a date is missing | No task, or no plan to watch people |

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet, and at most one part is Partly.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads the job line first and asks one question: if I read this to a friend, would the friend know what to put first on the home screen? The common weak answer lists what the app does, says "later" with no reason, or uses a count such as downloads as the signal. The usual follow-up question is "What would you do if only 2 of 5 people finished the task?" A good reply looks at where people stopped. It fixes that and checks again with new people. You can answer by voice, and you may add a photo of a handwritten page. An honest "not checked yet" beats a long answer that pretends everything is known.
