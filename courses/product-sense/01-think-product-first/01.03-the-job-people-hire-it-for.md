Maryam asked Adel, Rawan and a few classmates about the last time they were stuck. Their stories sound alike. An exam is close, they are stuck on a topic, and they want people to revise with. Now Maryam needs to write that down in a form she can build from.

## A job: situation, want and result

<MermaidDiagram title="The three parts of a job" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["When: exam soon, I am stuck"] --> B["I want to: find 2 or 3 classmates"]
  B --> C["So that: I do not study alone"]
`} legend="A job starts from a situation, names what the person wants to do, and ends with the progress they want." />

People hire a product, which means they choose it, to do a <GlossaryTerm term="job" definition="The progress a person wants to make in one situation." />. The course's template has three parts: When I (the situation), I want to (the action), and so that (the result). The picture fills them in for Halaqa.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the template and its three parts">

The idea of a job to be done comes from the Christensen Institute. A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. People hire a product to make it [A29]. The sentence "When I, I want to, so that" is this course's own template, built on that definition. This is practice, a way of working, and not a study. Use it as a tool, not as a law.

**When I** names the situation: where the person is, what just happened, what is missing. "When I study" is too wide. "When an exam is a few days away and I am stuck on a topic" is a situation you can picture.

**I want to** names the action in the person's own words, not a feature. "Find two or three classmates to revise with tonight or tomorrow" is something a person says. "Use a group search" is something a builder says.

**So that** names the progress. It is a change in the person's life, such as "I do not study alone". It is not "so that I can use voice rooms".

A quick test is to take the app out of the sentence. If the sentence still makes sense, it describes a person's need. Ghadeer's bootcamp gives another example: "When I finish a lesson and do not know if I understood it, I want to try one small task, so that I know whether to move on." No app appears in it, and it still says what to build.

Three common mistakes. Writing the solution into the want ("I want a chat"). Writing a result that is a feature ("so that I can see my badges"). Writing a situation so wide that every student fits it ("When I study, I want to learn").

</GoDeeper>

## Not a persona, not a feature list

<MermaidDiagram title="Three ways to describe an app" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["Persona: a student, 20 years old"] --> P2["Says who, not what comes first"]
  F["Feature list: 14 features"] --> F2["Says what exists, not why"]
  J["Job: stuck before an exam"] --> J2["Says what to put first"]
`} legend="Only the job starts from a situation, so only the job tells Maryam what to put first." />

A persona describes a kind of person. A feature list describes what the app can do. Neither tells Maryam what to put first. A job does, because it starts from a situation. Two students of the same age can have different jobs, and one student has different jobs on different days.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the course starts from the job">

The Christensen Institute defines a job by the circumstance and the progress, not by who the person is [A29]. A Harvard Business Review article from September 2016 argues that teams look too much at the product and too little at the job the customer is trying to get done [A28]. The page of that article that was available for this course held only an excerpt, so this course relies on the Institute's page for the definition.

None of this says that personas are useless. A description of a person can help in other ways, such as choosing words or knowing a person's phone. The point here is narrower. When you must decide what comes first on a screen, a situation and a goal give a clear answer. A kind of person does not.

A feature list is the opposite mistake. It starts from what the app has. Maryam's list has fourteen items, and the list does not say which one a student needs on the night before an exam. The job gives her a way to ask that question of every item, which chapter 1.4 does.

</GoDeeper>

## The same app takes a different shape

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-two-jobs" />

Switch between the three versions. As built, Halaqa fits no job, so nothing comes first. The second version fits a job: find a group tonight. Its first thing is tonight's groups, with seats left and a Join button. The third fits a different job, a weekly habit, and puts a week calendar and a streak first.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading the three screens">

**As built.** The home screen asks "What do you want to do?" and offers fourteen things of the same size. A new student must read all of them to find the one she needs. The screen has no job, so it cannot put anything first.

**Tonight.** The heading speaks to the situation: "Exam soon? Join a group tonight." The groups come with the facts a student needs to decide: the time, the place and the seats left. One button on each says what happens next. Streaks, badges and themes are not on this screen. They are not wrong. They do not serve this job.

**Weekly.** This version serves a student who wants to study a little every week. It shows the days of the week, the sessions done and the next session. The streak belongs here because the job is about keeping a habit.

Both shaped screens are good for their own job, and neither is the as-built screen with a new colour. Maryam can choose only one for the first version, because a screen can put only one thing first. Her stories point to the first job. The second job is real too. Her two friends enjoy streaks. That job waits, and chapter 1.4 shows how to say so with a reason.

An app can serve more than one job over time. What it cannot do is serve two jobs equally on one screen at the same moment.

</GoDeeper>

## One product, two jobs, in the milkshake story

<MermaidDiagram title="One product, two jobs" chart={`flowchart TD
  M["One milkshake"] --> J1["Job: a morning commute"]
  M --> J2["Job: an afternoon treat for a child"]
`} legend="The product stays the same. The situation and the progress change, so the job changes." />

The Christensen Institute gives a well-known example. People buy the same milkshake for a morning commute and, at another time, as an afternoon treat for a child. The product is the same. The situation and the progress are different, so the job is different. A product can be hired for more than one job.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the example does and does not show">

The example is used here for one idea only: the same product can be hired for different jobs in different circumstances [A29]. This course does not repeat figures or findings from the milkshake story, because the pages checked for this course did not give sources for them.

The lesson for Halaqa is practical. Adel and Rawan use the app for the weekly habit. The students Maryam asked need it on the night before an exam. The same app serves both, but a first version cannot serve both well. Writing the job tells you whose version you are building.

</GoDeeper>

## Write the job from the stories you heard

<MermaidDiagram title="From stories to a job" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Stories about the last time"] --> B["What is the same in them?"]
  B --> C["Write: When I, I want to, so that"]
  C --> D["Test: does it say what comes first?"]
`} legend="The job comes from what the stories have in common. The test is whether it tells you what to put first." />

Maryam's stories share a situation. She writes: "When an exam is a few days away and I am stuck on a topic, I want to find two or three classmates to revise with tonight or tomorrow, so that I do not study alone." The test: does it say what comes first? Yes. Finding a group does.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: test the job, then change it">

Read the job to a friend who has not seen Halaqa. Ask what they would put first on the home screen. If they say tonight's groups, the job works. If they say "I do not know", the sentence is too wide or it hides a feature.

A job can be wrong. After Maryam watches students use her app in chapter 1.5, she may learn something new. Her stories may have come only from friends. Or most students may look for groups on the same day, not days ahead. Then she changes the sentence. A job is a tool that you test, not a statement you defend.

Do not write one job for your whole project if the stories show two. Write both. Then decide which one the first version serves.

</GoDeeper>
