Adel is one of the two friends who use Maryam's app, Halaqa. This week he sends her a message: "Please add voice rooms." Maryam could build them in a weekend with her AI tool. First she asks a different question, because a request does not tell her what is wrong, and building the wrong thing would cost her a whole weekend. She wants to build the right thing, not the first thing.

## Sort nine lines into three buckets

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-problem-or-feature" />

A request names a fix, not what went wrong, so Maryam asks three questions: what problem would this solve, for whom, and when? Then she sorts each line by a rule, not by its first word. A problem already happened to a person. A feature names a thing to build, and a guess says what people would do. Some lines are written to mislead you, so read each one twice.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the three questions">

Each question closes a gap that a request leaves open. Shape Up, a free book from Basecamp, asks it this way: what is really going wrong?

**What problem would this solve?** A request such as "add voice rooms" does not say what went wrong. A problem is a moment when a person tried to do something and could not. Ask for the moment.

**For whom?** Pick one person, not "users". Adel studies at home. A student who studies in the library may not share his problem.

**When?** A problem happens in a situation: the night before an exam, on a weak connection, in a library room where talking is not allowed. The situation is the clue to the cheapest fix. Chapter 1.3 turns the situation into a job.

Shape Up gives an example. A request for complex permission rules became a warning on an archive action. It was a change of one day. The book describes a method from a software company, so treat it as practice, not as a study. Its lesson is to narrow the problem before you choose a solution.

Two cautions. If you ask "why?" again and again, the other person feels questioned. Ask once, then ask for the story. And sometimes the request is right. A classmate asks for dark mode because she revises at midnight in a shared room, and the bright screen wakes her sister. The problem is real, and dark mode as a choice she can switch on is a fair answer. The method does not say no to features. It says to know the problem first.

**Three requests, three questions.** Write the request, then the last time it happened, then the problem underneath. If you cannot fill the middle column, you have not asked yet.

| Request | The last time it happened | The problem underneath |
|---|---|---|
| Add a leaderboard (Rawan) | She could not tell if anyone else was revising her course | A student cannot see that others are studying the same exam |
| Add dark mode (a classmate) | She revised at midnight and the bright screen woke her sister | The screen is too bright in a dark room |
| Add polls (another classmate) | Her group could not agree on a time and lost two days | Members cannot settle a meeting time quickly |

Look at the third column. Each problem is smaller than the feature in the first column. Rawan's problem, for example, might be fixed by showing how many students have joined a course, which is far smaller than a leaderboard.

A guess is not bad. "Students would pay for AI summaries" may turn out to be true. A guess is a belief that nobody has checked, so it is a reason to ask, not a reason to build. A problem is also unchecked until you hear it from a real person. The difference is that a problem happened to someone, so you can ask about it.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask about last time, not next time

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-last-time" />

A question about next time gets a polite yes, and a yes costs nothing. A question about the last time gets a story: a day, a place and what the person tried. Look at what Maryam's notes can use from each answer. Adel is kind in both, but only one answer holds facts. This is the course's own habit, not a research finding, and you can use it today.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: asking about the last time">

People are kind about the future. They say yes to a feature because they want to help you. They are more exact about the past, because the past is a fact they remember. So ask for one real event: when it was, where they were, what they tried, and what happened next. Then be quiet and write it down.

Keep these rules. Do not name your feature in the question. "Don't you think voice rooms would help?" gets a yes. Do not ask one friend about everyone: ask about this person's own last time. Do not collect opinions about ten features at once. Ask about one problem, then stop.

Ask permission before you ask. Use made-up names if you write the story down for someone else, and do not share a classmate's words with a name on them.

No source in this course's research says this is the best way to check a problem. It is the course's own habit, and it is cheap. You can use it today with a classmate, and you will use it again in chapter 1.5, when you watch people try your app.

Typical mistakes are easy to spot. Asking only friends who want you to succeed. Asking someone about a problem they never had. Counting yeses. A story with a day and a place is worth more than ten yeses.

</GoDeeper>

## The problem was smaller than the request

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m1-the-cheaper-fix" />

On Tuesday Adel found a Statistics group marked online, but could not tell how to join the call. He gave up. The problem is not missing voice rooms: the group has no call link. When a creator picks online, the form can ask for the link, and one Join call button answers Adel. Both are much smaller to build than voice rooms, and Maryam can finish them this week.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the smaller fix comes first">

Voice rooms need audio in the app, a place to run it, and care about noise and who can speak. A call link needs one field for the person who creates the group and one button for the student who joins. Both answer Adel's story. Only one can be finished this week.

Choose the smallest fix that answers the problem you heard, then watch whether it works. If Adel still gets stuck, you have learned something real, and a bigger fix now has a reason behind it. If he does not, you saved weeks.

A smaller fix is not a lazy fix. It must still work well on a phone, say what it does, and handle the case where the link is missing. Chapter 3.2 covers those cases.

</GoDeeper>

## One story is a start, not proof

<MermaidDiagram title="Check the problem before you build" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Adel's story"] --> B["Ask two or three more people"]
  B --> C{"Same problem?"}
  C -->|"Yes"| D["Fix it, the smallest way first"]
  C -->|"No"| E["Ask again before you build"]
`} legend="Diamond: a question." />

One story is a start. Maryam asks Rawan and a classmate about the last time too. If they describe the same stuck moment, she has a problem worth fixing. If not, Adel's problem may be only his, and she keeps asking. She does not need a perfect answer, only more than one voice. Chapter 1.5 shows how to check with three to five people.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when the feature is the right answer">

Sometimes the problem needs the big feature. Suppose five students tell Maryam that online groups fail because nobody can hear each other well on a weak connection, and a call link does not help. Then voice rooms, or a different tool for calls, may be the honest answer.

What changes is the order. You hear the problem, you try the smallest answer, you check, and only then do you build more. A feature that comes from a problem has a test built in: did the problem go away?

If you cannot tell whether a problem is common, count honestly. One person is one story. Three people with the same story is a pattern worth acting on. Ten people who all say yes to a feature is not a pattern, because you have not heard what happened to any of them.

</GoDeeper>
