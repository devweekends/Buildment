Karam asks what the page should be instead. Sami says, "Say what I would say to a student at my counter." This chapter follows that idea.

## Three first screens for Wasel

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-point-of-view" />

Switch between the three first screens. The card grid has nothing Nour can use. In the second, the price list is the picture, and the headline says what happens next. In the third, the 4 pm promise is set large as the one bold choice, and it carries its condition: send before noon.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: real content is the best picture">

At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), both AI companies say the same thing about where a good page starts. Anthropic's design skill asks for a design grounded in the product's real content and audience, not in a generic brief. OpenAI's guidance asks you to settle the type, the colours, the number of sections and the one main action first, and to use real content. In the Nielsen Norman Group's (NN/g) test of AI prototyping tools, a detailed prompt or an attached design frame gave the closest match to a human design.

For Wasel, real content means the price list, the times and the place, exactly as Sami would say them. A picture should answer a question. A stock photo of a smiling student with a laptop answers none of the questions Nour has. A photo of the real price list on the counter, or the list typed as a receipt, answers "how much" at once.

Real content has a cost: you must have it. If you cannot write the price list, the hours and the promise, the page is not ready to design, and no layout will hide that. Ask the owner, copy the numbers exactly, and keep them in one place so that the page and the shop never disagree.

OpenAI's frontend guide adds an option for small shops: build the working screen as the first screen, and add a landing page only if it is needed. For Wasel, ask whether Nour needs a landing page at all, or whether the order form with the price list beside it is the page.

Evidence label: practice, from the vendors' guidance and one NN/g test.

</GoDeeper>

## Facts first, then the order

<MermaidDiagram title="How an editor lays out a page" chart={`flowchart TD
  F["List the real facts: prices, times, place"] --> O["Order them as the person asks"]
  O --> L["Make the one that matters most large"]
  L --> S["Keep the rest small and plain"]
`} legend="The layout comes from the facts and the questions, not from a template." />

An <GlossaryTerm term="editorial layout" definition="A page laid out like a good article: facts in the order a reader asks, with type showing what matters." /> follows the order a reader asks. Nour asks four things: what is this, how much, when, and how do I start? So the page answers in that order. Type does the work. Size and weight show what comes first.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading order and few boxes">

The four questions are the course's own template for a small shop, not a standard. In the course's view, they fit how a person on a phone reads: from the top, until she has what she came for. NN/g describes an F-shaped reading pattern on text-heavy pages that give no strong cues, and calls it bad for readers. Its advice is to put key points first, start headings with informative words and cut what is not needed.

The same idea sits in the eighth of NN/g's ten usability heuristics. A "minimalist" screen shows relevant information only. That is not the same as showing fewer things. Removing the price list from Wasel's page would make it emptier, not simpler, because the price list is the relevant part.

Few boxes does not mean no boxes. OpenAI's frontend guide asks for cards with a small corner radius and no cards inside cards. A box earns its place when it groups things a reader compares, as in the price cards of chapter 5.2. A box that only frames a single line does nothing but add edges.

For Wasel's second screen, the order from the top is this. A headline says what happens and when. Then come the price list, the Upload a file button, the condition about noon and the opening hours. Each answers the next question Nour would ask.

Evidence label: heuristic for the NN/g points, practice for the vendors' card advice, and the course's template for the four questions.

</GoDeeper>

## Bold everywhere, then bold in one place

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-boldness" />

Switch to a version, then to its blurred twin. When every line is large and heavy, the blurred picture has no first thing. This is the squint test from chapter 2.2. When one line is large, it survives the blur. Spend your boldness in one place. Notice that the bold line is also the fact the job needs.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: counting what is bold">

Count the sizes in the two screens. In the all-bold screen, four lines share one size and one weight, and the button is large too. The eye has five equal marks. In the one-bold screen the text uses three sizes: 48 for the time, 15 for the first line and 13 for everything else. The time is about 3.7 times the size of the small text (48 divided by 13).

NN/g advises no more than three sizes, with the most important element the largest. The one-bold screen follows that, and the squint test confirms it. Anthropic's design skill gives the same idea in one phrase: spend your boldness in one place.

Bold in one place is not the same as decorating one word. Anthropic's skill lists a single accented word in a headline, and small all-caps labels, among the commonest tells. The bold part has to be a whole fact the person needs, such as a time.

A blur is a quick check, not proof. It shows what stands out in a picture. It does not show whether it is the right thing for the person using it. For that, ask a few people, as in chapter 1.5.

Evidence label: heuristic, from NN/g on hierarchy and the squint test, plus the vendor's phrase.

</GoDeeper>

## A bold choice has a reason

<MermaidDiagram title="From the job to the bold choice" chart={`flowchart TD
  J["Job: collect it printed on the way to class"] --> W["Worry: missing the deadline"]
  W --> B["Bold choice: the 4 pm promise, set large"]
  B --> C["Check: ask three students what they saw first"]
`} legend="The bold part comes from the job and the person's worry, and it is checked with people." />

A bold choice is bold for a reason you can say aloud. Wasel's job is to send a file from a phone and collect it printed on the way to class. The student's worry is missing the deadline. So the time is the thing to make large. If you cannot say the reason, the bold part is decoration.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when a big number is a default">

Anthropic's skill names a big number with a small label, often with a gradient accent, as the default treatment of a top section. That looks like the third screen. How can one be a choice and the other a default?

The difference is the reason. A large count such as "40 orders served" would be decoration for Wasel, because no student decides anything from it. A large "4 pm" answers the exact worry that brings a student to the page. The shape is the same. What the number does for the person is different.

That is why the reason comes before the size. Write one sentence, "I made this large because...", and say it to someone. Then check it. Show the screen to three students for a few seconds and ask what they noticed first and what they expect to happen if they tap the button. A small check like this finds problems. It does not prove that the design is good.

If you did not run the check, say so. "I did not test that" is a better sentence in a case study than an invented number. Chapter 7.3 comes back to this.

Evidence label: practice, with the course's own habit of a small check from chapter 1.5.

</GoDeeper>

## The quality floor stays in place

<MermaidDiagram title="What a bold choice must still pass" chart={`flowchart TD
  B["Your bold choice"] --> C["Contrast: 4.5 to 1, large text 3 to 1"]
  B --> F["A visible focus mark on every control"]
  B --> M["Motion stops when the reader asks"]
  B --> P["The page fits a small phone"]
`} legend="A point of view sits on top of the basics. It does not replace them." />

Boldness does not excuse the basics. The 4 pm line must still pass contrast: 4.5 to 1 for normal text, 3 to 1 for large text. Every control needs a visible focus mark. Motion must stop when the reader asks. The page must fit a small phone. You met these floors in modules 2 and 3.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the floor, with numbers">

WCAG 2.2, the standard this course uses, sets the contrast floor at level AA. Normal text needs 4.5 to 1 against its background. Large text needs 3 to 1. Large text means at least 18 point, or 14 point bold, which is about 24 or 18.5 CSS pixels. A 48-pixel "4 pm" is large text. A thin grey line is not.

Computed with the WCAG formula, grey #999999 on white is 2.85 to 1, so it fails even as large text. Grey #777777 is 4.48 to 1 and fails by a hair for normal text. Grey #767676 is 4.54 to 1 and passes. Grey #595959 is 7.0 to 1, and black is 21 to 1. Check a bold accent colour in the light and the dark setting.

Keyboard focus must be visible: that is WCAG 2.4.7, level AA. For motion, the browser setting prefers-reduced-motion tells your page that the reader wants less. WCAG 2.2.2, level A, asks for a way to stop moving content that starts by itself and lasts over five seconds. WCAG 2.3.3, level AAA, goes further for motion triggered by what the reader does. AAA is an extra, not the course bar.

Anthropic's skill lists the same floor for its own designs: works on a small screen, visible focus, reduced motion respected and accessible colour. A bold page that fails these is not bold. It is hard to use.

Evidence label: standard for the WCAG items, practice for the vendor's list.

</GoDeeper>

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Hamza's page for a juice stand sets the headline, the prices, the hours and the button all in 24-pixel bold. A squint test shows no first thing. His job note says: 'When I am thirsty after class, I want to see today's price and order in one tap.' What should he change first?"
options:
  - "Make every line smaller, so that the page looks calmer and quieter"
  - "Pick the one fact the job needs, and set it larger than the rest"
  - "Add a bright gradient behind the headline, so that it stands out"
  - "Keep all lines bold, and make each one fade in so they take turns"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "Smaller lines of equal weight still give no first thing. The squint test would show the same flat page, only smaller."
  - "Right. His job note points to today's price. Making that one fact larger than the rest gives the page a first thing, and he can give the reason."
  - "A gradient is decoration. It adds colour but gives no reason, and it does not tell the eye which fact matters."
  - "Fades on every line are one of the generic defaults, and they do not make one fact stand out. Motion should show a change, not decorate."
```
