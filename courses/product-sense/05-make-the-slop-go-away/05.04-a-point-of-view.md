Karam asks what the page should be instead. Sami says, "Say what I would say to a student at my counter." This chapter follows that idea.

## Three first screens for Wasel

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-point-of-view" />

Switch between the three first screens. The card grid has nothing Nour can use. In the second, the price list is the picture, and the headline says what happens next and by when. In the third, the 4 pm promise is set large as the one bold choice, and it carries its condition: send before noon.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: real content is the best picture">

At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), both AI companies say the same thing about where a good page starts. Anthropic's design skill asks for a design grounded in the product's real content and audience, not in a generic brief. OpenAI's guidance asks you to settle the type, the colours, the number of sections and the one main action first, and to use real content. NN/g, the research company from chapter 1.5, tested AI prototyping tools. A detailed prompt or an attached design frame gave the closest match to a human design.

For Wasel, real content means the price list, the times and the place, exactly as Sami would say them. A picture should answer a question. A stock photo of a smiling student with a laptop answers none of the questions Nour has. A photo of the real price list on the counter, or the list typed as a receipt, answers "how much" at once.

Real content has a cost: you must have it. If you cannot write the price list, the hours and the promise, the page is not ready to design, and no layout will hide that. Ask the owner, copy the numbers exactly, and keep them in one place so that the page and the shop never disagree.

OpenAI's frontend guide adds an option for small shops: build the working screen as the first screen, and add a landing page only if it is needed. For Wasel, ask whether Nour needs a landing page at all, or whether the order form with the price list beside it is the page.

Evidence label: practice, from the vendors' guidance and one NN/g test.

</GoDeeper>

## Facts in the order a person asks

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-asked-order" />

An <GlossaryTerm term="editorial layout" definition="A page laid out like an article, in the order a reader asks." /> follows the order a reader asks. On Maryam's Halaqa page a student asks: what is this, which groups, when, and how do I start? The template page puts a slogan and three claims first and the one real fact last. The asked page answers in order.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading order and few boxes">

The four questions are the course's own template for a small app, not a standard. In the course's view, they fit how a person on a phone reads: from the top, until she has what she came for. NN/g describes an F-shaped reading pattern on text-heavy pages that give no strong cues, and calls it bad for readers. Its advice is to put key points first, start headings with informative words and cut what is not needed.

The same idea sits in the eighth of NN/g's ten usability heuristics. A "minimalist" screen shows relevant information only. That is not the same as showing fewer things. Removing the price list from Wasel's page would make it emptier, not simpler, because the price list is the relevant part.

Few boxes does not mean no boxes. OpenAI's frontend guide asks for cards with a small corner radius and no cards inside cards. A box earns its place when it groups things a reader compares, as in the price cards of chapter 5.2. A box that only frames a single line does nothing but add edges.

For a small shop such as Wasel, the same order reads like this from the top. A headline says what happens and when. Then come the price list, the Upload a file button, the condition about noon and the opening hours. Each answers the next question a student would ask.

Evidence label: heuristic for the NN/g points, practice for the vendors' card advice, and the course's template for the four questions.

</GoDeeper>

## Bold everywhere, then bold in one place

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-boldness" />

Switch to a version, then to its blurred twin. When every line is large and heavy, the blurred picture has no first thing. This is the squint test from chapter 2.2. When one line is large, it survives the blur. Spend your boldness in one place. Notice that the bold line is also the fact the job needs.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: counting what is bold">

Count the sizes in the two screens. In the all-bold screen, four lines share one size and one weight, and the button is large too. The eye has five equal marks. In the one-bold screen the text uses three sizes: 48 for the time, 15 for the first line and 13 for everything else. The time is about 3.7 times the size of the small text (48 divided by 13).

NN/g advises no more than three sizes, with the most important element the largest. The one-bold screen follows that, and the squint test confirms it. Anthropic's design skill gives the same idea in one phrase: spend your boldness in one place.

Bold in one place is not the same as decorating one word. Anthropic's skill lists a single accented word in a headline, and small all-caps labels, among the commonest signs of the default look. The bold part has to be a whole fact the person needs, such as a time.

A blur is a quick check, not proof. It shows what stands out in a picture. It does not show whether it is the right thing for the person using it. For that, ask a few people, as in chapter 1.5.

Evidence label: heuristic, from NN/g on hierarchy and the squint test, plus the vendor's phrase.

</GoDeeper>

## A bold choice has a reason

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-big-number" />

Both screens show one big figure and a small label. Only the second answers a question a student has before she sends a file. The job is to collect a printed file on the way to class, and the worry is missing the deadline. So the time is the thing to make large. If you cannot say the reason, the bold part is decoration.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when a big number is a default">

Anthropic's skill names a big number with a small label, often with a gradient accent, as the default treatment of a top section. That looks like the third screen in the first picture. How can one be a choice and the other a default?

The difference is the reason. A large count such as "30 to 40 orders" would be decoration for Wasel, because no student decides anything from it. A large "4 pm" answers the exact worry that brings a student to the page. The shape is the same. What the number does for the person is different.

That is why the reason comes before the size. Write one sentence, "I made this large because...", and say it to someone. Then check it. Show the screen to three students for a few seconds and ask what they noticed first and what they expect to happen if they tap the button. A small check like this finds problems. It does not prove that the design is good.

If you did not run the check, say so. "I did not test that" is a better sentence in a case study than an invented number. Chapter 7.3 comes back to this.

Evidence label: practice, with the course's own habit of a small check from chapter 1.5.

</GoDeeper>

## The quality floor stays in place

<MermaidDiagram title="What a bold choice must still pass" chart={`flowchart TD
  B["Your bold choice"] --> C{"Contrast passes?"}
  C -->|"No"| X["Fix it, then check again"]
  C -->|"Yes"| F{"Focus mark visible?"}
  F -->|"No"| X
  F -->|"Yes"| M{"Motion stops on request?"}
  M -->|"No"| X
  M -->|"Yes"| P{"Fits a small phone?"}
  P -->|"No"| X
  P -->|"Yes"| S["Ship it"]
  X --> C
`} legend="Contrast means 4.5 to 1 for normal text and 3 to 1 for large text. Large means 18 point, or 14 point bold." />

Boldness does not excuse the basics. The 4 pm line must still pass contrast. Every control needs a visible focus mark. Motion must stop when the reader asks. The page must fit a small phone. You met these floors in modules 2 and 3.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the floor, with numbers">

WCAG 2.2, the standard this course uses, sets the contrast floor at level AA. Normal text needs 4.5 to 1 against its background. Large text needs 3 to 1. Large text means at least 18 point, or 14 point bold, which is about 24 or 18.5 CSS pixels. A 48-pixel "4 pm" is large text. A thin grey line is not.

Computed with the WCAG formula, grey #999999 on white is 2.85 to 1, so it fails even as large text. Grey #777777 is 4.48 to 1 and fails by a small amount for normal text. Grey #767676 is 4.54 to 1 and passes. Grey #595959 is 7.0 to 1, and black is 21 to 1. The table of common greys in chapter 2.5 lists more, so you can check a colour on a phone. Check a bold accent colour in the light and the dark setting.

Keyboard focus must be visible: that is WCAG 2.4.7, level AA. For motion, the browser setting prefers-reduced-motion tells your page that the reader wants less. WCAG 2.2.2, level A, asks for a way to stop moving content that starts by itself and lasts over five seconds. WCAG 2.3.3, level AAA, goes further for motion triggered by what the reader does. AAA is an extra, not the course bar.

At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), Anthropic's skill lists the same floor for its own designs: works on a small screen, visible focus, reduced motion respected and accessible colour. A bold page that fails these is not bold. It is hard to use.

Evidence label: standard for the WCAG items, practice for the vendor's list.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Ziyad sets the club name, the pitch times, the prices and the Book button all in 24-pixel bold. Squinting at the page shows no first thing. His job note says: 'When I finish work, I want to see which pitch is free tonight and book it in one tap.' What should he change first?"
options:
  - "Make the club name larger than the other lines"
  - "Set tonight's free pitch time larger than the rest"
  - "Make every line smaller and lighter"
  - "Put a gradient behind the pitch times"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "A club name is a common first thing, but the job note is about a free pitch tonight. The name does not help the player decide."
  - "Right. The job note points to which pitch is free tonight. Making that one fact larger than the rest gives the page a first thing, and Ziyad can give the reason."
  - "Smaller lines of equal weight still give no first thing. The squint test would show the same flat page, only smaller."
  - "A gradient is decoration. It adds colour but gives no reason, and it does not tell the eye which fact matters."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Bayan's clinic desk page has two lines of grey #8A8A8A text on white, and a checker scores both at 3.45 to 1. One is 'Walk-ins until 5 pm' in 36-pixel bold. The other is 'Closed on Fridays' in 14-pixel regular. What is the smallest change that meets the AA bar?"
options:
  - "Leave the big line, and leave the small line"
  - "Darken the big line to at least 4.5 to 1, and leave the small line"
  - "Leave the big line, and darken the small line to at least 4.5 to 1"
  - "Darken both lines to at least 7 to 1"

  - "Large text needs 3 to 1, so the big line passes. Normal text needs 4.5 to 1, so the small line fails at 3.45."

  - "Right. 36-pixel bold is large text, which needs 3 to 1, and 3.45 passes. 14-pixel regular is normal text, which needs 4.5 to 1, so only the small line must change."

```
