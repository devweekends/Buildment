This is the gate for module 5. It asks how you now look at a page, not what you built, because your de-slopped page is already in chapter 5.5. Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

## Module 5 checklist

- [ ] I can say, for any part of a page, whether I chose it for this product, and I can give the reason when I did
- [ ] I can tap through a page and name the parts that carry the generic look, without calling any single part wrong in itself
- [ ] I know that swapping one default for another is not a choice, and I can say why
- [ ] I test each sentence with "could a hundred products say this?" and replace the failing ones with a fact
- [ ] I put real content first, in the order a person asks, and I spend boldness in one place with a reason
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 5.2 to 5.4, finished the practice in 5.5 and the quiz in 5.6

## Learning log

1. **Your last page:** Think of the last page or screen you made with an AI tool. Which part would you now have to admit you did not choose?
2. **Your words:** Pick one sentence from your project that a hundred products could say. What could you say instead, and where would you find the fact?
3. **Your boldness:** Where on your page did you spend your boldness, and what is the reason you could say aloud to Sami? If nowhere, where should it go?
4. **Your defaults:** Name one default you would keep on purpose, such as a font, a card or a colour, and the reason that makes it a choice.

## What changed in how you look at a page

Before this module, a page that looked polished probably looked finished to you. A glow, a badge and a row of three cards signal "done", and a tool can produce them in seconds. The change this module asks for is one question, asked of every part: did I choose this, and what does it do for the person? Karam did nothing wrong when he left the page as the tool made it. The tool made the choices because he did not, and you now know which choices those were.

The second change is in how you judge words. You may have judged a sentence by whether it sounded good. You now swap the product and see whether the sentence survives. "Revolutionize your printing experience" survives a swap to baking, so it tells a customer nothing. "Send before noon and collect it the same afternoon" does not survive, so it tells a customer something. This test does not need you to guess who wrote the sentence, and it keeps working when the fashionable words change.

The third change is about emphasis and defaults. You may have thought that making more things bold makes a page more memorable. A squint shows that with everything bold there is no first thing, and that one bold choice with a reason is what stays. You have also seen that a default is not a mistake, and that a calm page is not automatically a chosen one. A purple that matches a shop sign, a phone's own font on weak data and a row of cards that hold prices are all defaults that someone chose. The skill is not removing things. It is deciding, and being able to say why.

The fourth change is about disagreement. When someone asks you to put a part back, you do not have to win, and you do not have to give in. You can ask one question that shows whether a reason exists. If the person has a reason, the part becomes a choice and you keep it. If they do not, you have a reason of your own to leave it out. Either way the page improves, and the other person has been heard. This is also how you will answer a reviewer, a client or a classmate in the next modules.

## How your mentor will read it

This gate asks for a reply, not a report. Someone asks you to put back a part you removed, and you answer them in writing. A mentor reads your reply with the table below. Your page can be about any project. What counts is whether the thinking is there, and whether you would still change your mind if the person gave a good reason.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the part did | Names one specific part and says what it did for the person on the page | Names the part but not what it did | Names a whole style instead of a part |
| Chosen or not | Says honestly whether anyone chose it, and asks one question that would show a reason | The question is vague, such as "do you like it?" | Gives no question, or claims a choice with no reason |
| Both outcomes | Says what you would do if the answer gives a reason, and what you would do if it does not | Covers only one of the two outcomes | Makes no decision |
| The check | Names one check that someone else could repeat, such as a contrast ratio, the squint test or asking three students | The check is a feeling, such as "it feels better" | Names no check |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at most one part is "Partly".

The most common weak answer names a style instead of a part. "I removed the purple AI look" says nothing a mentor can check. A second weak answer gives in at once ("fine, I will put it back") or refuses at once, without a question. The usual follow-up question is: "What would have to be true for you to put that part back?" A good reply gives a condition you could check, such as "if three students could not find the price without it". You can answer by typing, or by dictating with your phone's microphone.

## Your answer

The learning log is for you, and it saves as you type. Write it before the open question, because your answers there often hold the part you will write about. If you cannot think of a part you did not choose, look at your last page again and ask the same question of each of its parts. Finding them is not an insult to the page or to you. It is the first step of the next draft.

Write the reply as you would send it to a person, in plain sentences. You do not need a long text. Five short parts, with one or two sentences each, are enough. Name the part exactly, as in "the purple glow behind the headline", and not as "the AI look". If you use your own project, change the names but keep the five parts.

Keep your reply honest. If you have not run the check, say how you would. An honest "I have not tested this yet" is worth more to a mentor than an invented number.
