This is the gate for module 5. It asks how you now look at a page, not what you built, because your de-slopped page is already in chapter 5.5. Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

## Module 5 checklist

- [ ] I can say, for any part of a page, whether I chose it for this product, and I can give the reason when I did
- [ ] I can tap through a page and name the parts that carry the generic look, without calling any single part wrong in itself
- [ ] I test each sentence with "could a hundred products say this?" and replace the failing ones with a price, a time, a thing a user did or a thing I gave up
- [ ] I put real content first, in the order a person asks, and I spend boldness in one place with a reason
- [ ] I check that a bold choice still passes the floor: contrast, a visible focus mark, motion that stops on request, and a small phone
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 5.2 to 5.4, finished the practice in 5.5 and the quiz in 5.6

## Learning log

1. **Your last page:** Think of the last page or screen you made with an AI tool. Which part would you now have to admit you did not choose?
2. **Your words:** Pick one sentence from your project that a hundred products could say. What could you say instead, and where would you find the fact?
3. **Your boldness:** Where on your page did you spend your boldness, and what is the reason you could say aloud to Sami? If nowhere, where should it go?
4. **Your defaults:** Name one default you would keep on purpose, such as a font, a card or a colour, and the reason that makes it a choice.

## What changed in how you look at a page

Before this module, a page that looked polished probably looked finished to you. A glow, a badge and a row of three cards signal "done", and a tool can produce them in seconds. The change this module asks for is one question, asked of every part: did I choose this, and what does it do for the person? Karam did nothing wrong when he left the page as the tool made it. The tool made the choices because he did not, and you now know which choices those were.

The second change is in how you judge words. You may have judged a sentence by whether it sounded good. You now swap the product and see whether the sentence survives. "Revolutionize your printing experience" survives a swap to baking, so it tells a customer nothing. "Send before noon. It is ready by 4 pm" does not survive, so it tells a customer something. This test does not need you to guess who wrote the sentence, and it keeps working when the fashionable words change.

The third change is about emphasis and defaults. You may have thought that making more things bold makes a page more memorable. You have seen that with everything bold, a squint shows no first thing, and that one bold choice with a reason is what stays. You have also seen that a default is not a sin. A purple that matches a shop sign, a phone's own font on weak data and a row of cards that hold prices are all defaults that someone chose. The skill is not removing things. It is deciding, and being able to say why.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer with the table below. Your page can be about any project. The default you pick can be any one you met. Examples are a glow, a gradient, a card grid, a pill, an icon tile, a template label, a fade on every section or a line of copy. What counts is whether the thinking is there.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The default | Names one specific part of a real page and says what it did for the person | Names a part but not what it did | Names no specific part, or says a whole style is wrong |
| Choice or not | Says honestly whether the part was chosen, and gives a reason tied to the product or the person when it was | The reason is only "it looks good" | Calls it a default and moves on, or claims a choice with no reason |
| The decision and the give-up | Keeps, changes or removes it, and says what was given up | Makes a decision but names nothing given up | Makes no decision |
| The check | Names one check that someone else could repeat, such as a contrast ratio, the squint test or asking three students | The check is a feeling, such as "it feels better" | Names no check |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at most one part is "Partly".

The most common weak answer names a style instead of a part. "I removed the purple AI look" says nothing a mentor can check. "I removed the glow behind the headline, because it said nothing about printing" is a part, a reason and a decision. A second weak answer treats the lists from the two AI companies as rules of good design and removes everything on them. The lists are questions. A part with a reason can stay.

The follow-up question a mentor usually asks is: "What would have to be true for you to put that part back?" A good reply gives a condition you could check, such as "if three students could not find the price without it". You can answer by typing, or by dictating with your phone's microphone.

## Your answer

The learning log is for you, and it saves as you type. Write it before the open question, because your answers there often hold the default you will pick below. If your answer to question 1 was a glow or a row of cards, you probably have your part already. If you cannot think of a part you did not choose, look at your last page again. Ask the same question of each of its parts. If you made it with an AI tool in one evening, it probably has several. Finding them is not an insult to the page or to you. It is the first step of the next draft.

Choose one default from your own project, or from the Wasel landing page: the glow, the "AI-powered" pill, the headline, the three icon cards, or the "Get started" button. Write about it in five short parts. What was it doing on the page? Did you choose it, and what is the reason, or did you not choose it? What is your decision, and what do you give up by it? What one check will you make to see whether the decision was right?

Keep it honest. If the part was a default and you kept it anyway, say why. If you have not run the check, say how you would. An honest "I have not tested this yet" is worth more to a mentor than an invented number.
