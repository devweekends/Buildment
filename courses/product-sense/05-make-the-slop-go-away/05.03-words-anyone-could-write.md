## One test for every sentence

<MermaidDiagram title="The hundred products test" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Read one sentence"] --> T{"Could a hundred products say it?"}
  T -->|"Yes"| C["Cut it or replace it"]
  T -->|"No"| K["Keep it"]
  C --> R["Use a price, time, user action or trade-off"]
`} legend="The test looks at what the sentence tells the reader, not at which words it uses." />

Take any sentence on the page and ask one question: could a hundred different products say this? If yes, a customer learns nothing from it. Cut it, or replace it with something only this product can say. If no, keep it. The test does not look at which words you chose. It looks at what the sentence tells.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a test and not a word list">

The test works by swapping. Take a sentence from your page and change the product: printing becomes baking, a shop becomes a gym. If the sentence is still true and still reads well, a hundred products could say it, and the reader has learned nothing. A sentence that breaks when you swap, because it holds a price or a place, has something to say.

The rule is the course's own, and it agrees with what the two AI companies write. Anthropic's design skill asks for plain description of what a thing is or does, rather than selling it. OpenAI's guidance lists filler copy, repeated mood statements and aspirational headlines among the defaults to avoid. Its frontend prompt page also says not to put visible text on a screen that only describes the app's own features.

Notice that none of this bans a word. A word such as "fast" can be fine when a number stands behind it. "Ready by 4 pm if you send before noon" is fast, and a customer can check it. The same word with nothing behind it is the problem.

Evidence label: practice and a rule of thumb. Nobody measured the test. It is a way to ask, sentence by sentence, whether the reader gains anything.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort Wasel's sentences

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-hundred-products" />

Sort the sentences into the two buckets. Watch the tricky one: "Quality prints at great prices" has no hype words, yet a hundred shops could say it. A sentence with a price, a time or a place passes, even when it sounds plain. Each wrong try shows a reason, so use the reasons to learn the pattern.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: specific, and also complete">

Wasel's small grey line says "Ready by 4 pm". That is specific, because a student can check it. But it is not complete. Wasel's real rule is that orders sent before noon are ready by 4 pm. A student who sends a file at 1 pm may stand at the counter at 4 and wait. The full sentence, "Send before noon. It is ready by 4 pm", is the one on the canvas.

So a good sentence has two parts. It holds a fact that only this product can state, and it holds the condition that makes the fact true. A time without its condition makes a promise that Sami cannot always keep, and that costs him more than a vague line would.

Numbers need the same care. "30 to 40 orders a day in exam weeks" is the case's own figure, and it is useful because it explains why to send early. Use a number like that on a page only when it is true and you can say where it came from. An invented number is worse than a vague sentence, because a customer may believe it.

Evidence label: practice.

</GoDeeper>

## Four things make a sentence specific

<MermaidDiagram title="What replaces a vague sentence" chart={`flowchart TD
  V["A sentence a hundred products could say"] --> P["A price you can check"]
  V --> T["A time you can keep"]
  V --> U["A thing a user did"]
  V --> G["A thing you gave up"]
`} legend="Any one of the four is enough to turn a vague sentence into a specific one." />

Replace a vague sentence with one of four things. A price: 0.50 shekels a page. A time: ready by 4 pm if you send before noon. A thing a user did, from a real check. A thing you gave up: a limit you chose, such as "one bake a day, so we sell out by noon". A reader can check each one.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where each fact comes from">

You cannot invent these four things. They come from people and records.

A price comes from the owner. Ask Sami for the price list, and copy it exactly. A time comes from how the shop really works: ask what is true on a normal day, and what changes on a busy one. A thing a user did comes from a small check like the one in chapter 1.5. If three of five students sent a file in under a minute, say so. If you have not run the check, do not write the sentence. A page that tells a made-up story about users is not specific, it is false.

A thing you gave up is the most useful and the least common. It shows that you made a choice. In Ehab's fictional bakery it could read, "We bake once a day, at 7 am, so we sell out by noon." A customer learns when to come. On Razan's fictional tutoring page it could be, "Groups of at most four students, so everyone speaks." The sentence names what is missing and why, which a vague line never does.

Nothing here replaces the words of a resume or a profile. Those belong to course 16, and the same test helps there.

Evidence label: practice. The four replacements are the course's own list.

</GoDeeper>

## Labels and errors are copy too

<MermaidDiagram title="Vague words and what they could say" chart={`flowchart LR
  A1["Get started"] --> B1["Upload a file"]
  A2["Submit"] --> B2["Send my order"]
  A3["Invalid input"] --> B3["Choose black and white or colour"]
`} legend="Each vague label on the left becomes a line that says what happens or what to do." />

Button labels and error messages are words too. A button should say what happens next: "Upload a file" tells Nour what she will do, and "Get started" does not. An error should say what went wrong and what to do about it. "Invalid input" says neither. Every word on a screen is a choice, and a default label is a default.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: copy is part of the design">

Anthropic's design skill gives short examples of the same rule. It prefers "Save changes" to "Submit". Errors should say what went wrong and how to fix it. Empty states should invite an action. The idea is that every word on a screen is a design choice, and a default label is a default.

The error rules have a longer history. Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) advises putting an error near its cause and using plain language. It asks you to name the specific problem, offer a next step, avoid blaming the person and keep what they typed. GOV.UK's Design System, the one real product described in this course, gives templates for error messages, such as "Enter [whatever it is]". At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), its text input page also explains the reason for each rule.

On Wasel's order form, the message "Invalid input" appears at the top whatever is wrong. Each field needs its own message beside it: one for a missing file, one for the colour choice, one for the pickup time. Module 3 teaches how to place them. Here the point is that the words come first.

Evidence label: heuristic for the NN/g rules, practice for GOV.UK and Anthropic.

</GoDeeper>

## A banned word list goes out of date

<MermaidDiagram title="Why a word list dates" chart={`flowchart TD
  L["A list of overused words spreads"] --> D["Use of the listed words falls"]
  D --> R["Other words keep rising"]
  R --> O["The list is out of date"]
`} legend="Writers change, so a list of words is always a step behind." />

In studies of scientific papers, some words became much more common after AI chat tools appeared. After lists of them spread, use of one listed word fell, while another kept rising. So a word list dates fast. It also cannot tell you who wrote one sentence. Test the sentence, not the vocabulary. Then you never need to guess who wrote it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the word studies show">

One research team studied about 15.1 million abstracts of scientific papers, from 2010 to 2024. Style words such as "delves", "underscores" and "showcasing" became far more common in 2024 than earlier trends predicted. The authors estimate that at least 13.5 percent of 2024 abstracts were processed with AI language tools. They state their limits. The figure is a lower bound. It works only across a whole collection and cannot label a single abstract. It also cannot tell AI use from people picking up AI-style words.

Another team found no evidence that the structure of the models, their method or their training data explains the overuse of such words. They said the pattern was consistent with a training step that rewards answers people prefer. The cause is not settled. A third study, not yet reviewed by other scientists, found that people who used a chatbot briefly began to use its favourite words. So a person can use "delve" without an AI.

After the list was publicised in early 2024, use of "delve" in abstracts of papers posted online fell, while "significant" kept rising. The authors suggest that writers adapted. AI text also differs from human text in grammar as well as words: one study found more participial clauses and noun forms than people use.

Two things follow. First, do not try to detect AI writing by its words. These studies describe whole collections of text and cannot label one text. Second, some people say AI always writes in threes, such as "Fast. Secure. Smart." We found no measurement of that, so the course does not use it as a test. The problem with those three cards is not the count. It is that none of them holds a fact.

These studies are about scientific papers, not shop pages. No study of "seamless" or "powerful" in product copy was found.

Evidence label: research, with the limits above.

</GoDeeper>
