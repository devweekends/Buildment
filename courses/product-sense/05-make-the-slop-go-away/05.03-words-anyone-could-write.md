## One test for every sentence

<MermaidDiagram title="The hundred products test" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Read one sentence"] --> T{"Could a hundred products say it?"}
  T -->|"No"| K["Keep it"]
  T -->|"Yes"| F{"Do you have a real fact to put in?"}
  F -->|"Yes"| W["Replace it with the fact"]
  F -->|"No"| X["Cut it and leave nothing"]
`} />

Take any sentence on the page and ask one question: could a hundred different products say this? If yes, a customer learns nothing from it. Replace it with a real fact that only this product can state. If you have no true fact, cut the sentence. An invented fact is worse than a vague line.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a test and not a word list">

The test works by swapping. Take a sentence from your page and change the product: printing becomes baking, a shop becomes a gym. If the sentence is still true and still reads well, a hundred products could say it, and the reader has learned nothing. A sentence that breaks when you swap, because it holds a price or a place, has something to say.

The rule is the course's own, and it agrees with what the two AI companies write. Anthropic's design skill asks for plain description of what a thing is or does, rather than selling it. OpenAI's guidance lists filler copy, repeated mood statements and aspirational headlines among the defaults to avoid. Its frontend prompt page also says not to put visible text on a screen that only describes the app's own features.

Notice that none of this bans a word. A word such as "fast" can be fine when a number stands behind it. "Ready by 4 pm if you send before noon" is fast, and a customer can check it. The same word with nothing behind it is the problem.

Evidence label: practice and a rule from practice. Nobody measured the test. It is a way to ask, sentence by sentence, whether the reader gains anything.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort Wasel's sentences

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-hundred-products" />

Sort the sentences into the two buckets. The rule is the swap: change the product to a bakery and see whether the sentence still works. Digits do not decide it. A line with a digit can fail, and a plain line with a place or a condition can pass. Each wrong try shows a reason.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: specific, and also complete">

Wasel's small grey line says "Ready by 4 pm". That is specific, because a student can check it. But it is not complete. Wasel's real rule is that orders sent before noon are ready by 4 pm. A student who sends a file at 1 pm may stand at the counter at 4 and wait. The full sentence, "Send before noon and collect it the same afternoon", is the one on the canvas.

So a good sentence has two parts. It holds a fact that only this product can state, and it holds the condition that makes the fact true. A time without its condition makes a promise that Sami cannot always keep, and that costs him more than a vague line would.

Numbers need the same care. "30 to 40 orders a day in exam weeks" is the case's own figure, and it is useful because it explains why to send early. Use a number like that on a page only when it is true and you can say where it came from. An invented number is worse than a vague sentence, because a customer may believe it.

That is why "5 stars from our happy customers" fails even though it has a digit. There is no count, no source and no customer behind it.

Evidence label: practice.

</GoDeeper>

## Four things make a sentence specific

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-four-fixes" />

Replace a vague sentence with one of four things. A price: a loaf is 3 shekels. A time: out of the oven at 7 am. A thing customers did: 31 of 40 came before 8 am. A thing you gave up: one bake a day, so we sell out by noon. A reader can check each one.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where each fact comes from">

You cannot invent these four things. They come from people and records.

A price comes from the owner. Ask the owner for the price list, and copy it exactly. A time comes from how the shop really works: ask what is true on a normal day, and what changes on a busy one. A thing a user did comes from a small check like the one in chapter 1.5. If three of five students sent a file in under a minute, say so. If you have not run the check, do not write the sentence. A page that tells a made-up story about users is not specific. It is false.

A thing you gave up is the most useful and the least common. It shows that you made a choice. On Ehab's fictional bakery page it reads, "One bake a day, so we sell out by noon." A customer learns when to come. On Razan's fictional tutoring page it could be, "Groups of at most four students, so everyone speaks." The sentence names what is missing and why, which a vague line never does.

Nothing here replaces the words of a resume or a profile. Those belong to course 16, and the same test helps there.

Evidence label: practice. The four replacements are the course's own list.

</GoDeeper>

## Labels and errors are copy too

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m5-labels-errors" />

Switch between the two sets of words on the same form. The labels and the fields do not change. The title, the error and the button do. "Invalid input" says neither what is wrong nor what to do. A button should say what happens next. Every word on a screen is a choice, and a default word is a default.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: copy is part of the design">

Anthropic's design skill gives short examples of the same rule. It prefers "Save changes" to "Submit". Errors should say what went wrong and how to fix it. Empty states should invite an action. The idea is that every word on a screen is a design choice.

The error rules have a longer history. NN/g, the research company from chapter 1.5, advises putting an error near its cause and using plain language. It asks you to name the specific problem, offer a next step, avoid blaming the person and keep what they typed. GOV.UK's Design System, the one real product described in this course, gives templates for error messages, such as "Enter [whatever it is]". At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), its text input page also explains the reason for each rule.

Think of a form that shows one message at the top, whatever is wrong. Each field needs its own message beside it, so that the person knows which field to fix. Module 3 teaches how to place them. Here the point is that the words come first.

Evidence label: heuristic for the NN/g rules, practice for GOV.UK and Anthropic.

</GoDeeper>

## A banned word list goes out of date

<MermaidDiagram title="Why a word list dates" chart={`flowchart TD
  L["A list of overused words spreads"] --> D["Writers avoid the listed words"]
  D --> R["Other words rise instead"]
  R --> N["A new list is needed"]
  N --> L
`} />

In studies of scientific papers, some words became much more common after AI chat tools appeared. After lists of them spread, use of one listed word fell while another kept rising. A word list is always a step behind, and it cannot tell you who wrote one sentence. Test the sentence, not the vocabulary.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the word studies show">

One research team studied about 15.1 million abstracts of scientific papers, from 2010 to 2024. Style words such as "delves", "underscores" and "showcasing" became far more common in 2024 than earlier trends predicted. The authors estimate that at least 13.5 percent of 2024 abstracts were processed with AI language tools. They state their limits. The figure is a lower bound. It works only across a whole collection and cannot label a single abstract. It also cannot tell AI use from people picking up AI-style words.

Another team found no evidence that the structure of the models, their method or their training data explains the overuse of such words. They said the pattern was consistent with a training step that rewards answers people prefer. The cause is not settled. A third study, not yet reviewed by other scientists, found that people who used a chatbot briefly began to use its favourite words. So a person can use "delve" without an AI.

After the list was publicised in early 2024, use of "delve" in abstracts of papers posted online fell, while "significant" kept rising. The authors suggest that writers adapted. AI text also differs from human text in grammar as well as words: one study found more participial clauses and noun forms than people use.

Two things follow. First, do not try to detect AI writing by its words. These studies describe whole collections of text and cannot label one text. Second, some people say AI always writes in threes, such as "Fast. Secure. Smart." We found no measurement of that, so the course does not use it as a test. The problem with those three cards is not the count. It is that none of them holds a fact.

These studies are about scientific papers, not shop pages. No study of "seamless" or "powerful" in product copy was found.

Evidence label: research, with the limits above.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "An AI tool writes the top line for Hamza's juice stand: 'Fresh, healthy and delicious, every day.' Which rewrite passes the hundred products test?"
options:
  - "Over 5,000 happy customers choose our juice every year"
  - "Each cup is squeezed to order, so the line is slow at noon"
  - "Pure fruit, 100 percent natural, made with real care"
  - "The best juice in town, loved by students everywhere"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "A number sounds specific, but nothing says who counted or when. Any juice stand could print it, so it fails the test."
  - "Right. It states a limit the stand chose and what it costs the customer, and no other stand can say it. It has no digit and still passes."
  - "Every stand can say 'pure', 'natural' and 'real care'. The '100 percent' is a digit with nothing behind it."
  - "'The best' is a claim with no proof, and any stand could make it. The line names no price, time or place."
```
