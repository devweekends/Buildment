Mais opens three room-booking apps (made up for this chapter) and says, "App B is nicer." Walid, her study partner, asks one question: nicer at what? She has no answer yet. This chapter gives her a way to find one, using only what she can see and check.

## Collect the first screen of one task

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m4-same-task" />

Mais picks one task: book a group room for tomorrow at 4 pm. She opens the same task in three apps and keeps the first screen of each. Same task, same day. Switch between the apps and write only what you see, before you judge anything. Notice the fields, the words on the buttons, and what is largest.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: collecting well">

Pick one task that a real person does, and use the same task in every product. If the tasks differ, the differences you find belong to the tasks and not to the products. Three products are enough. Ten gives you more notes than you can compare.

Use the same phone, and work on the same day if you can. Write the date on every note, because products change without warning. Take a screenshot of each step of the task, not only the first screen. A first screen is a start. The task is the whole path to the result.

Choose products you can open without paying or signing up. Never type your own ID, your phone number or a classmate's details into a product you are only studying. If a product needs an account to show the next screen, write "needs an account" and stop there. That is also something you saw.

Keep your screenshots for your own study. Pictures of someone's product belong to them. If you publish your log, describe what you saw in words, and let readers open the product themselves.

On weak data, one small screenshot per step is enough. You can also write down what you see: the words on the button, the number of fields, what is largest.

</GoDeeper>

## A critique has three parts

<MermaidDiagram title="Three parts of a critique" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["What I see, with a date"] --> B["The rule it follows or breaks"] --> C["The evidence"]
`} legend="Evidence can be a measurement, a standard, or what the maker says, if the maker says anything." />

Write what you see, with the date. Write the rule it follows or breaks. Then give the evidence: a measurement, a standard, or what the maker says. These three parts are a critique, as in 4.2. It judges the screen against its goal, not the person who made it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: choosing rules you can name">

Write four rules before you open the products, so that you do not choose rules that favour the one you like. Each rule needs a source and a kind. Contrast of at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text is a standard [A1]. A label that stays visible above its field is a heuristic from NN/g and practice from GOV.UK [A25, A74]. Show only what helps the task is the eighth of the ten usability heuristics, and "minimalist" there means relevant, not empty [A67]. "Start with user needs" and "Do less" are principles that the UK government publishes, which is practice [A75].

Do not use a rule from the list of popular rules that fail a check. Chapter 4.5 gives that list. A critique built on the three-click rule uses a rule that failed its test, and a reader who knows the evidence will say so.

A heuristic evaluation is a critique against a named list, done by several people who each work alone first. A violation is not automatically a problem, because the right trade-off depends on the context [A66]. So write "breaks the rule" and then say what that costs the person doing the task. Rules help you find problems. Watching people confirms them.

</GoDeeper>

## A critique, an opinion and a guess

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m4-critique-or-opinion" />

Sort nine sentences from a critique log. A critique names what you see, a rule and proof. An opinion is a feeling about the look. A guess claims to know why the maker did something, and no one can see that on the screen. Only the first kind helps Mais decide what to change.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: turning opinions into critiques">

An opinion is not worthless. "It feels calm" may point at something real. The skill is to ask what you see that makes it feel calm. Perhaps there are only three lines on the first screen, and one button. That is a sentence a critique can use.

NN/g describes how a facilitator does this in a design critique. They turn an opinion such as "this is too red" into a question about the goal. For example: does the red help the person see the Join button? [A31]. You can do this to your own sentences.

"Make the Book button pop" becomes "On 9 Nov, the Book button is the same weight as three other buttons. The goal is that Book comes first." The second version tells the maker what to change.

A guess about the maker is different. Only the maker knows the reason. "App A asks for five fields because the owner wants data" may be true. You do not know it, and a log full of guesses teaches you to invent reasons. Never guess a company's reasons. Write what the screen shows and what it costs the person. If the maker has written a reason, quote it and name where you found it.

</GoDeeper>

## Date what you saw, leave out guesses

<MermaidDiagram title="What goes in the log" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Seen by you, with a date"] --> K1["Keep it"]
  D["Written by the maker"] --> K2["Keep it and name the source"]
  G["Guessed by you"] --> X["Leave it out"]
`} legend="Three kinds of note. Two belong in the log and one does not." />

Products change, so a note without a date goes stale. Keep what you saw, with the date. Keep what the maker wrote, and name where you found it. Leave out what you only guess. A note is safe when someone else could open the same screen and see the same thing.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: fairness and care">

If you did not open a product, do not describe it. A blog post or a video from last year may show screens that have changed. You may write what the post says, name the post and its date, and mark that you did not see it yourself.

Be fair to the maker. They had a goal and limits that you cannot see: a deadline, a small team, a rule from their company. Write about the screen and what it costs the person, and not about whether the people were careless. No mockery. A critique that sounds clever and unkind teaches nobody.

Write for a reader who may be the maker. If the maker read your line, would they know what to check? "The Book button is below the list, and the list can grow" is something they can check. "Bad layout" is not.

Last, keep a record of what you have not tested. If you could not try the error state because you had no account, say so. "Not tested" is an honest note, and it stops a reader from thinking you checked.

</GoDeeper>

## GOV.UK writes its reasons down

<MermaidDiagram title="A rule with its reasons" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["GOV.UK rule: keep the label visible above the input"] --> A["Reason: a placeholder disappears when you type"]
  R --> B["Reason: not every screen reader reads a placeholder"]
  R --> C["Reason: default placeholder styles often fail contrast"]
`} legend="The GOV.UK Design System text input page, checked on 2026-10-25, states the rule and three reasons." />

GOV.UK is one product that writes its reasons down. Its Design System says to keep the label above the input, and gives three reasons. When the maker says why, you can quote it and name where you found it. That is stronger than a reason you invent. Most makers say nothing, so you leave their reasons out.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what GOV.UK writes">

The GOV.UK Design System text input page says that all text inputs need labels, in most cases visible and above the input. It tells makers not to use placeholder text instead of a label, or for hints or examples. It gives the three reasons in the picture. It also gives templates for error messages, such as "Enter" followed by what is needed [A74].

The government design principles page lists eleven principles. The first is "Start with user needs", and one of them is "Do less". The page says it was last updated on 2 April 2025 [A75].

Both pages were opened on 2026-10-25. They are practice, which means a maker stating its own rules and reasons. They are not research, and the pages report no tests. Use them as one source among several. A good critique line might read: "9 Nov: App A has placeholder-only labels. GOV.UK keeps labels visible and says placeholders disappear when you type."

Notice what makes this line safe. The date and the screen are yours. The rule and the reason are GOV.UK's, with a name on them. Nothing is guessed. In 4.6 you write a whole log in this form for three products of your own choice.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Walid compares three apps. In App A he books a room. In App B he checks the balance of his library card. In App C he reads the opening hours. He writes: 'App B is the fastest.' What is the main problem with this comparison?"
options:
  - "He should have asked each maker why, since a critique needs the maker's reasons"
  - "He should have scored each app out of ten, since a number is easier to compare"
  - "He should have used five apps, since three are too few to see a pattern"
  - "He compared three different tasks, so the apps cannot be set against each other"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Makers rarely write their reasons, and guessing them is worse than leaving them out. When a maker has written a reason, you may quote it. A critique does not need it."
  - "A score is an opinion in a number. It does not say what Walid saw or which rule it follows."
  - "Three products are enough. The problem is not how many he opened but what he did in each one."
  - "Right. Booking, checking a balance and reading hours are different tasks. A difference in speed may come from the task and not from the app. Use one task in all three."
```
