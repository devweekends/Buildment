## What you make, and why it matters

You make a critique log: a short, dated record of three products that do the same task. Each line names what you see, the rule it follows or breaks, and the evidence. You also compare them, and say what to copy, what to avoid and which rule is weakest.

This is the first draft of part 2 of your capstone. In module 7 you will hand in a critique log about your own redesign. Each of those lines adds the change and what you gave up. The skill is the same: a line that a stranger can check. Learn it first on other people's products, where you have no pride and no choice of your own to defend.

Plan about 50 minutes. Everything fits on a phone. If you cannot open three products, the Wasel case below writes three order forms out in words, so you need no account.


The rules come before the lines, and the lines come before the conclusion. A conclusion without lines is an opinion, and lines without rules are only notes.

## The steps

1. **Pick one task and three products.**

Choose a task that a real person does in about two minutes, such as booking a room, ordering a print or joining a group. Choose three products that do the same task, and open them without paying or signing up. A difference between products only means something when the task is the same. Do not type your own ID, a phone number or a classmate's details into a product you are only studying.

2. **Write four rules before you look.**

Each rule needs a short name, its kind (standard, research, heuristic or practice) and its source. Take them from the Good and Best lists in chapter 4.2. Examples are contrast of 4.5 to 1, a label that stays visible, relevant content only, or one clear first thing when you squint. Rules written afterwards tend to favour the product you liked. Do not use a rule that failed a check in chapter 4.5.

3. **Collect the screens, with the date.**

Take two or three screens of the task from each product, on one phone and one day, and write the date on each. Write "needs an account" if the next step is locked, and stop there. Products change without warning, and a note without a date cannot be checked later.

4. **Write three lines per product.**

Each line has three parts: what you see, with the date; the rule it follows or breaks; and the evidence, which is a measurement, a standard, or something the maker wrote and where you found it. For contrast, find the grey in the table in 2.5, or work the ratio with the formula from 2.5. Write "not tested" where you could not check. If you find yourself writing "probably" or "they want", the sentence belongs outside the log.

5. **Compare, and place each product on the scale.**

For each rule, write one sentence on how the three products differ. Then place each product on the four-level scale for this task, as far as you can check, and write "cannot tell" where a check needs people. The scale makes you say what the screens cannot show you yet.

6. **Write a conclusion.**

Name one thing you would copy and one you would avoid, each with a reason. Then name the rule with the weakest source, and say what would make you drop it.

## A worked example on Halaqa

Maryam wants to improve how a student joins a study group, the job from module 1. Her task is "join a study group for an exam". On 9 November she opens three joining apps on her phone, with no account, and keeps their first two screens. The apps are made up: Lamma, a club sign-up app; Hadath, an events app; and Qaa Rooms, a library room page.

She writes four rules. Normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1, which is a standard. A label stays visible above its field, a heuristic from NN/g and a practice at GOV.UK (checked 2026-10-25). Show only what the job needs, the eighth of the ten usability heuristics. One clear first thing when you squint, a heuristic from NN/g. She first wrote a fifth rule: every page within three clicks.

Her mentor asked where the fifth rule came from. Maryam had read it in a blog and never checked it. In chapter 4.5 she had seen that it failed its test: a study of 44 people and 620 tasks found that success did not depend on click count. She had used it to give Qaa Rooms a lower mark for needing four taps. She dropped the rule and the mark, and judged Qaa Rooms again with the relevance rule. This was her one wrong choice, and the fix took ten minutes.

Her lines are specific. For Lamma she wrote: "9 Nov: the first screen has five fields that use placeholders only. This breaks the rule that a label stays visible. GOV.UK keeps labels visible and says placeholders disappear when you type." For Hadath: "9 Nov: the Join label is #999999 on white, which is 2.85 to 1 by the grey table in 2.5. Normal text needs 4.5 to 1 (a standard)." For Qaa Rooms: "9 Nov: the first screen lists only the rooms that are free tonight. This follows the rule to show only what the job needs. Not tested: what happens after I tap a room."

She was tempted to write that Hadath hides its prices to collect sign-ups. Then she saw that she had only seen a missing price, so she wrote that, with the date.

Her comparison says that Qaa Rooms comes closest to her job because tonight's rooms lead. She places Lamma and Hadath at Average. She places Qaa Rooms at Good on the checks she could verify, with "cannot tell" for the check with people. She would copy Qaa Rooms's idea of putting tonight first into Halaqa, and avoid Lamma's long form. Her weakest rule is the squint test, because it is a heuristic and not a measurement. She would drop it if three classmates found Join fast on a screen where two things were equally strong.

## The provided case: Wasel

If you have no project, or you cannot open three products, use Wasel. Wasel is a print and copy shop near the university gate, owned by Sami and open from 8 am to 6 pm. Black and white pages cost 0.50 shekels each, colour pages 2 shekels each, and binding 5 shekels. Orders sent before noon are ready by 4 pm. Sami's nephew Karam built the order app in one evening with an AI tool. The job: a student has an assignment due tomorrow morning and no printer, so they send the file from a phone and collect it printed on the way to class.

Your task is "send a file to be printed and choose when to collect it". You compare three order forms, all written out here in full. Use four rules. Normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 (a standard). A label stays visible above its field (a heuristic and practice). An error sits next to its cause, says what is wrong in words and says what to do next (a heuristic, and WCAG 3.3.1 asks for text). A finger target is about 44 to 48 pixels high, with 24 as the AA floor (a standard and design-system guidance).

Wasel's order form has four fields with placeholders only: "File", "Copies", "Colour?" and "Pickup time". The placeholder grey is #999999 on white, which is 2.85 to 1. It has no hints. The "Submit" button is 80 by 28 pixels. When something is wrong, the field shows a red border and nothing else, and one message at the top of the screen says "Invalid input".

Nuqta Copy is another shop, made up for this exercise. Its form has four fields, and every label sits above its field and stays visible. The pickup field has a hint: "Orders sent before noon are ready by 4 pm". Under the fields, a line shows the price as you change things. The text is #333333 on white, which is 12.6 to 1. One wide button, 48 pixels high, says "Send my order". If the time is too early, a message under the field says "Choose a time after 4 pm today".

Safha Print is a second shop, also made up. Its form has five steps, one question on each, with the line "Step 2 of 5". Every label is visible. The hint text is #999999 on white, which is 2.85 to 1. The Next button is 30 pixels high. No price is shown until step 5. If a field is empty, a red message under it says "This field is required".

Write nine lines, three for each form, using the same three parts. Take the date from today.

## Rubric

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same task, same rules | One task, three products, four rules written first, each with a kind and a source | Rules named, but two or fewer have both a kind and a source | Different tasks, or fewer than three rules |
| Three-part lines | Nine lines or more, each with a date, what you see, the rule and the evidence | Six lines with all three parts, or nine lines with a part missing in a few | Fewer than six full lines, or mostly opinions |
| Nothing guessed | No guess about a maker's reasons, nothing described unseen, no rule that failed in 4.5 | One slip that you marked or fixed | Two or more slips |
| Comparison and conclusion | Compares on two rules or more, copy and avoid with reasons, weakest rule named | Two of the three present | One of the three, or none |

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet, and at most one part is Partly.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor looks first at the dates and at whether each line has all three parts. A line with no date, or with a rule but no evidence, is the first one they mark. The common weak answer is a log of preferences ("App B is cleaner") or a log full of guesses about why a company did something. The follow-up question they usually ask is: which rule on your list has the weakest source, and what would make you drop it? You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a link to your screenshots.
