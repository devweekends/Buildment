This is the gate for module 4. The practice in chapter 4.6 produced your critique log, so this gate asks something different: how do you now think about a screen? Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

## Module 4 checklist

- [ ] I can place a screen on the four-level scale and name the first check it does not pass
- [ ] I can tell a default from a choice, and say why I keep or change each one
- [ ] I write critique lines with a date, what I see, the rule and the evidence, and I leave out guesses about a maker's reasons
- [ ] I check a popular design rule against its evidence before I repeat it
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 4.2 to 4.5, finished the practice in 4.6 and the quiz in 4.7
- [ ] I wrote my answer to the open question below

## Learning log

1. **Your old verdict:** Think of the last screen you called "fine" or "cheap" before this module. What would you say about it now, using the three questions or the scale?
2. **Your level:** Think of a screen you made. Which is the first check it does not pass, and how do you know?
3. **Your default:** Name one default you keep on purpose, with a reason that would convince a stranger. If you cannot name one, name one you will check this week.
4. **Your rule:** Which design rule did you believe that failed a check? What will you say the next time someone repeats it?

## What changed in how you judge

Before this module, Huda and Saeed could only say "fine" and "cheap". By now you have three tools that they did not have. The first is the scale. It turns "is it good?" into smaller questions that you can answer with yes or no, and it tells you which check to fix first. The second is the difference between a default and a choice. It lets you keep a good default without feeling that you copied, and it makes you look again at the defaults that nobody chose. The third is the critique line, with a date, what you see, a rule and the evidence. It keeps your feelings and your guesses out of the log.

There is also a habit under all three: you ask where a claim comes from. In chapter 4.5 several popular rules failed their test or were misread. That does not mean that every design rule is false. It means that a rule with a source can be checked, and a rule without one cannot. Your own rules now carry a kind and a source.

The scale has limits. It cannot tell you whether the job is the right one, because module 1 is for that. It cannot grade the top level, because you defend that in words. It cannot replace watching three to five people try your screen. Where a check needs people, "cannot tell yet" is an honest answer.

## Your answer: a reply to Huda and Saeed

Huda said a screen looks fine, and Saeed said it looks cheap. Neither gave a reason, so you write back. Choose one screen from your own project, or use Wasel's order form. Write the reply as a short message to the two of them. It should do four jobs. It says what level you give the screen and which check it fails first, with the rule and the evidence. It names one default you would keep or change, with a reason. It names one popular rule that you will not use to judge it, and what that rule failed. It says what only people can tell you, and how you will find out.

If you have no project, use Wasel's order form. Wasel is a print and copy shop near the university gate. The job is to send a file from a phone and collect it printed on the way to class. The form has four fields with placeholders only: "File", "Copies", "Colour?" and "Pickup time". The placeholder grey is #999999 on white, which is 2.85 to 1. It has no hints and one button labelled "Submit", which is 80 by 28 pixels. When something is wrong, the field gets a red border, and one message at the top says "Invalid input".

For contrast on your own screen, find the grey in the table in 2.5, or work the ratio with the formula from 2.5. Both work on a phone. If a check needs a tool you do not have, write "not tested" and say so. An honest "not tested" is better than a number you did not measure.

Write for two readers who like each other and disagree. Keep each sentence plain. Point at things on the screen that they can open and look at. Do not say who was right, and do not blame the maker. A reply that sounds clever and unkind teaches nobody. Read your message once before you send it, and cut any sentence that only says how you feel.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your reply with the table below. Your screen can be anything. What counts is whether the checks are there and whether you can show them.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level and first failing check | A level, the first check not passed, its rule and its kind | A level and a failing check, but no rule or kind | A level with no check, or a feeling as the reason |
| Evidence | A measurement or a thing you saw, with what you did not test | Evidence for the check, but untested checks not marked | No evidence, or a guess about a maker's reasons |
| Default and rule | One default with a keep or change reason, and one dropped rule with why it failed | One of the two, with a reason | Neither, or a reason like "it looks good" |
| Limits | One thing only people can tell you, and how you will find out | Names a limit but not how to find out | Claims the screen is proven good |

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet, and at most one part is Partly.

After you submit, the mentor replies with one or two short questions. The most usual one is: which check are you least sure of, and how would you find out? A good reply points at something you can show or measure. A weak reply says you think it is fine. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a link to your screenshots.
