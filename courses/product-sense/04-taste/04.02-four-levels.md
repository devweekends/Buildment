Huda and Saeed need a shared way to say what they see. Their <GlossaryTerm term="taste" definition="The skill of telling better work from worse work, and saying why." /> can grow, and the scale below gives it checks to answer.

## Each level adds checks to the one below

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m4-level-ladder" />

Drag along Omar's printer page. Average works when nothing goes wrong, with the defaults unchanged. Good passes the standards from modules 2 and 3. Best puts one clear first thing on the page and cuts what the job does not need. It has also been tried by three to five people, with the problems they found fixed. Remarkable adds one choice that fits the job, with a reason.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the checks at every level">

Every check below comes from a source, and each source has a kind. A standard is a published requirement. Research is a study with a stated method. A heuristic, as in 2.2, is a rule from practice that often helps but does not always hold. Practice is advice from a person or a design system, with no stated test. No source defines the four level names. The scale is this course's own method, built from checks that are each sourced.

Average has two checks: it works when nothing goes wrong, and most defaults are unchanged. Both are the course's own observation. The Tailwind CSS and Bootstrap documents show what their defaults are.

Good is where the standards live. Normal text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1. Large text and meaningful parts of the interface have at least 3 to 1 (standards). Every field has a label that stays visible. A standard asks for labels or instructions, and the advice to keep them visible is a heuristic and practice. Everything works with a keyboard and focus is visible (standards). Targets are about 44 to 48 CSS pixels, and the AA floor is 24 with spacing (a standard and design-system guidance). Empty, loading and error states each say what is happening (heuristics).

Best asks for more. When you squint at the screen, one thing comes first (a heuristic). Only content that helps the task is on the page (a heuristic). Spacing follows one step and its multiples (practice). An error keeps what the person typed and says what to do next (a heuristic). Three to five people try the task, you fix what stops them, and you try again. This is a heuristic. It finds problems and does not prove a design is good.

Remarkable adds one thing no source can give you: a choice that fits this job, and a reason a reader can name. It is judgment. You defend it in words, and no test grades it.

</GoDeeper>

## Polished is not the same as good

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m4-polish-or-pass" />

Switch between the three versions of Rand's form. Polished has pale grey text, labels that vanish and a small button, so it fails three Good checks. Plain passes them. Clear also puts one thing first and drops what the job does not need. Looks are not a check.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why polish misleads">

People tend to see an attractive design as easier to use than it is. This is called the <GlossaryTerm term="aesthetic-usability effect" definition="People judge an attractive design as easier to use than it really is, which can hide problems." />. NN/g describes a 1995 study of 26 cash-machine screens with 252 people. Ratings of beauty matched perceived ease of use more than actual ease of use. The study is described second-hand, so treat it as a heuristic, not as proof.

The lesson is not that looks do not matter. A calm, clear screen is worth making. The lesson is that looks cannot take the place of the checks. When Huda says "it looks fine", she may be seeing real care, or she may be seeing polish. The scale asks her to check first.

The reverse is also true. A plain screen can be at Good. Plain is not a failure, and it is often the right place to start.

</GoDeeper>

## Place each claim on the scale

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m4-four-levels" />

Each card states facts about a screen. Put it at the highest level whose checks the facts show are met. One failed check keeps a screen at the level below, even after a test with people. Remarkable cards name a choice and a reason. The canvas checks only that a reason is stated. You judge whether it is a good one.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to place a screen">

Start at the bottom and move up. Ask whether every Average check is met, then every Good check, then every Best check. The first check that is not met sets the level. A screen that passes everything in Good except one check is at Average, not at "almost Good". A test with people does not repair a failed standard.

Some checks need a person, so "I cannot tell yet" is a real answer. Rand's clear form from the picture passes every Good check and shows one thing first when blurred. Nobody has tried it, so it stays at Good until three to five people have used it and Rand has fixed what stopped them.

Use the scale on work you can see, not on people. A screen can be at Average today and reach Best next week. It also does not say whether the job itself is right. Module 1 is for that. A page at Best that solves the wrong problem is still the wrong page.

</GoDeeper>

## A top-level choice needs a reason

<MermaidDiagram title="Choice or feeling" chart={`flowchart TD
  C["The choice: seats left is the largest text"] --> Q{"Take it away. Does the person lose something?"}
  Q -->|"Yes, and I can name it"| R["A reason: write it down"]
  Q -->|"No, I only like it"| F["A feeling: not a reason"]
`} />

A remarkable screen is Best plus a choice that fits the job. Take the choice away and ask what gets worse for the person. If you can name it, you have a reason. Then point at the evidence, such as a small check where people found the seats fast. No answer key can grade this level. You defend it in words, and a reader may disagree.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: defending the top level">

A good reason points at the job from module 1. For Halaqa the job is to find two or three classmates to revise with tonight or tomorrow. Seats left decide whether a student can join, so the number leads. A weak reason would be "big numbers look impressive". The first can be checked by watching a student find the seats. The second cannot.

If you can only say "it looks modern" or "I like it", you have a feeling. Say what the person would lose without the choice.

Most work does not need the top level. Stopping at Good or at Best is a fine result, and a screen is never worse for lacking a bold choice. The top level matters when you want a stranger to remember your work, which module 7 covers. Chapter 4.3 shows how a default differs from a choice.

</GoDeeper>

## Taste grows by making and critiquing

<MermaidDiagram title="How taste grows" chart={`flowchart TD
  M["Make a small thing"] --> C["Critique it against its goal"]
  C --> F["Fix one thing"]
  F --> S["Finish it and show it"]
  S --> M
`} />

You can often see what is good before you can make it. That is normal. What helps is to make many small things, check each against its goal, fix one thing and finish. A <GlossaryTerm term="critique" definition="Judging a design against its goal, with reasons you can check. It judges the design, not the person." /> tied to a goal teaches more than a verdict on the person.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the taste gap and critique">

Ira Glass, a radio producer, said in a talk that beginners usually have good taste. Their early work is not yet as good as what they can see. Many people stop at that point. His advice, as we paraphrase it, is to make a large amount of work and to set deadlines that force you to finish. This is a talk, not a study (practice).

A design critique analyses a design against its objectives. It does not judge the designer. In a studio there is a presenter, a critiquer and a facilitator. A good facilitator turns an opinion such as "this is too red" into a question about the goal. For example: does the red help the person see the Join button? Agree the goal before you start.

A heuristic evaluation is a critique against a named list of rules, and several people do it alone first. The scale in this chapter is the list. Chapter 4.4 teaches you to write the critique itself.

</GoDeeper>
