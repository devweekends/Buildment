In a review, Omar's lead says, "Keep every page within three clicks. It is a known rule." Omar cannot say if it is true, so he decides to check it. Judging evidence is part of taste.

## Ask three questions before you repeat a rule

<MermaidDiagram title="Check a rule first" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q1["Who says it, and what did they test?"] --> Q2["Does that test match my case?"]
  Q2 --> Q3["Is the evidence mixed or revised?"]
  Q3 --> D["Repeat it, qualify it or drop it"]
`} legend="Three questions turn a popular rule into a claim you can check." />

A rule is a claim. Before you repeat it, ask who says it and what they tested. Ask whether that test matches your case. Ask whether the evidence is mixed, or was revised later. Then repeat the rule, qualify it or drop it. Your critique log in 4.6 may only use rules that pass these questions.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: kinds of evidence">

Rules come with different kinds of backing. A standard is a published requirement, such as the WCAG contrast rule [A1]. Research is a study with a stated method and sample. A heuristic is a practical rule from experts, useful for finding problems and not proof of anything. Practice is advice from a maker or a design system.

A rule can be popular and have no backing at all. It can have a study behind it that was small, old or about something else. It can also be a true finding that people stretched to a case it never tested.

When you meet a rule, look for the words that show its kind. "Studies show" asks: which study, how many people, doing what? "Experts say" is a heuristic. "Always" and "never" are warnings: few design results hold without exceptions.

Sources can also change. A rule that was fine in 2009 may have been revised since. In this chapter every claim was checked against the pages opened on 2026-10-25, and the labels describe those pages only.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort nine popular rules

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m4-rule-or-myth" />

Nine rules you may have heard. A supported rule has a standard, a study or a written practice behind it. A misread rule has a true core used in the wrong place. An unsupported rule failed its test, has mixed evidence, or was dropped by the person who first drew it. A wrong try tells you why.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: more rules that fail a check">

"Users do not scroll" is wrong as stated. In a 2018 eye-tracking study of 120 people, 57 percent of viewing time was above the fold (visible without scrolling). 74 percent was in the first two screens. People scrolled when they had a reason (research) [A53]. Attention is high at the top, so put the important thing there.

"Dark mode is better for your eyes" is not supported as a general claim. NN/g's review found that for people with normal vision, light mode gave better visual performance most of the time. A few small studies suggest some people with cataract-related conditions do better in dark mode. NN/g says to let users choose, and says dark mode might extend battery life but gives no measurements (heuristic) [A65].

"Add a product tour" is a weak default. NN/g reports that tutorials frequently do not perform well in its studies, and that help shown when the person needs it works better (heuristic) [A62].

"A social network found that 7 friends in 10 days is the magic number" is folklore. A writer at an analytics company described such numbers as round figures in a range and not scientific thresholds, and no first-hand source was found (practice) [A73].

"The five-second test proves a design works" overclaims. It measures a first impression on simple screens, and its five seconds rest largely on anecdote [A71]. "Minimalism means fewer pixels" misreads heuristic 8, which is about relevance to the task [A67]. "Good looking means good to use" is risky, as chapter 4.2 showed [A58].

</GoDeeper>

## A true finding, used in the wrong place

<MermaidDiagram title="A finding used where it was not tested" chart={`flowchart LR
  M["1956: people hold about seven chunks in memory"] --> X["Applied to menus, which it never tested"] --> N["NN/g: large menus can work"]
`} legend="The memory finding is real. The rule about menus is a misreading." />

A paper from 1956 is about how much information people hold in short-term memory at once, about seven <GlossaryTerm term="chunks" definition="Groups of items that a person remembers as one unit." />. It did not test menus. NN/g found that large menus can work, on qualitative evidence. The finding is real. The rule "seven items in a menu" is a misreading of it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: misreadings and the F-pattern">

A misread rule has a true core. The memory paper is a real finding about immediate memory span. It says nothing about how long a menu should be [A55]. NN/g reports that mega menus can work well on large sites, on qualitative evidence, which means observation and not measurement (heuristic) [A83].

The F-pattern is another misreading. NN/g describes it as the way people scan text-heavy pages that give no strong cues, and calls it bad for users. The advice is to front-load key points, start headings with informative words, and cut unnecessary content [A68]. So "lay out your page to follow the F-pattern" turns a failure into a goal.

The five-user rule is a third. The finding is real, and the next beat shows its limits.

A good habit: when a rule has a number in it, find where the number came from. Seven, three and five all have origins. Each origin is narrower than the rule that grew from it.

</GoDeeper>

## Evidence can be mixed or later revised

<MermaidDiagram title="Evidence that was revised" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["2013: 1,333 observations of how people hold phones"] --> B["Thumb reach maps spread from it"]
  B --> C["2017: the author says to ignore the reach diagrams"]
`} legend="Evidence can be revised by the person who produced it." />

A 2013 article counted 1,333 observations of people using phones. Thumb reach maps spread from it. Later the author said the diagrams are unreliable and should be ignored, because people touch the centre of the screen most. The golden ratio and "blue means trust" have mixed or context-dependent evidence.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: thumb maps, the golden ratio and blue">

The phone study counted 1,333 observations, and 780 of them involved touching the screen. Among those 780, people held the phone with one hand in 49 percent, cradled it in 36 percent and used two hands in 15 percent. The observations are real. The reach diagrams drawn from them are not reliable, and the author said so in a 2017 comment. This is research with low confidence, because it is observational and its author revised it [A59].

The golden ratio is a number, about 1.618, that is said to make shapes beautiful. A 2002 article traces an experiment from the 1860s that was later challenged, and later work that was mixed. It says the ratio should not be treated as a universal standard of beauty (practice, from a popular science article) [A56].

"Blue means trust" is context dependent. A 2015 review of colour research says that effects depend on context, that the field is young, and that applying its results as design advice is premature. Some brand studies link blue with trust [A57].

None of these means the opposite is true. It means you cannot cite them as rules. Use contrast, labels and checks with people, which have standards or tests behind them.

</GoDeeper>

## Even good evidence has limits

<MermaidDiagram title="What five people can tell you" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Five people, one group, one round"] --> B["About 85 percent of that group's problems"]
  B --> C["Fix them, then test again with new people"]
  C --> D["Different groups need more people"]
`} legend="The five-user model finds problems round by round. It does not prove a design is good." />

A well-known model says five people find about 85 percent of the problems that one group of users meets in a round. It says to fix them and test again. Different groups need more people. The check finds problems. It does not prove a design is good, so "five users find all problems" is a misreading.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the five-user model and the five-second test">

The model says the share of problems found by n people is 1 minus (1 minus L) to the power n. Here L is the share that one person finds. In the authors' projects L was about 31 percent, and five people found about 85 percent. The article recommends several small rounds, about three rounds of five, over one large round. Fixes may not work, so you test again. One person can be unrepresentative, so use at least three. Two different user groups need three or four people each, and three or more groups need three each (heuristic) [A20].

In chapter 1.5 you planned a check with three to five people. Now you know its limits. Keep the finding: you will see the biggest problems quickly. Drop the claim that it finds everything.

The five-second test shows a design for five seconds, hides it, and asks what the person took away. The idea is credited to a design researcher in the mid-2000s. It tests a first impression, mostly of simple screens. For a complex page, ten seconds may work better. It is practice, resting largely on anecdote [A71].

A fair summary for your log: cheap checks find problems and are worth doing, and none of them proves that a design is good.

</GoDeeper>

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Rand's lead says: 'Keep every menu to seven items. A famous paper proved it.' What is the best reply?"
options:
  - "That paper covers memory, not menus, so test the menu with people"
  - "That paper settled it, so every menu should be cut to seven items"
  - "That paper is old, so none of its findings can be trusted today"
  - "That paper studied seven people, so the rule needs a larger group"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. The paper is a real finding about how many chunks people hold in short-term memory. It never tested menus, so a menu is checked with people who use it."
  - "The paper did not study menus, so it cannot have settled their length. This repeats the misreading."
  - "Age is not the problem. The finding about memory is still sound, and the fault is in applying it to menus."
  - "The number seven is the span of items people remember, not the number of people in the study. The reply invents a fact."
```
