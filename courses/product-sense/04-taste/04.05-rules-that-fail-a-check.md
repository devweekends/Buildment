In a review, Omar's lead says, "Keep every page within three clicks. It is a known rule." Omar cannot say if it is true, so he decides to check it. Judging evidence is part of taste.

## Ask three questions before you repeat a rule

<MermaidDiagram title="Check a rule first" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["A popular rule"] --> B{"Was it tested?"}
  B -->|"No"| X["Unsupported: drop it"]
  B -->|"Yes"| C{"Does the test match my case?"}
  C -->|"No"| M["Misread: qualify it"]
  C -->|"Yes"| D{"Mixed or revised since?"}
  D -->|"Yes"| X
  D -->|"No"| S["Supported: repeat it"]
`} />

A rule is a claim. Ask whether anyone tested it, whether the test matches your case, and whether the evidence was mixed or revised later. A rule that fails the first or the last question is Unsupported. A real finding used where it was not tested is Misread. Your critique log in 4.6 may only use rules that pass these questions, so ask them before you write a line.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: kinds of evidence">

Rules come with different kinds of backing. A standard is a published requirement, such as the WCAG contrast rule. Research is a study with a stated method and sample. A heuristic, as in 2.2, is a rule from practice, useful for finding problems and not proof of anything. Practice is advice from a maker or a design system.

A rule can be popular and have no backing at all. It can have a study behind it that was small, old or about something else. It can also be a true finding that people applied to a case it never tested.

When you meet a rule, look for the words that show its kind. "Studies show" asks: which study, how many people, doing what? "Experts say" is a heuristic. "Always" and "never" are warnings: few design results hold without exceptions.

Sources can also change. A rule that was fine in 2009 may have been revised since. In this chapter every claim was checked against the pages opened on 2026-10-25, and the labels describe those pages only.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort nine popular rules

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m4-rule-or-myth" />

Supported: a standard, a study or written practice backs the rule as stated. Misread: a real finding used where it was not tested. The seven in "seven items" is memory span, not menus. Unsupported: no test backs the rule as stated. It failed, the evidence is mixed, or its author took it back. A wrong try tells you the rule that separates the buckets. Digits do not decide the bucket.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: misreadings and the F-pattern">

A misread rule has a true core. A paper from 1956 is a real finding about how much information people hold in short-term memory at the same time, about seven <GlossaryTerm term="chunks" definition="Groups of items that a person remembers as one unit." />. It says nothing about how long a menu should be. NN/g reports that mega menus can work well on large sites, on qualitative evidence, which means observation and not measurement (heuristic).

The F-pattern is another misreading. NN/g describes it as the way people scan text-heavy pages that give no strong cues, and calls it bad for users. The advice is to front-load key points, start headings with informative words, and cut unnecessary content. So "lay out your page to follow the F-pattern" turns a failure into a goal.

The five-user rule is a third, and the picture below shows its limits.

A good habit: when a rule has a number in it, find where the number came from. Seven, three and five all have origins. Each origin is narrower than the rule that grew from it.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: thumb maps, the golden ratio and blue">

A 2013 article counted 1,333 observations of people using phones, and 780 of them involved touching the screen. Among those 780, people held the phone with one hand in 49 percent, cradled it in 36 percent and used two hands in 15 percent. Thumb reach maps spread from it. The author said in 2017 that the diagrams are unreliable and should be ignored, because people touch the centre of the screen most. This is research with low confidence, because it is observational and its author revised it.

The golden ratio is a number, about 1.618, that is said to make shapes beautiful. A 2002 article traces an experiment from the 1860s that was later challenged, and later work that was mixed. It says the ratio should not be treated as a universal standard of beauty (practice, from a popular science article). That is why it is Unsupported: no test backs the rule as stated.

"Blue means trust" is context dependent. A 2015 review of colour research says that effects depend on context, that the field is young, and that applying its results as design advice is premature. Some brand studies link blue with trust. No test backs the general rule, so it is Unsupported.

None of these means the opposite is true. It means you cannot cite them as rules. Use contrast, labels and checks with people, which have standards or tests behind them.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: more rules that fail a check">

"Users do not scroll" is wrong as stated. In a 2018 eye-tracking study of 120 people, 57 percent of viewing time was above the fold (visible without scrolling). 74 percent was in the first two screens. People scrolled when they had a reason (research). Attention is high at the top, so put the important thing there.

"Dark mode is better for your eyes" is not supported as a general claim. NN/g's review found that for people with normal vision, light mode gave better visual performance most of the time. A few small studies suggest some people with cataract-related conditions do better in dark mode. NN/g says to let users choose, and says dark mode might extend battery life but gives no measurements (heuristic).

"Add a product tour" is a weak default. NN/g reports that tutorials frequently do not perform well in its studies, and that help shown when the person needs it works better (heuristic).

"A social network found that 7 friends in 10 days is the magic number" is folklore. A writer at an analytics company described such numbers as round figures in a range and not scientific thresholds, and no first-hand source was found (practice).

"The five-second test proves a design works" overclaims. It measures a first impression on simple screens, and its five seconds come largely from anecdote. "Minimalism means fewer pixels" misreads heuristic 8, which is about relevance to the task. "Good looking means good to use" is risky, as chapter 4.2 showed.

</GoDeeper>

## Testers overlap, so each adds less

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m4-five-testers" />

In the model, one tester finds about 31 percent of one group's problems. Testers meet the same problems, so each new one adds less. The article says five find about 85 percent. The formula gives 84.4, so the picture shows 84. After twelve testers the bar barely moves at all. The check finds problems. It does not prove a design is good, and it covers one group only.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the formula">

The model says the share of problems found by n people is 1 minus (1 minus L) to the power n. Here L is the share that one person finds. In the authors' projects L was about 31 percent. With L at 0.31, one person finds 31 percent, two find 52.4, three find 67.1, five find 84.4 and twelve find 98.8 percent. The article rounds the five-person figure to about 85 percent (heuristic).

This is why "five users find all the problems" is a misreading. The figure is a share of one comparable group's problems in one round, and the curve never reaches all of them.

</GoDeeper>

## Fix, then test again with new people

<MermaidDiagram title="Rounds of testing" chart={`flowchart TD
  T["Test with 3 to 5 people from one group"] --> F["Fix what they found"]
  F --> N["Test again with new people"]
  N --> Q{"Big new problems?"}
  Q -->|"Yes"| F
  Q -->|"No"| D["Stop, or test another group"]
`} />

The article recommends several small rounds, about three rounds of five people, over one large round. Fixes may not work, so you test again with new people. Use at least three testers, because one tester can be unrepresentative. Two different user groups need three or four people each. In chapter 1.5 you planned a check with three to five people; this is its limit.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: limits, and the five-second test">

The article's limits are part of the finding. Fixes may not work, so iterate. One user can be unrepresentative, so use at least three. Two distinct user groups need three or four people each, and three or more groups need three each (heuristic).

In chapter 1.5 you planned a check with three to five people. Now you know its limits. Keep the finding: you will see the biggest problems quickly. Drop the claim that it finds everything.

The five-second test shows a design for five seconds, hides it, and asks what the person took away. The idea is credited to a design researcher in the mid-2000s. It tests a first impression, mostly of simple screens. For a complex page, ten seconds may work better. It is practice, based largely on anecdote.

A fair summary for your log: cheap checks find problems and are worth doing, and none of them proves that a design is good.

</GoDeeper>
