Maryam is not sure about the screen where a student starts a new study group in Halaqa. It is late and no mentor is awake. She opens three free AI chat tools in three tabs. She gives each one the same screenshot and the same question: what is wrong with this screen?

## Three tools give three different fixes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m6-scene" />

Tool one says four fields are too many, so keep two. Tool two says the grey hint text is too light. Tool three says to add a purple header and rename the button "Get started". Tap each version to see the screen it would make. All three answers are invented for this case.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the rest of Maryam's evening">

Maryam reads the three answers twice. Each one is written in a calm, sure voice. None says "I am not sure". Each one lists its fixes as a short, tidy list, so each looks like it could be right.

She cannot do all three. Tool one removes the Where field, and the hint that tool two wants to change lives inside that field. Tool three renames the button, and the other two do not mention the button at all. Even before she thinks about which advice is good, the advice does not fit together.

She sees three easy ways out. She could follow the tool she likes best. She could follow the answer in the first tab, because it came first. She could do the fix that takes the least time. She notices that none of these is a reason. Each one only picks an answer without asking whether the answer is true.

She could also ask a fourth tool to choose between the three. But that would be a fourth opinion, and she would be in the same place with one more voice. So she waits. She writes down the one question she wants answered before she touches the screen: how can I tell which of these points is true?

The rest of the module answers that question. It is a question about checking, not about trusting.

</GoDeeper>

## Who decides when the tools disagree?

<MermaidDiagram title="Three answers, one person" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Tool one: keep two fields"] --> M["Maryam: which one?"]
  B["Tool two: darker hints"] --> M
  C["Tool three: purple header"] --> M
  M --> Q["What is the reason?"]
`} legend="Three answers arrive at one person. The answers do not say which one is right. A reason has to come from somewhere else." />

Each answer sounds sure. Choosing the tool she likes best, or the answer that came first, is not a reason. This module teaches a better way: check each point, then decide and say why.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why this matters for your work">

Anyone can paste a screenshot into a chat tool and get a list of fixes in seconds. That is no longer a skill. What stays rare is the person who can say: "I asked three tools. I checked this point with a number. I checked that one by watching three classmates. I took one fix and left two, and here is why."

That sentence is the difference between using an AI opinion and being led by it. It also keeps your work honest. If a client, a mentor or a reviewer asks why the screen looks the way it does, "the AI said so" is not an answer. A checked reason is.

This module connects to the rest of the course. In module 5 you learned to ask whether each element of a page was chosen. In this module, the tool is one more voice that suggests elements, and you ask the same question of it. Your critique log in the capstone needs at least one AI point that you checked and one that you rejected with a reason. The practice chapter in this module makes that log entry.

One habit comes from course 13 and applies here too: never paste a client's or employer's screens or data into an AI tool without permission. This module uses Maryam's own app, and the practice uses your own project or the provided Wasel case.
