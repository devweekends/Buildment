## What you make

You make a three-opinion critique of one screen. It is a short table with one row for every point the tools raised. The columns are: the point, which tool said it, the check you ran, the result, your decision, and what you give up. Under the table you write a few sentences on where the tools disagreed and what you did not check.

This output matters for the capstone. In chapter 7.6 you hand in a critique log with at least one AI point you checked and accepted, and one you rejected with a reason. This chapter makes those entries. The method is the course's inference from research, so your notes should say what you checked and what you did not.

Plan about 50 minutes: 10 to set up and ask, 25 to check, 15 to write.

## The steps

```mermaid

  S["One screen + one rubric"] --> T["Three tools, same prompt"]
  T --> P["All points in one table"]
  P --> C["Check: rule, person or drop"]
  C --> D["Decision + reason + what you give up"]
  D --> L["Critique log"]
  L --> K["Capstone part 1 (7.6)"]
```

**1. Pick one screen and write the rubric first.** Choose a screen a person must finish, such as a form. Write the person, the device and the job in one sentence. Then write five checks: what is seen first, text size and contrast, space and grouping, labels and error words, and the words on buttons. Write them before you read any answer, or you will pick checks that make the answers look right.

**2. Choose three tools from different companies, and check each one opens for you.** At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), three examples are Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude. Gemini and ChatGPT are for people 13 or over, with a guardian's permission under 18 for ChatGPT. Claude is for people 18 or over. If only two open, make the third opinion your own pass with a paper checklist, labelled as yours. If none opens, use the Wasel route.

**3. Make the screenshot safe.** Use your own app, or a screen you have permission to share, and remove names, phone numbers and private data. Never paste a client's or employer's screens or data into an AI tool without permission; course 13 teaches the full habit. Send the screenshot at full size, because a button looks smaller in a small picture.

**4. Ask each tool the same prompt, in a fresh chat.** Use the prompt from chapter 6.3, with your sentence and your five checks. It ends with "no score, no verdict". Save each answer under the tool's name before you read the next, so the first answer does not change how you read the rest.

**5. Put every point in one table.** Merge repeats and note which tools said each point. Mark where the tools disagree. A disagreement often shows a matter of taste, and taste cannot be measured.

**6. Sort each point and run the check.** Choose: check a rule, ask a person, or drop. For a rule point, measure and write the number. On a phone, find the grey in the table of eight greys in 2.5, or work the formula by hand as 6.3 shows. Read a button's size from your code. For a person point, write the question, who you will watch and what you will look for. If nobody is free today, mark the decision "test later". For a dropped point, write one line: it names no problem.

**7. Decide, and write what you give up.** Each decision is change, leave or test later. Write the reason and the cost. Cutting a field gives up its data, and a taller button gives up space.

**8. Make the changes that passed a check, and look again.** Open the screen in a browser. A fix that only exists in your notes is not a fix. Then write the sentences under the table.

## Worked example: Maryam's create-group screen

Maryam uses the screen from 6.1: the form where a student starts a new group. It has four labelled fields (Course, When, Where, Seats) and a Start the group button. The hint under Where reads "Library, online or cafe" in grey #767676. Her sentence: a student, on a phone, the night before an exam, wants to start a group in under a minute. She writes her five checks.

Her first try was a mistake. She asked three tools for a score from 1 to 10 and got 6, 8 and 7. She could not say what an 8 meant, and she had started to treat the 8 as the winner, which lets a number decide. She deleted the scores and started again with the rubric, a request for evidence and "no score, no verdict".

The second round gave her eight distinct points once she merged the repeats. Four named a rule. The tools said the grey hint under Where was too light. She worked the formula: #767676 on white is 4.54 to 1, and normal text needs 4.5 to 1. Decision: leave it, and note not to make the grey lighter. All three tools said the Start the group button was too small. She read its size in her code: 44 pixels tall and as wide as the screen, inside the aim of 44 to 48. The three tools agreed, and all three were wrong. She had sent the screenshot at 40% of its size to save data, so the button looked small. Decision: leave it, and note that agreement is not evidence.

One tool said errors show only as a red border. She entered 0 in Seats and read the message under the field: "Seats must be 1 or more. Others need room to join." She had fixed this in 3.3. Decision: leave it. Tool three told her to rename the button to "Get started". The rule is that a button says what happens. "Start the group" says it, and "Get started" does not. Decision: leave the name and write the reason. This was her rejected point for the capstone log.

Three points were about people. Two tools said a student may not know what Seats counts. No rule decides that, so she asked three classmates to start a pretend group for a Thursday exam, and watched. Two stopped at Seats and asked: does that number include me? Decision: keep four fields and add the hint "Including you" under Seats. She gave up one more line of text. This was her accepted point for the capstone log. Nobody stopped at the other fields, so she kept all four. Tool one said a time list would be better, but all three classmates typed "Tonight, 8 pm" without trouble. Three people is a small sample, so that one is test later.

The last point, a purple header, named no problem a student has. Decision: drop it. Under her table she wrote that the tools disagreed on fields, hints and the button. She had measured two things, tried one error and watched three people. She had not checked a very small phone. That last sentence cost a minute and made the log honest.

## The provided Wasel case

Use this if you have no project, or if no AI tool opens for you. Wasel is a print and copy shop near the university gate, owned by Sami. It is open 8 am to 6 pm. Black and white printing is 0.50 shekels a page, colour is 2 and binding is 5. Orders sent before noon are ready by 4 pm. Sami's nephew Karam built the order app in one evening with an AI tool.

The job: "When an assignment is due tomorrow and I have no printer, I want to send a file by phone and collect it, so that I do not miss the deadline."

The screen is the order form, on a phone 375 CSS pixels wide. It has four fields with placeholders only: "File", "Copies", "Colour?" and "Pickup time". There are no labels and no hints. The placeholder text is #999999 on white. A small button says "Submit". It is 80 pixels wide and 28 pixels tall, with plenty of space around it. When a field is wrong, its border turns red. At the top, one message says "Invalid input". It does not say which field is wrong. The form shows no price anywhere: the price list is on the landing page only.

Three AI tools looked at this form. Their answers are invented for this exercise. Below are nine points.

| # | Tool | Point |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One | The grey placeholder text is hard to read. |
| 2 | One | Make the screen feel more modern. |
| 3 | One | The Submit button is too small. |
| 4 | Two | Show the total price before the customer submits. |
| 5 | Two | The button should say what happens, not "Submit". |
| 6 | Two | Use a gradient background to look more premium. |
| 7 | Three | "Invalid input" does not say which field is wrong. |
| 8 | Three | Customers may not know which file types the shop accepts. |
| 9 | Three | I give this form 5 out of 10. |

Your task is the same as Maryam's from step 5 on. Write your rubric of five checks first, then sort the nine points. For each rule point, work out or read the result from the facts above. For point 4, the rule comes from 5.4: real content that a customer needs goes before the step that needs it, so look for it on the form. For each person point, write the question, who you will watch and what you will look for. Then decide, give a reason and name what you give up.

## The rubric

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| A fair setup | Rubric written first; one prompt for every tool (or points listed by tool); nothing private; three sources named | Three of those four | Two or fewer, or a different prompt for each tool |
| Every point checked | At least seven points sorted; each rule point has a number; each person point has a question, who and what to watch | One point without a check | Two or more points without a check, or a score treated as a finding |
| Decisions with reasons | At least three decisions with a reason and a cost; one AI point accepted after a check, one rejected with a reason | Reasons given but no cost named, or only one of accepted and rejected | A tool's fix copied with no check |
| Honest limits | Says where the tools disagreed, what was not checked, and marks "test later" where no person was available | One of those three is missing | Claims an AI tool proved something, or reports a result nobody measured |

**Pass rule:** no part is Not yet and at most one is Partly.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor goes straight to the check column. They look for numbers and questions, not for good fixes. A contrast ratio with its two colours, a button height in pixels, or "I watched three classmates start a group" all show a check. "The tool suggested this, so I did it" does not. The common weak answer accepts every point, or rejects every point because "AI is often wrong". The usual follow-up is: "Which point did you reject, and what would have made you accept it?" A strong reply names a check.
