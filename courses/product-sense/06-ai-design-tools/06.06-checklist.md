## Module 6 checklist

- [ ] I can write a design brief with five parts, and a short skill checklist whose every line I can check on a finished screen
- [ ] I check whether each AI tool opens for me, at my age, and I keep a fallback that needs no account
- [ ] I give the same screen and the same rubric to three tools from different companies, and I ask for questions and evidence, not a score
- [ ] I sort each AI point into check a rule, ask a person or drop, and I write a decision, a reason and what I give up
- [ ] I open what a tool built in a browser and measure it, instead of believing what the tool says about it
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 6.2 and 6.3, finished the practice in 6.4 and the quiz in 6.5

## Learning log

1. **Your first move:** When an AI tool gave you a list of fixes this week, what did you do first? What will you do first next time?
2. **Your sure voice:** Think of a time you followed advice because it sounded sure. What would a check have looked like?
3. **Your hardest bucket:** Which of the three buckets, check, ask or drop, is hardest for you to use honestly? Why?
4. **Your line:** Where does an AI opinion stop and your judgment start, in your own work? Write it as one sentence you could say to a client.

## What changed

Before this module, an AI critique arrived as a finished answer. It had a calm voice, a tidy list and, sometimes, a score. The easy thing was to follow the list, or to pick the tool you liked best. Maryam's evening in 6.1 shows why that does not work: three tools gave three lists, and the lists could not all be followed.

Now an AI critique arrives as a set of questions. Each point has to meet a check before it changes your screen. If the point names a number, you measure it. If it names what a person knows or does, you watch a person. If it names no problem, you drop it and write why. The decision stays with you, and so does the reason.

Notice how little of this is about AI. The same habit works for advice from a senior colleague, a client's sudden idea or a comment on a forum. The module used AI tools because they are the advisers that you will meet every day, and because they sound the most sure. The rule you take away is plain: a point earns a change when a check says it should.

## A strong answer, told about a fictional fellow

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about Haneen. She is a fellow who builds a booking screen for her family's small dental clinic. She uses made-up patient names in her screenshot, because the real appointment book is not hers to share.

Haneen asked three tools from different companies the same question, with the same five checks and no score. Only two opened for her, so her third opinion was her own pass with a paper checklist, labelled as hers. She got eight points. Three were about numbers. The date hint was too light: she computed 3.45 to 1 against 4.5 to 1, accepted the point and darkened the colour. The Book button was small: she measured 40 pixels tall, against an aim of about 44 to 48, accepted it and raised it. Errors showed only as colour: she tried it, saw a red border and no words, accepted it and added the words.

Two points were about people. One tool said the time picker had too many steps. She asked two receptionists to book a visit, and watched. Both paused at the second list. She merged the two lists into one and marked the change "test again with five". The other said patients would like to see the doctor's photo. No rule decides that, and she had no patients to watch, so she wrote "test later" and did not change the screen. Three points she dropped: "make it feel more caring", a score of 6 out of 10, and a gradient header. Each one named no problem.

The tools disagreed about the button. Two said it was too small and one did not mention it. She measured, and the measurement settled it. Her last lines said what she had not checked: the screen on a very small phone, and with a screen reader.

Look at the shape of her answer. The setup was fair. Every point has a bucket and a check with a number or a question. Every decision has a reason and a cost. The limits are said out loud. Your mentor will probably ask about the photo point, because "test later" shows that she knew where her evidence stopped.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. They are not looking for a perfect screen or for the same decisions as Haneen. They are looking for evidence that each decision came from a check and not from the tool's voice.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| An accepted point | Names what the tool said, the check you ran with its result, and what you changed | The check or the result is vague | A change with no check |
| A rejected or dropped point | Gives the reason and says what evidence would have changed your mind | Gives a reason but not what would change it | Rejected because "AI is often wrong" |
| An unsure point | Says what you would do to find out, and who you would ask or watch | Says it is unsure but not how to find out | Pretends to be sure |
| Your line | One sentence that says where the AI opinion stops and your decision starts, with a reason | A sentence with no reason | Says the tool decides, or that tools are useless |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at most one part is "Partly". Your reply to the mentor's follow-up question must also answer it with evidence.

After you submit, the mentor replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What would have made you accept the point you dropped?" or "Which check could you run tomorrow for the point you were unsure about?" A good reply points to something you can show: a number, a question you wrote, a row in your table. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a voice note link as well.
