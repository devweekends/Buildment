## Module 6 checklist

- [ ] I can write a design brief with five parts, and a short skill checklist whose every line I can check on a finished screen
- [ ] I check whether each AI tool opens for me, at my age, and I keep a fallback that needs no account
- [ ] I give the same screen and the same rubric to three tools from different companies, and I ask for questions and evidence, not a score
- [ ] I sort each AI point into check a rule, ask a person or drop, and I write a decision, a reason and what I give up
- [ ] I open what a tool built and measure it, instead of believing what the tool says about it
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 6.2 and 6.3, finished the practice in 6.4 and the quiz in 6.5

## Learning log

1. **Your first move:** When an AI tool gave you a list of fixes this week, what did you do first? What will you do first next time?
2. **Your sure voice:** Think of a time you followed advice because it sounded sure. What would a check have looked like?
3. **Your hardest bucket:** Which of the three buckets, check, ask or drop, is hardest for you to use honestly? Why?
4. **Your line:** Where does an AI opinion stop and your judgment start, in your own work? Write it as one sentence you could say to a client.

## What changed

Before this module, an AI critique arrived as a finished answer. It had a calm voice, a tidy list and, sometimes, a score. The easy thing was to follow the list, or to pick the tool you liked best. Maryam's evening in 6.1 shows why that does not work: three tools gave three lists, and the lists could not all be followed.

Now an AI critique arrives as a set of questions. Each point has to meet a check before it changes your screen. If the point names a number, you measure it. If it names what a person knows or does, you watch a person. If it names no problem, you drop it and write why. The decision stays with you, and so does the reason.

Notice how little of this is about AI. The same habit works for advice from a senior colleague, a client's sudden idea or a comment on a forum. The module used AI tools because they are the advisers you will meet every day, and because they sound the most sure. The rule you take away is plain: a point earns a change when a check says it should.

## Why a reply to a client

In real work, the AI critique rarely stays on your screen. Someone else sees it. A client, a manager or a friend forwards a tool's list and asks you to apply it. That person did not see your checks. They see a long list that looks careful, and they ask a fair question: can you do all of this?

Your reply is where your judgment becomes visible. A weak reply says yes to everything, because it is polite, or no to everything, because the tool is not trusted. A strong reply is short and calm. It says what you did. It names a point you will apply and what the check showed. It names a point you will not apply and what would change your mind. It names a point you cannot judge yet and how you will find out.

The reply is also kind to the client. They asked for a result and they get one, with a reason they can follow. They do not need your table. They need to see that each change has a check behind it, and that you have said where your evidence stops. That honesty makes the next conversation easier.

Write the reply in your own words, as you would send it to a person. You can dictate it with your phone's microphone. Keep it between 120 and 220 words.

## Four moves in a good reply

A good reply has four moves. Each one answers a question the client would otherwise ask.

First, say what you did: you asked three tools the same questions, and you checked each point before you changed anything. This tells the client that the list was neither ignored nor copied. Second, pick one point to apply. Say what the tool claimed, what you measured or watched, and what you will change. The client sees that the change has a reason.

Third, pick one point you will not apply. Say why, and say what would change your mind. This is the move that shows judgment, because it shows you will change your mind for evidence. Fourth, pick one point you cannot judge yet. Say how you will find out, and when. Then finish with your line: one sentence about who decides and why.

Choose the points from your own table in 6.4. You do not need to cover every point, because the client asked for a result and not a report. A point that all three tools made and you left alone is a good one to explain. The client will notice it.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor is not looking for a perfect screen or for the same decisions as anyone else. They are looking for evidence that each decision came from a check and not from the tool's voice. After you submit, they usually reply with one or two short questions. One example: what would have made you accept the point you did not apply? A good answer points to something you can show: a number, a question you wrote, a row in your table.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| A point you apply | Names what the tool said, the check you ran with its result, and what you will change | The check or the result is vague | A change with no check |
| A point you do not apply | Gives the reason and says what evidence would change your mind | Gives a reason but not what would change it | Rejected because "AI is often wrong" |
| A point you cannot judge yet | Says how you will find out, and who you will watch or ask | Says it is unsure but not how to find out | Pretends to be sure |
| Your line | One sentence that says who decides and why, in plain words a client can follow | A sentence with no reason | Says the tool decides, or that tools are useless |

**Pass rule:** your reply passes when no part is "Not yet" and at most one part is "Partly". Your answer to the mentor's follow-up question must also point to evidence.
