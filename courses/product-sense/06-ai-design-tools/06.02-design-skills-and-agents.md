## A vague prompt gets the most common screen

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m6-vague-or-briefed" />

Tap the three prompts. The first is only style words: it gets a glow, three icon cards and a button that says "Get started". A brief removes those and gives one list with one Join button, but the words are filler. Real content makes the screen Halaqa's: real groups, times and seats left.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a vague prompt gets the common answer">

**Evidence label: research and vendor guidance.** Two AI companies say this about their own tools. Anthropic's cookbook says unguided output tends toward generic, common choices [B2]. OpenAI's frontend guidance says an underspecified prompt makes a model fall back on high-frequency patterns, which gives bland structure and weak hierarchy [B5].

Style words such as "beautiful" and "modern" are what most prompts already say. They give the tool no fact about your product. That is the course's inference from [B5], and it is why the first picture keeps the common look. A real tool may do something else on a given run.

Independent work points the same way. NN/g, the research company from 1.5, tested AI prototyping tools and found that, without visual specifications, the screens tended to look "flat and interchangeable". It linked this to the tools' training data. It noted that the tools often default to Tailwind CSS, a styling kit, and to shadcn/ui, the component kit from 4.3. Broad prompts gave very different results from each other. Detailed prompts, or an attached design frame, came close to human designs [B8]. An academic study of 120 interfaces from five tools found that the tools converged in appearance and layout, with more variation in colour [B9].

Two limits matter. The study had no human-made reference, so it shows that the tools resemble each other, not how far they are from human practice [B9]. And web design was already getting more alike before AI, which was linked to shared frameworks such as Bootstrap [B10]. So the sameness is partly a template effect, not only an AI effect.

The three screens in the picture are drawn by hand for this lesson. They are not the output of a real tool. Real tools differ from each other and from one run to the next, and many invent plausible group names instead of leaving placeholders. Even a good draft can have problems with hierarchy, grouping, contrast and spacing [B8]. That is why the last beat of this chapter asks you to check a draft yourself.

Notice the order in the picture. The brief moves the screen away from the default. Real content moves it away from every other app. Both steps are yours to do. The tool cannot know your students' real course names, times and rooms.

</GoDeeper>

## A brief fills five places the tool guesses

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m6-brief-parts" />

Switch between the two prompts. The one-line prompt leaves five places for the tool to guess. A <GlossaryTerm term="design brief" definition="A short written description of what to build, for whom, and in what style." /> puts one fact in each place. Only you know these facts. The tool can only guess them.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a brief for Halaqa's home screen">

**Evidence label: vendor guidance and research, shaped into the course's own five-part template.** The levers come from Anthropic's and OpenAI's own guidance. Steer each design dimension. Give a reference. Name the defaults you do not want [B2, B5]. NN/g adds that a detailed prompt or an attached design frame gave the best match in its test [B8]. Here is a brief for Halaqa's home screen that uses all five parts.

```text
Screen: the home screen of Halaqa, a study-group app.
Who and where: a university student on a phone, the night before an exam.
Real content: show three groups. "Data structures, exam Thursday, tonight
8 pm, library room 2, 2 seats left". "Statistics 101 revision, tomorrow
5 pm, online, 4 seats left". "Arabic literature reading, Saturday 10 am,
cafe, full": show the full group as a plain line, with no Join button.
Type and colour: one sans-serif font, body text 16 px. One accent colour,
used only on the Join button. Dark text on a white background.
Sections and main action: one list of groups and a small link to the
create-group form. The main action is "Join".
Reference: a plain list like a bus timetable: time first, no decoration.
Do not use: a gradient, an icon in a coloured tile, or filler words about
speed and ease.
```

Two warnings. First, the brief should hold facts you chose, not facts you want the tool to invent. Second, never put real people's names or data in a brief. Use made-up examples, as above. Course 13 teaches the full privacy habit: never paste a client's or employer's screens or data into an AI tool, or show them publicly, without permission.

You can also ask for the design plan before the screen. Anthropic's skill describes a plan first (colour, type, layout, principles), then a review of that plan for generic defaults, then the build [B3]. You can read a plan in a minute and change it before any screen exists. That is cheaper than fixing a finished draft.

A brief does not have to be long. The goal is not more words. It is fewer places for the tool to guess. If a line in your brief does not change a decision, delete it.

</GoDeeper>

## A skill writes the rules once

<MermaidDiagram title="Rules typed again, or written once" chart={`flowchart TD
  subgraph W["Without a skill"]
    P1["Prompt 1 plus the rules"] --> S1["Screen 1"]
    P2["Prompt 2 plus the rules again"] --> S2["Screen 2"]
  end
  subgraph K["With a skill"]
    F["SKILL.md: the rules, once"] --> T1["Screen 1"]
    F --> T2["Screen 2"]
  end
`} />

A <GlossaryTerm term="skill" definition="A folder with one SKILL.md file of written instructions that an AI tool reads." /> is a checklist the tool reads before it builds. Each line must be something you can check on a finished screen. You can also paste it as plain text into any chat.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what is inside a SKILL.md file">

**Evidence label: practice, plus the tools' own documentation.** A skill is a folder with one file named `SKILL.md`. The file starts with a short name and a description, then the instructions. Claude Code, OpenAI Codex and Google Antigravity all use this layout [B28, B33, B40]. Claude Code also lets plugins ship skills. Codex scans a folder named `.agents/skills`. Antigravity loads a skill when its description matches the task. Nobody has tested whether one folder works unchanged in all three, so use the same idea and read each tool's own folder rules.

Anthropic's `frontend-design` skill is one example of a design skill [B3, B4]. Its text lists looks that tools produce by default and tells the tool to avoid them unless the brief asks for them. Do not copy that file. Its licence is in a separate file, so write your own checklist from the principles instead. A checklist that names your product is more useful than a general one anyway.

On the free claude.ai plan, skills work if you turn on code execution and upload the folder as a ZIP file under Customize, Skills [B30]. That is a web path to the same idea. Claude Code, the coding agent, is different: it needs a paid or Console account, and the free Claude plan does not include it [B29].

Here is a checklist for Halaqa that fits in one file. Every line is something you can check on a finished screen.

```md
---
name: halaqa-screens
description: Checklist for building or changing any Halaqa screen.
---

# Halaqa screen checklist

1. Audience: a university student the night before an exam, on a phone.
2. One main action per screen. The button names it: "Join", not "Submit".
3. Real content only: real course names, times and seats. No lorem ipsum.
4. Body text at least 16 px. Hint text must pass 4.5 to 1 on its background.
5. One accent colour, used for the main action only.
6. Every input has a visible label. Errors say what to fix, in words, by the field.
7. No glow, gradient or icon tile unless the brief asks for it and says why.
8. At the end, list what you chose and why.
```

Line 8 matters. It makes the tool state its reasons, and the last beat shows why you check them. Line 1 is the weakest: it sets a scene, and nothing on the screen can be checked against it. Lines 2, 4 and 6 are the best kind, because a number or a yes-or-no answers them.

</GoDeeper>

## Two tools for each job, and a fallback

<MermaidDiagram title="Pick a tool, then check it opens" chart={`flowchart TD

  A --> Q{"Does it open for you, at your age?"}
  C --> Q
  Q -->|"yes"| U["Use it, then check the draft"]

For a design skill in a coding agent, two examples are Claude Code and OpenAI Codex. For a prompt-to-screen canvas, two examples are Stitch and v0. These are examples of a job, not a ranking. If a tool does not open for you, move down the chain.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each tool says about itself">

**Evidence label: what the vendors' own pages say, checked 2026-10-25.** These facts can change within weeks. Do not plan around a limit or a price, and never assume a tool works in your country.

Claude Code needs a paid or Console account. The free Claude plan does not include it. It runs on a laptop, not a phone [B29]. Claude is for people 18 or over [B32]. OpenAI Codex is listed on ChatGPT's Free and Go plans, and the page does not state the limits [B34]. ChatGPT is for people 13 or over, with a guardian's permission under 18.

Stitch is a Google Labs design canvas that turns prompts into screens [B42]. A forum reply from Google reports that it is for people 18 or over, so read the terms before you sign in [B43]. v0, from Vercel, builds apps with Tailwind CSS and shadcn/ui [B46]. Vercel's terms say the people using it must be at least 16 [B48]. That explains one thing you may notice: its output can look like other sites built with the same kit [B8, B13].

Neither Stitch nor v0 has a confirmed entry for Palestine on the pages this course read. So the honest instruction is: check whether it opens for you. Do not assume it does or does not.

The fallbacks are on the web and list Palestine on their vendors' pages. Claude artifacts need a Claude account, so they are for people 18 or over. Gemini Canvas is for Gemini users aged 13 or over [B31, B44, B45]. Both are places to try a small screen from your brief. Below those, the last path needs no account and no internet. Write the brief and the checklist on paper. Sketch the screen by hand, and check the sketch against the checklist. The checking is the skill. The tool is a helper.

NN/g's 2025 test found that narrow helpers, such as rewriting text, renaming layers or finding more like a selection, were more useful than broad generators [B17]. So a good use of an AI tool may be small: ask it to rewrite one error message, not to design the app.

Plans and limits change often. This course gives no numbers for them on purpose. Read each tool's own plan page on the day you plan to use it.

</GoDeeper>

## Check what the tool says it did

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m6-said-or-built" />

A stated reason is a claim. In one study of five tools, about a quarter of the stated design reasons were not in the finished screen. So open the draft and measure what the tool said it did. In the picture, one claim holds and two do not.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a five-minute check, with a phone path for each step">

**Evidence label: practice, built on standards.** The study found that about 25% of the tools' stated reasons were not implemented in the interface [B9]. That is one study of five tools, so do not read it as a rate for every tool. It is enough reason to look.

Here is a short check for any draft, in the order a person meets the screen. Every step can be done on a phone.

1. Open the draft on your own phone, which is the size that matters. On a laptop, set the browser window to about 375 pixels wide. Is anything cut off? Does a line of text run past the edge?
2. Squint at it, or blur it. What do you see first? Is that the main action? This is the squint test from 2.2 [A69].
3. Find the body text size and the hint text colour. Body text starts at 16 px. Normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 [A1]. On a phone, find the grey in the table of eight greys in 2.5, or work the formula by hand as 6.3 shows. Do not guess it from how the colour looks.
4. Check the focus order. With a keyboard, press Tab and watch the mark move. On a phone with no keyboard, read the controls from top to bottom: that is the order Tab follows, and 3.4 draws it for the create-group form.
5. Make the text bigger. On a laptop, zoom to 200%. On a phone, raise the text size in the display settings and reload the page. Does the content survive? [A15]
6. Check each control's size. Aim for about 44 to 48 CSS pixels. The AA floor is 24, and only with spacing around the target [A4, A16, A38, A39]. Read the size from the code (height, padding and font size), or use a laptop's inspector.
7. Read every word on every button. Does it say what happens? "Join" does. "Get started" does not.
8. If the tool listed its choices, tick off each one that you can find on the screen.

NN/g also noticed that these tools produce output that looks good from a distance, and then shows problems with hierarchy, grouping, contrast and spacing up close [B8]. It reported that the tools do not have the judgment to weigh trade-offs for you [B8]. That is why this module keeps the decision with you.

A draft that passes the check is not finished. It is a draft that has met a floor. Module 4 reminds you that passing standards makes a screen "good", not "remarkable".

</GoDeeper>

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Tala builds a booking page for her cousin's barber shop. She types 'Make a modern booking page' and gets a purple glow, three icon cards and a 'Get started' button. She wants the next draft to be her cousin's shop. Which next prompt moves the draft most?"

  - "Add: make it more modern, premium and clean, in a calmer style"
  - "Add the shop's real services, prices and hours, and one main action"
  - "Send the same prompt to another tool and keep whichever draft she likes"
  - "Add: avoid purple, and give the glow a different colour instead"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "More style words are still vague. Tala would be giving the tool another chance to guess, and the most common answer is the likely guess."
  - "Right. Real services, prices and hours are things only Tala knows, and one main action gives the page a job. These are the gaps the tool was filling with common choices."
  - "A second tool given the same vague prompt tends to give a similar look. In one study, five tools made similar layouts for the same prompt."
  - "Naming a default to avoid is one useful lever, but changing the glow's colour keeps the glow. The page would still say nothing about the barber shop."
```
