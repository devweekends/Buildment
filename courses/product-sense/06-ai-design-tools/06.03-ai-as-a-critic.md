Maryam has three AI critiques of one screen and no way to choose between them. Her question from 6.1 stays the same: how can I tell which of these points is true? She now asks for something different: not a verdict, but questions she can check. The method in this chapter is the course's inference from research, not a tested recipe. Tool facts here are true at the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25).

## Three tools, one rubric, one table

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m6-one-table" />

Give the same screenshot and the same <GlossaryTerm term="rubric" definition="A short list of the checks you want applied, written before you ask." /> to three tools from different companies. Each answers alone. Merge every point into one table. If only two tools open for you, add your own paper pass and label it as yours.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why three tools, and what that does not prove">

**Evidence label: research, with a clear limit.** No study has tested whether asking three AI tools improves the design of a fellow's screen. The method is the course's inference from nearby evidence. What the nearby evidence says:

In one large benchmark, a strong AI judge agreed with human judges more than 80% of the time, about as often as two humans agree with each other. The same study found that AI judges can favour the first answer they see, favour longer answers, and favour their own style. Another study found that a panel of several smaller models from different families beat a single large judge on six datasets. The panel also showed less bias from any one model. Those tests scored text answers. They did not test screens.

For design in particular, a 2026 paper built a Figma plugin that gave critique from three expert roles: UX, product and engineering. In two studies with 48 people in total, it beat a static checker. NN/g's 2023 article says AI critiques of screenshots can surface points a team missed. It also says many of the insights are wrong, and that junior staff should probably avoid them. In NN/g's 2026 survey of 521 practitioners, time savings were smallest for evaluating designs. That survey is a convenience sample, so it is a hint, not a measure.

Put together: more than one opinion, from different companies, reduces the habits of any one model. It does not make any point true. Only a check does.

Which tools? At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), three examples are Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude. Gemini is for people 13 or over. ChatGPT is for people 13 or over, with a guardian's permission under 18. Claude is for people 18 or over. Anthropic's and Google's country pages list Palestine. Check whether each one opens for you, at your age.

If only two open, use the two and add a third pass of your own with a paper checklist, and say so in your notes. Never present your own pass as a tool's answer. Three chats with one tool are one opinion repeated, because they share that tool's habits.

One privacy sentence: never paste a client's or employer's screens or data into an AI tool, or show them publicly, without permission. Course 13 teaches the full habit.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask for questions, not a verdict

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m6-verdict-or-questions" />

NN/g warns that AI critiques of screens can sound sure and still be wrong. So do not ask whether the screen is good. Ask what a student might struggle with, which rule it touches, and what you could measure. Questions are safe to receive. Verdicts are not.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a prompt you can copy">

**Evidence label: practice.** Write the rubric before you ask. Five checks cover most screens at this stage of the course. First, what you see first (the squint test, 2.2). Second, text size and contrast (2.3 and 2.5). Third, space and grouping (2.4). Fourth, labels, hints and error words (3.3). Fifth, the words on buttons (5.3). Write the job and the person too, because a critique with no job is only a list of opinions.

```text
This is a screenshot of one screen of my app. The person using it is a
university student on a phone, the night before an exam. They want to start
a study group in under a minute.
Look at it using only these five checks:
1. What is seen first, and is it the main action?
2. Is the text big enough, and is there enough contrast?
3. Is the space inside each group smaller than the space between groups?
4. Does every field have a visible label, and would an error say what to fix?
5. Does each button say what happens?
For every point, give three things: what you see in the screenshot, the rule
or evidence you are using, and one way I could check it (a measurement, or a
question to ask a real person).
Do not give a score. Do not say whether the screen is good. If you are not
sure, say so.
```

Three habits make the answers easier to use. Use the same words for all three tools; if you change the prompt for one tool, you cannot compare the answers. Ask for short answers. And save each answer under the tool's name before you read the next, so the first answer does not change how you read the others.

Do not paste personal data, other people's names or a client's screen into the prompt. Use your own app, a made-up example or a screen you have permission to share.

</GoDeeper>

## Swapping the order can change the winner

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m6-order-swap" />

AI judges can favour the answer they see first, and their own writing. When you ask a tool to compare two versions, ask twice with the order swapped. If the winner flips, you learned the tool's habit, not your screen's quality. Do not ask a tool to review its own work either.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: judges have favourites">

**Evidence label: research (preprints and a benchmark), tested on text answers.** Three biases are described in the studies. Position bias: changing the order of two answers can flip a judge's ranking. Swapping the order and combining both results reduced the effect in the one study that tested it. Self-preference: models can recognise their own text and rate it higher than other text, even when people rate the two equally. Verbosity bias: longer answers can win.

What this means for screens. Say two tools each build a draft, and a third tool is asked which is better. Treat the pick as a hint, because the judge may favour the first draft it sees. When one tool judges a draft that it built itself, expect it to like it. Neither bias is a reason to avoid AI opinions. Both are reasons to put a check between the opinion and the decision.

A simple routine. Ask each tool for a short answer. Compare two versions in both orders. Do not ask a tool to review its own output. When three tools agree, still check: they may share a habit, and agreement is not evidence. When they disagree, that is useful information, because it shows which points are matters of taste and which can be measured.

The panel result is the best reason for three tools from different companies. It is tested on scoring text answers, so it supports the idea only loosely. That is why this course calls the method an inference and keeps a human check at the end.

</GoDeeper>

## Sort each point: check, ask or drop

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m6-check-the-critique" />

Tap or drag each point into a bucket. A standard can measure some points. Normal text needs 4.5 to 1. Large text (18 point, or 14 point bold) needs 3 to 1. A button aims for 44 to 48 pixels. What a student understands, you ask a person. A point that names no problem, you drop.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to check a rule, and how to ask a person">

**Evidence labels are in the table.** A rule check gives a yes or a no. A person check gives a question and a pattern you watch for. A heuristic, as 2.2 said, is a rule from practice and not a law.

| Point | Check | What it rests on | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text is too light | Contrast ratio of text and background: 4.5 to 1 for normal text, 3 to 1 for large text | WCAG 1.4.3 | Standard |
| Icons and borders hard to see | 3 to 1 against what is next to them | WCAG 1.4.11 | Standard |
| Only colour shows a state | Add words, a shape or an icon | WCAG 1.4.1 | Standard |
| Button too small | 24 CSS pixels is the AA floor with spacing; aim for about 44 to 48 | WCAG 2.5.8, 2.5.5; Apple, Material | Standard and practice |
| Nothing stands out | Squint at it; use no more than three sizes | NN/g | Heuristic |
| Field has no label | Visible label above the field | NN/g, GOV.UK | Heuristic and practice |

Large text means at least 18 point, or 14 point bold. Contrast is computed with the WCAG formula, never judged by eye.

For a person check, write the question before you meet anyone. Ask about the last time something happened, not whether they would use something. Give a classmate one task: "start a group for your Thursday exam". Watch where they stop, and do not help. Three to five people can show you most of the big problems of one group, and you then fix and test again. This finds problems. It does not prove that a design is good.

A point can need both. "Four fields are too many" has no number behind it, so it goes to a person. "The button is too small" has a number, so it goes to a rule. Many points that sound like taste hide a number. Many points that sound like facts hide a person.

</GoDeeper>

## Every point ends in a decision

<MermaidDiagram title="Every point ends in a decision" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["One AI point"] --> K{"Which check?"}
  K -->|"a standard can measure it"| R["Measure it"]
  K -->|"it depends on a student"| H["Watch 3 to 5 people"]
  K -->|"no problem named"| D["Drop it"]
  R --> E["Change, leave or test later, with a reason"]
  H --> E
  D --> E
`} />

Every point ends in one of three decisions: change it, leave it, or test it later. Write the reason and what you give up. Even a dropped point gets a written reason. If three tools agree, still check, because agreement is not a measurement. A decision with a reason is yours. A copied fix is the tool's.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a worked check, and the decision log">

**Evidence label: practice, using a standard.** Take one point from Maryam's critiques: "the grey hint text is too light". The hint under Where is #767676 on white. Work the formula by hand, as you would on a phone calculator. Each colour channel of this grey is 118. Divide by 255 to get 0.463. Then work out ((0.463 + 0.055) / 1.055) to the power 2.4, which is 0.181. All three channels match, so 0.181 is the luminance, the brightness number from 2.5. White is 1. The ratio is (1 + 0.05) / (0.181 + 0.05), which is 4.54.

Normal text needs 4.5 to 1, so the hint passes. The check does not support the point. Decision: leave it. Reason to write down: it passes by a small margin, so do not make the grey any lighter later. Now suppose the colour had been #777777. The same working gives 4.48 to 1, which fails, and the decision would be to change it. One digit made the difference. That is why you measure. You can also read both greys in the table of eight greys in 2.5.

Keep a decision log with one row per point. The columns are: the point, which tool said it, the check, the result, the decision, and what you give up. The check is a rule with a number, or a question and the person you will watch. "What you give up" is easy to skip. If you cut two fields, you give up the data they held. If you darken the hint, you give up a lighter look. Writing it down shows you chose.

Your capstone critique log needs at least one AI point that you checked and then accepted, and one that you rejected with a reason. A rejected point can be a good entry. It shows judgment: "Tool three told me to rename the button to Get started. I rejected it because a button should say what happens, and Start the group already does."

Be fair to the tools. A point that fails a check was still useful if it made you check. The cost of a wrong point is small when you check it. The cost is large only when you copy it.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Alaa asks a tool to review her sign-up screen for a coding club. It says 'the grey page heading is too light to read'. She finds the facts: the heading is 18 point regular, grey #858585 on white, a contrast ratio of 3.69 to 1. What should she do?"
options:
  - "Leave the colour as it is"
  - "Darken the heading until it reaches 4.5 to 1"
  - "Ask five classmates whether they can read it"
  - "Check the heading again with a second tool"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. 18 point is large text, and large text needs 3 to 1. At 3.69 to 1 the heading passes, so the point does not survive its check. She leaves it and notes the small margin."
  - "4.5 to 1 is the limit for normal text. A heading at 18 point is large text, which has the lower limit of 3 to 1, so the heading already passes."
  - "Classmates could add a view, but contrast is a number and she already has it. The rule gives a clear answer, so the check comes first."
  - "A second tool gives a second claim, not a measurement. She has the ratio and the rule, so there is nothing left for a second opinion to settle."
```
