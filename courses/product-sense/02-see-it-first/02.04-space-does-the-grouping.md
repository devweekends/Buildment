## Drag the space between the groups

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-spacing-groups" />

Drag across the picture. The space inside each group stays at 6. At 6 between groups, the list is nine lines with no groups. At 22, each group reads as one. At 56, the groups drift apart and a small screen shows fewer of them. This is <GlossaryTerm term="proximity" definition="The closer two things are, the more they look like one group." />.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: proximity, and why equal gaps fail">

Material 3 says spacing groups related content and directs attention, and that consistent spacing between related items makes them easier to scan. NN/g's form guidance uses the same law of proximity: put things that belong together close, and things that do not belong together far apart. Both are professional guidance, not test results, so the right label is practice and heuristic. The effect is strong enough that you can see it for yourself in the picture.

The rule is about a difference. It is not about one number. If every gap on a screen is equal, nothing groups, whatever the number is. This is why adding more space everywhere does not help. If you make every gap 12 or every gap 24, the nine lines are still nine lines. The groups appear only when the space between them is larger than the space inside.

No source we checked gives an exact ratio. In the picture, 22 between and 6 inside is about three to one, and it reads clearly. Start with at least double, then check with a squint. Too much space has a cost as well: in the last step only one group fits on a small phone, so the reader scrolls to compare groups.

The amounts in the picture are drawn units. They show the effect. They are not the amounts you should type into your own code, which come from your spacing step in the last beat.

</GoDeeper>

## Each label belongs to one field

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-label-gap" />

Switch between the two forms. With equal gaps, each label floats halfway between two fields, and the reader has to think about which field it names. With 4 below a label and 22 below a field, each label stays with its own field. Nothing changed but space, and nothing needs a line or a box.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: labels, fields and groups of fields">

NN/g's guidance on form labels has three parts. Put a label closer to its own field than to the other fields. Put labels above fields, because they are easier to scan. Group related fields. All three follow the law of proximity. Chapter 3.3 teaches forms in full. This beat only uses forms to show that proximity decides who owns what.

The same idea works in any list with a heading and a detail. A heading belongs to the lines under it, so there is less space below it than above it. A price belongs to its product, so it sits next to it and not halfway to the next product. If you find yourself adding a line or a box to show which parts belong together, try space first. Lines and boxes add shapes to the screen. Space adds none.

In the picture, the label is semibold, and it lines up with the left edge of its field. Space is one signal among several. It is the one that costs nothing to add.

What kind of rule is this? A heuristic from NN/g, supported by design-system guidance. Test it by asking someone to point to the field a label belongs to.

</GoDeeper>

## Squint at groups made by space

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-squint-groups" />

Squint at both lists. You cannot read the words, but one list still shows three groups. The squint test from chapter 2.2 shows the groups that space makes, as well as the first thing on a screen. Use it every time you change the spacing. One smear means the space between groups is too small. Six shapes mean the space inside groups is too large.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: finding groups on a real screen">

NN/g says the squint or blur test shows the real groupings on a screen, and any emphasis you did not mean. For spacing, you count the shapes. On a screen of three study groups, you should see three shapes. If you see one smear, the space between groups is too small or the space inside is too large. If you see six shapes, the space inside groups is too large, and each line has become its own group.

Groups can also be shown by common regions, which means a shared box or a shared background. NN/g lists them next to proximity and whitespace. Use them when space alone is not enough, for example when a card must also be tapped as one thing. Do not use a box around everything. When every part has a box, no part looks grouped, and the boxes add shapes for the eye to cross.

A short check you can run on your phone: take a screenshot, make it small, and count the groups. Then ask a classmate to point to the groups without reading the words. If they point to what you meant, the spacing works.

What kind of rule is this? A heuristic. It is quick, and it matches what you see.

</GoDeeper>

## Pick one step and use its multiples

<MermaidDiagram title="One spacing step, used everywhere" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["One step: 4"] --> B["Multiples: 8, 12, 16, 24, 32"]
  B --> C["Inside a group: 8"]
  B --> D["Between groups: 24"]
  C --> E["Groups show and spacing is consistent"]
  D --> E
`} legend="The step is a choice. Consistency is the point. Inside a group use a small multiple and between groups use a larger one." />

Choose one spacing step, for example 4, and use only its multiples. Inside a group use a small multiple, such as 8. Between groups use a larger one, such as 24. The picture's units show the effect; on a real screen, snap your amounts to your step. Any step works. What matters is that every gap is a multiple of it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the 8 point grid is a convention">

Many designers say "use an 8 point grid". The honest version is smaller. Material 2 aligns most measurements to 8 dp, and aligns icons to a 4 dp grid. Tailwind CSS uses a default spacing unit of 0.25rem, which is 4 px at the usual size. The Apple layout guidance we opened does not state an 8 point rule. So an 8 point grid is Material's convention, and 4 is Tailwind's. Do not say that all platforms use an 8 point grid.

The source for the reason is Material 3: consistent spacing between related items makes them easier to scan. A step gives you that, because it gives you fewer choices. A stray 13 between two 16s looks like a mistake, even if nobody can say why. With a step, every gap is 8, 16 or 24, and the eye learns the rhythm.

The pictures in this chapter use 6, 12, 22, 36 and 56 on purpose, to show the effect. Those are not a scale to copy. In your own work, pick 4 or 8, write it at the top of your file or notes, and use only its multiples. Chapter 2.6 asks you to name your step.

What kind of rule is this? Practice. It is a convention from design systems, not a test. Its value is consistency, and any single step does that.

</GoDeeper>

## Fix a screen's spacing in four steps

<MermaidDiagram title="Four steps for spacing" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Squint and count the groups"] --> B["Pick one step"]
  B --> C["Inside a group: a small multiple"]
  C --> D["Between groups: a larger multiple"]
  D --> A
`} legend="Count the groups, choose a step, set small gaps inside and larger gaps between, then squint and count again." />

Squint at your screen and count the groups. Pick one step. Set small gaps inside groups and larger gaps between them. Then squint and count again. Repeat until the number of shapes you see is the number of groups you meant.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a worked fix on Halaqa">

Maryam's list had every gap at 8. She squinted and saw one smear. She picked a step of 4. She set the lines inside each group 4 apart. She set the gap between groups to 24, which is 6 times her step. She squinted again and saw three shapes.

Then she noticed a new problem. The 24 gap pushed the third group below the bottom of her phone. She tried 16 and the groups still showed, so she kept 16 and checked a second time. The steps are a loop, because the first answer is often not the last one.

Her screen changed in only one way. She added no lines and no boxes. If you find you need to scroll to see the second group, reduce the gap between groups before you reduce anything else, and check that three shapes still show.

</GoDeeper>
