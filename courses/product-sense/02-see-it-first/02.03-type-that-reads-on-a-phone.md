Maryam's classmate Qusai opens a Halaqa group description on his phone in the library, holds the phone close and frowns. The words are on the screen, but they are hard to read.

## Drag the body text from small to large

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-type-ladder" />

Drag the size. At 11 the words strain the eye. At about 16 they read well. At 20 they still read, but the page grows. At 26 few words fit on a line and Join moves down. Sixteen <GlossaryTerm term="px" definition="A CSS pixel, the unit web pages use for sizes." /> is a common start, not a law.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where 16 comes from">

Frameworks show what the common defaults are. At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), Tailwind CSS sets its base text size to 1rem, which is 16 px, with a line height of 1.5. Bootstrap 5.3 sets body text to 1rem on a browser default of 16 px. Apple's Human Interface Guidelines give 17 points as the default text size on iOS and 11 points as the minimum. One typography guide recommends 15 to 25 px for body text on the web, tuned to the typeface. Together these say that 16 is a common start and that a range around it is fine.

The minimum is not a target. Eleven points is the minimum in Apple's guidelines, for text such as a fine-print label. It is not a good size for the paragraphs a person must read. The <GlossaryTerm term="WCAG" definition="The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, a standard with levels A, AA and AAA. This course uses AA as its bar." /> does not set a minimum text size. Its rule, in the third beat, is about letting the reader scale the text.

What kind of rule is this? Practice. These numbers come from design systems, framework defaults and one expert's guide, not from a test with readers. They are good starting points, and your own check on a real phone decides.

For Arabic, do not copy these numbers. The sources give sizes for Latin letters. No source we checked gives Arabic sizes. So the course rule is this: show your screen to a native reader on a real phone. Ask whether they can read it for a few minutes without strain. The fourth beat lets you try it.

</GoDeeper>

## Line height gives the eye a path

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-line-height" />

The gap from one line to the next is the <GlossaryTerm term="line height" definition="The distance from one line to the next, as a multiple of the text size." />. The words and size are the same in all three. At 1.0 the lines crowd each other. At about 1.5 each line is easy to follow. At 2.2 the lines drift apart.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: line height numbers and where they come from">

Material 3 recommends a line height of about 1.5 times the text size for body and label text, and about 1.2 for large display, headline and title text. Large text gets a smaller multiple. Tailwind's default is also 1.5 for its base size. One typography guide suggests 120 to 145 percent of the text size, which is 1.2 to 1.45. These agree closely enough: pick a value between 1.2 and 1.5 and look at it with your own font.

WCAG 1.4.12 (Level AA) sets a related rule. A page must keep working when a reader changes four spacing settings. Line height becomes 1.5 times the font size. Paragraph spacing becomes 2 times, letter spacing 0.12 times and word spacing 0.16 times. You do not have to use these values yourself. The rule means your boxes must not clip or overlap text when a reader does. WCAG 1.4.8 (Level AAA) asks for at least 1.5 line spacing inside paragraphs and no justified text, and it is an extra, not the floor.

What kind of rule is this? Both. The WCAG rules are standards. The 1.2 to 1.5 range is practice and a design system's default.

The picture's 2.2 is not wrong in a measured way. It is an observation: at that spacing the lines begin to look like separate lines instead of one paragraph. Nobody has proven a limit, so use your eyes and a native reader for Arabic.

</GoDeeper>

## At 200 percent, nothing may be lost

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-text-zoom" />

Some readers make their phone's text larger. WCAG asks that a page still work when text is scaled to 200 percent. Switch between the cards. When boxes grow with their text, the words wrap, the page gets taller and nothing is lost. A box with a fixed height cuts off text and buttons, and the page fails the check.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how a page loses content at 200 percent">

WCAG 1.4.4 Resize Text (Level AA) says text can be resized up to 200 percent without assistive technology and without losing content or function. It is a standard, and it is one of the AA rules this course uses as its bar.

A page fails this check in a few common ways. A box with a fixed height clips the text that no longer fits. A header with a fixed height hides the button under it. A page that stops the reader from zooming blocks the check altogether. Text that wraps is fine. Text that disappears is not.

A simple habit helps. Write text sizes in rem, the unit that follows the reader's own size setting, as Tailwind and Bootstrap do for body text. Let boxes grow with their text. Then test: set your phone's text size to its largest and open your page. Check the button, the form fields and every menu.

The picture shows the easy case, a card with three lines. A real page has menus, forms and tables, and each needs the same check on a real phone.

</GoDeeper>

## Arabic text needs a native reader

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-arabic-size" />

The numbers above are for Latin letters. No source we opened gives Arabic sizes or line heights. You are the native reader. Drag the size and read the paragraph for a minute at each stop. Then check the same screen on a real phone, change one thing at a time, and ask again.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to ask a native reader">

Ask three plain questions. Can you read this size without holding the phone closer? Do the lines feel too tight, too loose or about right? Do your eyes hurt after a few minutes? Write down the answers, change one thing, and ask again. Changing one thing at a time tells you which change helped.

This is a course rule, not a sourced number. The sources we checked cover Latin and Chinese, Japanese and Korean text. They do not give an Arabic body size, line length or line height, so any number we gave would be a guess. Direction in markup and logical CSS for right-to-left text are taught in module 3.

If you have no native reader nearby, test with two different people and an old and a new phone, since screen sharpness changes how small text looks. Never decide only from a laptop screen. The picture draws Arabic letters in your reader's font, which may differ from the font in your app.

</GoDeeper>

## Run the type checks in this order

<MermaidDiagram title="The type checks, and the Arabic fork" chart={`flowchart TD

  D --> E{"Is the text Arabic?"}
  E -->|"Yes"| F["A native reader checks on a real phone"]
  E -->|"No"| G["Done"]

`} legend="16, 1.5 and 45 to 90 are practice. 200 percent is WCAG AA. Arabic has no numbers, so a person decides." />

The second check is the <GlossaryTerm term="line length" definition="The number of characters in one line, counting spaces." />. It matters most on a laptop: set a maximum width so that lines hold 45 to 90 characters. Run the checks in order, and note which fails first.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: line length and the limits of these numbers">

One typography guide recommends lines of 45 to 90 characters, counting spaces. Apple's layout guidance also restricts text width so that text stays readable. WCAG 1.4.8 (Level AAA) asks for a way to keep lines to 80 characters or fewer, and 40 for Chinese, Japanese and Korean text. It is an AAA extra, and a page does not have to meet it. Do not say that WCAG requires 80 characters.

Too short is also a problem. If a column is so narrow that only two or three words fit on a line, the eye must jump to the next line again and again. The 26 unit step of the first picture shows this. Too long makes the eye lose its place when it goes back to the start of the next line.

All of these numbers are for Latin text, and WCAG gives a number for Chinese, Japanese and Korean text but none for Arabic. This is why the last check is a person. Ask a native reader: is the size comfortable, do the lines feel too tight or too loose, and does the text hurt after a few minutes? Write down what they say and change one thing at a time.

What kind of rule is this? Line length is practice. The 80 character limit is a standard at AAA, and it is optional.

</GoDeeper>

```quiz
  - "Raise the body text size from 16 to 18 on phones and laptops"

  - "Stop the page from scaling text past 150 percent"

explanations:
  - "Sixteen is a common start for body text, and the size is not what removes the lines. A larger size would make the cut-off worse."
  - "About 60 characters is inside the 45 to 90 range, so line length is not the problem. A narrower column would not bring the lost lines back."
  - "Right. At 200 percent a page must keep all its content. Lines that disappear mean a box has a fixed height, so the card must grow with its text."

```
