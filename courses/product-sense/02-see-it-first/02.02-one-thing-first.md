## Add signals until one thing comes first

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-signals" />

Drag to add one signal at a time to the group's name. The five signals are size, weight, contrast, position and grouping. One signal is only a hint. Several together decide what the eye reads first. The <GlossaryTerm term="visual hierarchy" definition="The order in which the eye reads a screen." /> of a screen is the order they set.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the five signals">

NN/g, the research company from chapter 1.5, says visual hierarchy guides the eye in the order of intended importance. It names scale, contrast and grouping as the sources. Contrast here means a difference in value and saturation: how light or dark a colour is, and how strong it is. It does not mean the colour itself. A dark title on a pale page is stronger than a pale title on a pale page, whatever the colour.

Grouping comes from parts that sit close together, parts that share a box or a background, and space around a part. Chapter 2.4 teaches the space part. Weight and position are common practice in the same spirit. A bold line is read before a regular line of the same size. Position is a layout habit: designers put the first thing near the top. It is not a measured rule here, so check it with a squint.

What kind of rule is this? A <GlossaryTerm term="heuristic" definition="A guideline from experts that helps you find problems. It is not a proven law." />, not a test result. Use it to find problems, then check with a person.

Signals add up. A large, bold, dark title near the top is read first on almost any screen. The reverse is the problem you saw on Fares's page in chapter 2.1: every line has the same signals, so no line wins.

</GoDeeper>

## Squint at the screen to see its order

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-squint-test" />

The <GlossaryTerm term="squint test" definition="Blur a screen, or half close your eyes, until the words are unreadable. What stays visible is the order the eye follows." /> shows the order of a screen. Switch between the four versions. As built, nothing is first and the squint is an even smear. In the fixed version the group's name is first and the Join button is second.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to run the squint test">

NN/g describes the squint or blur test as a way to see the real groupings and any emphasis you did not intend. You can do it on a phone with no tools. Hold the screen at arm's length and half close your eyes. Or take a screenshot, open it in your photo app, and make it small or blurred. Then ask two questions. What do I see first? Is that what I want a person to see first? The questions are the course's own wording, built on the NN/g test.

Look at the fixed version of Halaqa's screen. After the blur, two shapes remain: the group's name, large and bold, and the Join button, the only filled shape. That is the order Maryam wants. People see which group, then what to do. The other actions, "Create group" and the note about the menu, fade out. They are still there for a person who looks for them.

The test has limits. A blur shows size, weight and contrast. It does not show meaning. A screen can pass the squint test and still put the wrong thing first, for example a large banner for a feature nobody uses. The test finds an unclear order. You still have to decide what the right first thing is, and that comes from the job you wrote in module 1.

What kind of rule is this? A heuristic from NN/g. It is quick and free, which is why it is worth doing on every screen.

</GoDeeper>

## Three sizes are enough to set the order

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-three-sizes" />

Switch between one, three and six sizes. Only the size changes: weight and colour stay the same. With one size there is no order. With six sizes that ignore importance, the sizes compete. Three steps are enough: a large title, a medium size for details and a small size for notes. Keep them far apart, with the most important thing largest.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: sizes, weight and when to break the guide">

NN/g advises no more than three sizes on a screen and says the most important element should be the largest. This is a guide from one expert source. A long page with several kinds of heading may need a fourth step, and that is fine if you can say why.

You can make more steps without more sizes. Weight makes a second step inside one size: a semibold line is read before a regular line at the same size. Colour value does the same: grey text is read after black text. The picture holds weight and colour the same so that you see the effect of size alone. In the fixed version of Halaqa's screen, the seats line has the same size as the time and place, but it is semibold. It is read slightly earlier without adding a size.

Sizes also need to be far enough apart to look different. A title at 22 and a detail at 16 are different. A title at 17 and a detail at 16 look almost the same, and the small difference looks like a mistake. If two sizes look almost equal, make them equal or make them clearly different.

The numbers in the picture are drawn units, close to pixels on a phone. Chapter 2.3 explains what size body text needs to be readable.

What kind of rule is this? A heuristic. It is a good default, and it is also easy to check: count the sizes on your screen.

</GoDeeper>

## Put the key point first

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-front-load" />

Switch between the two pages and read only the first words of each line. NN/g calls the F-shaped scan a failure, not a goal. Readers fall into it on a text-heavy page that gives no cues. The fix is in the second page: the key point first, headings that start with the meaningful word, and the extras cut.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the F pattern and relevant content only">

People sometimes copy "the F pattern" as a layout. That reverses the finding. NN/g reports that readers fall into this scan when a text-heavy page gives them no strong cues, and it says the pattern is bad for users. Its advice is to front-load the key points, use clear headings that start with the words that carry the meaning, and cut unnecessary content.

Front-loading means the first words of a heading or a line do the work. "Join: 2 seats left tonight" tells the reader something in its first word. "An overview of the options for joining" does not.

Cutting content is the other half. The eighth of NN/g's ten usability heuristics is "aesthetic and minimalist design". NN/g explains that this means showing relevant information only, and removing what does not help the task. It is not a visual trend and it does not mean fewer pixels. Halaqa has 14 features. The main screen needs the ones that serve the job from module 1: finding a group for tonight. The rest can live in a menu. Every extra part on a screen weakens the signals of the parts that matter.

What kind of rule is this? Both are heuristics from NN/g. They find likely problems; a short check with real people confirms them.

</GoDeeper>

## Weaken what competes, then look again

<MermaidDiagram title="Maryam's loop for the first screen" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Squint at the screen"] --> B{"Do two things compete?"}
  B -->|"Yes"| C["Make one of them weaker"]
  C --> A
  B -->|"No"| D{"Is the first thing the job's?"}
  D -->|"No"| E["Give the job's thing the strongest signals"]
  E --> A
  D -->|"Yes"| F["Stop"]
`} legend="A squint finds an unclear order. Only the job from module 1 says which thing should be first." />

Maryam picks the group and the Join button. She makes the group's name the largest line and Join the only filled button. She makes the rest weaker. Then she squints again, and she repeats until both answers are the ones at the bottom of the picture.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a worked fix, and a common mistake">

Maryam's screen had five bold lines at one size and three identical buttons. Her first idea was to make everything bigger so it would be easier to see. The squint test showed that it did not help: a larger even grey is still an even grey.

So she asked the question from chapter 2.1: what should a person see first? Her answer, from the job in module 1, was the soonest group with seats left, and the way to join it. She made the group's name 22, bold. She set the time and place at 16 in grey. She made "2 seats left" semibold at 16. She filled one button, "Join this group", and made "Create group" a small plain button at 13. Three sizes: 22, 16 and 13.

A common mistake is to use a strong signal on too many things. If the title is large, the button is bright, the seats line is red and there is a banner, the eye has four places to go. Use one strong signal for the first thing, a weaker one for the second, and nothing for the rest. If your squint test still shows two shapes of equal strength, make one of them weaker.

Another mistake is to make a part stand out because it is new or because you worked hard on it. The order should follow what the person needs, not what you enjoyed building. Chapter 2.6 asks you to do all of this on one screen of your own.

</GoDeeper>

  - "Give each button its own colour, so each one is noticed"
  - "Add a fourth text size, so that the three buttons look different"

explanations:
  - "The name already comes first, so making it larger changes nothing. The squint showed that the three buttons compete for the next place."
  - "Right. One filled button for the action the bakery wants gives the screen a clear second step after the name, and the plain buttons stay available."
  - "Three colours give three equal signals, so the eye still has no order to follow. When every button is bright, none of them is noticed."
  - "A fourth size breaks the guide of three sizes, and size is not the problem here. The buttons have the same signals, and a new size would not say which one matters."
