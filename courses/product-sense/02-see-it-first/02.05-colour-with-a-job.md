Maryam makes the "seats left" text on Halaqa amber, so that it is easy to see, and at home it looks fine. In the bright light of the library window, her friend cannot read it.

## Give each colour one job

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-colour-jobs" />

Switch between the two pages. On the first, every part has its own colour, so no colour means anything. On the second, one <GlossaryTerm term="accent colour" definition="The one strong colour kept for the main action, so that it means: act here." /> marks the Join button, and the status is written in words.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: colour with a job">

A colour has a job when a reader can say what it means. In the second page, the accent means "do this". It is used for the main action and for nothing else. Grey means "information". If you add a red for a dangerous action such as deleting a group, it has one job too, and the word "Delete" says it in text.

Look at the first page. The red pill says "Exam Thursday". Nothing is wrong, so red has no job there. The green pill says "Open", the amber one says "2 seats left", and the blue one says "Library". Four colours compete, and a reader learns that colour does not carry meaning on this screen. That makes the colours that do carry meaning, such as an error, weaker.

One accent is the course's practice, not a law. A product may have a second colour with a clear job. The test is the same as in chapter 2.2: squint at the screen. If you see more than one strong colour shape, ask what each one is for.

Maryam picks blue for Join because white text on it measures 5.17 to 1, not because of what blue is said to mean. The evidence for colours meaning things like trust is mixed and depends on context, so pick colours for their contrast and their job.

What kind of rule is this? Practice. It is a way to keep a screen clear, and you check it with the squint test.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the grey from light to black

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-contrast-ladder" />

On white, #999999 is 2.85 to 1 and fails the AA bar of 4.5. #777777 is 4.48 and just fails. #767676 is 4.54 and passes. The eye cannot see that step, but the answer flips. A <GlossaryTerm term="contrast ratio" definition="How different two colours are in lightness, from 1 (none) to 21." /> comes from a formula, and it belongs to a pair of colours, as the table shows.

| #6B6B6B | 5.33 pass | 4.84 pass | pass |
| #757575 | 4.61 pass | 4.19 fail | pass |

| #999999 | 2.85 fail | 2.59 fail | fail |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: ratios belong to a pair, and the table">

The table is for a phone with no tools. Every ratio was computed with the WCAG formula. "Pass" and "fail" in the first two columns mean the bar for normal text, 4.5 to 1. The last column uses the bar for large text, 3 to 1.

A ratio belongs to a pair of colours, not to one colour. The same #767676 that is 4.54 to 1 on white is 4.13 to 1 on the light grey panel #F3F4F6. That fails. Whenever you place text on a tinted card, a photo or a gradient, measure again with the real background.

Light secondary text is the most common failure. Placeholder grey in a field, a hint under a label, a date in a list and a disabled-looking link all tend to be pale. The standard treats hint text and field borders as text or non-text parts that still need a ratio. A control that is really switched off is excluded, but a control that only looks faint is not.

Passing the bar does not make a design good. A page can pass every ratio and still put the wrong thing first or crowd its lines together. Contrast is a floor: it keeps text readable for more people, including anyone in bright light on a cracked or dim screen.

Check both light and dark appearances. At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), Apple asks for this in its accessibility guidance. NN/g reviewed the research. For people with normal vision, light mode leads to better visual performance most of the time. NN/g recommends letting people switch to dark mode. Offer dark mode as a choice, and check contrast in both.

</GoDeeper>

## The bar depends on the text size

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-large-text" />

Switch between the three sizes. The grey never changes, but the answer does. Normal text needs 4.5 to 1. Large text needs 3 to 1. Large text is at least 18 point, about 24 CSS pixels, or 14 point bold, about 18.7 pixels. Regular text at 16 is not large, and neither is bold text at 16.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the standard and the formula">

WCAG 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), Level AA, sets 4.5 to 1 for text and images of text, and 3 to 1 for large-scale text. A heading is not large text just because it is a heading. Normal body text is not large text. The standard excludes inactive controls, pure decoration, text that nobody can see, and logotypes.

WCAG 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast, also Level AA, asks for 3 to 1 in two cases. One is the parts of a control that a person needs to see to find it and its state. The other is the parts of a graphic needed to understand it. Borders of input fields and icons that carry meaning are examples. Text inside a graphic still follows 1.4.3. At the time of writing (checked 2026-10-25), Material also asks for 3 to 1 between a button's container and the background. A standalone element such as a floating action button is the exception.

The formula needs three steps. First, change each colour channel into a number c from 0 to 1 by dividing its value, from 0 to 255, by 255. This step uses <GlossaryTerm term="sRGB" definition="The standard way web screens describe colour, as red, green and blue values." /> channels. If c is 0.04045 or less, use c divided by 12.92. Otherwise, use ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) to the power 2.4.

Second, the <GlossaryTerm term="relative luminance" definition="How much light a colour gives off, from 0 for black to 1 for white." /> L is 0.2126 times red plus 0.7152 times green plus 0.0722 times blue. Third, the ratio is (L of the lighter colour + 0.05) divided by (L of the darker colour + 0.05).

Take #767676 on white. Each channel is 118, and 118 / 255 is 0.4627. Then ((0.4627 + 0.055) / 1.055) to the power 2.4 is 0.1812, so L is 0.1812. White has L equal to 1. The ratio is 1.05 / 0.2312, which is 4.54 to 1.

On a phone, turn the calculator sideways to get its scientific keys. A grey has the same value in all three channels, so step one is done once, and L is that number. Work out 0.4627 + 0.055, divide by 1.055, then use the power key for 2.4. Then divide 1.05 by L + 0.05. For a colour with different channels, do step one for each channel and use step two.

What kind of rule is this? A standard. WCAG 2.2 is a published standard, and the ratios are exact.

</GoDeeper>

## Colour alone is not a message

<InteractiveCanvas slug="product-m2-colour-alone" />

Switch between the two forms. In the first, the only sign of an error is a red tint. A person who cannot tell that tint from its neighbours misses it. In the second, a sentence says what to do, and the red adds to it. WCAG 1.4.1 says colour must not be the only way to show something.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: never colour alone">

WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Color, Level A, says colour must not be the only visual means of showing information. This includes indicating an action, prompting a response and telling one element from another. Add a shape, an icon or text. WCAG 3.3.1 Error Identification, also Level A, says that when an error is found automatically, the item in error is identified and the error is described in text. Colour may add to the text, and it may not replace it.

Common failures in student projects are easy to list. A red border is the whole error message. Seats are shown as a green or red dot with no words. A link inside a paragraph differs from the text around it only by colour. A chart tells its lines apart only by colour. In each case, a word, an icon or a different shape fixes it.

The standards describe broad needs: low vision, differences in colour vision, bright light and small or cheap screens. We do not state a share of users in this course, because we did not open a source for one. The practical point does not need a number. If the message disappears when you view the screen in black and white, the message depended on colour.

What kind of rule is this? A standard at Level A, plus the NN/g heuristic that an error message should sit near its cause, be plain and say what to do.

</GoDeeper>

## Check each text pair in a loop

<MermaidDiagram title="The contrast loop for one text pair" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Take one text pair on the screen"] --> B["Look up or compute its ratio"]
  B --> C{"Does it meet its bar?"}
  C -->|"No"| D["Darken the text or lighten the background"]
  D --> B
  C -->|"Yes"| E{"Is the message also in words?"}
  E -->|"No"| F["Add words, an icon or a shape"]
  E -->|"Yes"| G["Next pair"]
  F --> G
`} legend="Bars: 4.5 to 1 for normal text. 3 to 1 for large text, and for borders and icons that carry meaning." />

Run the loop for every text pair on the screen: main text, secondary text, button label, link, error and hint. Measure the pair, not the colour alone, and write the ratio in your notes. Then check dark mode if the product has one.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to keep the checks cheap">

You do not need to measure every colour on the screen. Most screens have a small set of text pairs. Make a short table of those pairs and measure each one once. When you change a colour, measure again only the pairs that use it.

Run the accent check by squinting: how many strong colour shapes do you see? Run the colour-alone check by taking a screenshot and viewing it in grayscale on your phone if it has that setting, or by asking whether every message also has words. Run the dark mode check by opening the screen in dark mode if your product has one.

A contrast checker is a tool fact that changes. This course does not name one. Use the table in this chapter for greys on white, or any checker that shows the ratio to two decimals. Repeat one measurement by hand with the formula so that you know it is right.

</GoDeeper>
