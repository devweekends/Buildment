This is the gate for module 2. It asks what changed in how you look at a screen. The work on a screen was in chapter 2.6, so this gate has one short writing task and asks you to think. Plan about 30 minutes. Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

## Module 2 checklist

- [ ] I can squint at a screen, say what I see first, and say whether that is what the person needs first
- [ ] I keep a screen to three text sizes or fewer, with body text at 16 or more and a line height of about 1.5
- [ ] I put less space inside a group than between groups, and I take my gaps from one step
- [ ] I measure the contrast of every text pair and I know the bars: 4.5 to 1, 3 to 1 for large text, and never colour alone
- [ ] I check Arabic text with a native reader on a real phone instead of copying numbers for Latin letters
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 2.2 to 2.5, finished the practice in 2.6 and the quiz in 2.7

## Learning log

1. **Your first thing:** Think of the last screen you built or used a lot. What did a person see first on it, and was that what you wanted? How would you find out without asking yourself?
2. **Your numbers:** Which number from this module will you remember, and what will you use it for? It could be 16, 1.5, 3 sizes, 4.5 to 1, or a ratio you measured yourself.
3. **Your surprise:** What did you believe about colour, spacing or type that turned out different when you measured or squinted? For example, a grey that looked dark enough.
4. **Your check:** Of the checks in this module, which one are you most likely to skip on a tired evening? What will you do to remember it?

## What this module changed

At the start of the module, Nadia opened ten project pages and remembered one. Every page said true things. What differed was which thing came first. You have now learned the idea behind that: a screen has an order, and the order comes from signals that you can choose. Size, weight, contrast, position and grouping decide what a person reads first.

You also learned to check instead of trust. The squint test takes away the words and shows you the order. A ratio takes away the opinion and shows you a number. A grey that looked dark enough turned out to be 4.48 to 1, and the standard counts that as a fail. Rules such as three sizes, a line height of 1.5, or an 8 point grid are guides from experts and design systems. You can use them as starting points, and you test them on your own screen with a real person.

The gate does not ask you to fix a screen again. You did that in the practice. It asks you to look back at how you think about a screen, and to say what you see now that you did not see before. Your mentor reads for the change in your thinking, and not for a perfect screen.

## Your answer

Write four short parts. First, what you looked at on a screen before this module, and what you ignored. Second, what you see now on a screen of your choice: your own screen from chapter 2.6, or Wasel's landing page from the same chapter. Say what comes first when you squint, and give one number you measured. Third, one rule from this module that you would test before you trust it, and how you would test it. Fourth, one check you will run on every screen from now on, and why that one.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. Qusai studies at a coding bootcamp and builds small shop pages for freelance clients. Before this module, he looked at whether a page looked modern, and he ignored sizes and spacing because he used the framework's defaults. Now he squints at a page for a client's print shop and sees a purple banner first and the prices not at all. The number he measured was the price text: #999999 on white, which is 2.85 to 1, below the bar of 4.5.

The rule he would test is "no more than three sizes". It is a guide from one expert source, and he would test it by counting the sizes on his page and asking a friend which line they read first. The check he will run every time is the squint, because it takes only a few seconds and he can do it on his phone in a bus queue. He says he picked it because it finds the problem before he spends an hour fixing the wrong thing.

Look at the shape of Qusai's answer. A specific old habit, a specific new one, a number he measured himself, a rule treated as a guide, and a check he can really run. This gate gives you less help than the practice on purpose. For each of your four parts, ask whether a mentor could see evidence in what you wrote. A number from your own screen is evidence. The words "I notice more now" are not.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads for four things. They look first for a number you measured, because it shows that you used the tools and did not only read about them. Then they look for a rule you would test, because that shows you treat guides as guides. They also look for a habit that you can really run, and for an old habit that you describe honestly. A weak answer says that the screen "looks cleaner and more professional". Another weak answer lists the rules of the module without one example from your own screen.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before and now | A specific old habit and a specific new way of looking, with an example | Both named, but vague or without an example | Only one of the two, or neither |
| A measured number | A size, gap or ratio from your own screen or Wasel's page, used as evidence | A number given, but not from a screen you looked at | No number |
| A rule you would test | One rule named, plus a way to test it with a squint, a formula or a person | A rule named, but no way to test it | No rule, or a rule treated as always true |
| A habit you will run | One check, why it is the one, and when you will run it | One check named, but no reason | Nothing you could really do |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at most one part is "Partly".

After you submit, the mentor replies with one or two short questions. A common one is: "Which number from your own screen would change your mind about a design, and why?" A good reply points to a ratio, a size or a squint you can show. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a link to your screenshots.
