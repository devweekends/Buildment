This is the gate for module 2. It asks what changed in how you look at a screen. The work on a screen was in chapter 2.6, so this gate has one short writing task and some questions about how you think. Plan about 35 minutes. Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

## Module 2 checklist

- [ ] I can squint at a screen, say what I see first, and say whether that is what the person needs first
- [ ] I keep a screen to three text sizes or fewer, with body text at 16 or more and a line height of about 1.5
- [ ] I put less space inside a group than between groups, and I take my gaps from one step
- [ ] I measure every text pair, and I know the bars: 4.5 to 1, 3 to 1 for large text, and never colour alone
- [ ] I check Arabic text with a native reader on a real phone instead of copying numbers for Latin letters
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 2.2 to 2.5, finished the practice in 2.6 and the quiz in 2.7

## Learning log

1. **Your first thing:** Think of the last screen you built or used a lot. What did a person see first on it, and was that what you wanted? How would you find out without asking yourself?
2. **Your numbers:** Which number from this module will you remember, and what will you use it for? It could be 16, 1.5, 3 sizes, 4.5 to 1, or a ratio you measured yourself.
3. **Your surprise:** What did you believe about colour, spacing or type that turned out different when you measured or squinted? For example, a grey that looked dark enough.
4. **Your check:** Of the checks in this module, which one are you most likely to skip on a tired evening? What will you do to remember it?

## What this module changed

At the start of the module, Nadia opened ten project pages and remembered none of them. Every page said true things. What differed was which thing came first. You have now learned the idea behind that: a screen has an order, and the order comes from signals that you can choose. Size, weight, contrast, position and grouping decide what a person reads first.

You also learned to check instead of trust. The squint test takes away the words and shows you the order. A ratio takes away the opinion and shows you a number. A grey that looked dark enough turned out to be 4.48 to 1, and the standard counts that as a fail. Guides such as three sizes, a line height of 1.5 or an 8 point grid come from experts and design systems. You can use them as starting points, and you test them on your own screen with a real person.

One more change is about who can run a check. In this module every check was small enough for a phone. You used a squint on a screenshot, a table of greys, your phone's text size set to 200 percent, and a native reader for Arabic text. None of them needs a laptop or a paid tool. That is on purpose. A check that you can run anywhere is a check that you will really run. From now on, when you look at a screen, ask three questions. What comes first? Can everyone read it? Which gap groups which parts?

## Your answer: a note to a friend

In the practice you fixed your own screen. In this gate you look at a screen that someone else made, and you write to them. This is how most design feedback works in a team, and it is harder than fixing your own work, because you cannot change the screen yourself. You can only say what you see and why.

Pick one screen that you did not make. It can be an app on your phone, a page that a friend built, or Wasel's landing page from chapter 2.6, which you can squint at in the picture there. Write a note to the person who made it, in four parts. First, what you see when you squint, and whether it is what a user needs first. Second, the one change you would make first, the rule behind it and one number that you measured or counted. Third, one rule from this module that you would test here before you trust it, or would not apply, and why. Fourth, one check you would run on a real phone before you send the note.

Choose a screen that you know well enough to say what its user needs. If you choose Wasel, remember its job: a student with an assignment due tomorrow morning wants to send a file from a phone and collect it printed. An app that you use every day is often a better choice than a page that you have never seen, because you already know what you want from it.

Keep the note kind. A good note names what the screen already does well in one sentence, and then gives one change instead of ten. You may dictate it with your phone's microphone.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor looks first for the number. A note that says the text is "too small" is weaker than a note that says the facts are 11 and the body is not 16. Then they look for the rule behind the change, and for whether you treat that rule as a guide and not as a law. They also check the last part, because a check you cannot run on a phone is not a check. The most common weak note says the screen "looks outdated" and lists five changes with no numbers. The usual follow-up question is: "If the maker disagreed with your first change, what would you show them to change their mind?" A strong reply points to a squint, a ratio or a person.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| What you see first | What the squint shows is named, and compared with what the user needs first | The squint is described, but not compared with the user's need | No squint, or only "it looks busy" |
| One change | One change, its rule and a number from the screen | A change and a rule, but the number is missing or guessed | No rule, or several changes with no order |
| A rule you would test | One rule named, with a way to test it or a reason not to apply it here | A rule named, but no way to test it | A rule treated as always true |
| A check on a phone | One check that a phone can do, with what a pass looks like | A check named, but a laptop or tool is needed | No check |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at most one part is "Partly".
