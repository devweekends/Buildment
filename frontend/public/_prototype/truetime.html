10.6 · PROTOTYPE · VISUAL FIRST

TrueTime: when a timestamp is a range

Two writes, 1 ms apart

Server A stamps its write .000. Server B stamps its write .001.

So A came first. Or did it?

Every clock is a little wrong

The black line is the real time. The dot is what the clock shows. They drift apart.

A careful clock does not lie about it: it says "now is somewhere in this band". The half-width of the band is called epsilon.

Go deeper

Google's TrueTime returns exactly this: TT.now() gives earliest and latest. Each machine syncs to GPS and atomic clock masters every 30 seconds; between syncs it assumes a worst-case drift, so epsilon grows like a sawtooth, from about 1 ms to 7 ms, and is about 4 ms most of the time (Spanner paper, OSDI 2012).

If the bands overlap, nobody knows the order

Overlapping bands mean the clocks cannot prove which write came first.

Only a gap bigger than both bands together gives a sure answer.

The bug: Bob sees a photo he should not

Alice unfriends Bob, sees "done", then posts a photo. Server A's clock runs fast and B's runs slow.

So the photo gets the earlier timestamp. A reader in between sees the photo and still sees Bob as a friend.

Go deeper

A snapshot read at time t returns every write stamped at or before t. Here s2 = 22.4 ms and s1 = 27.6 ms, so a snapshot at 25 ms includes the photo but not the unfriend. Alice and Bob are a made-up example.

The fix: wait out the doubt

Server A picks the top of its band as the timestamp, then waits until even the bottom of its band is past it. Only then does Alice see "done".

That wait is about 2 × epsilon. Anything Alice does next is guaranteed a later timestamp.

Go deeper

This is Spanner's commit wait: TT.after(s) must be true before the commit becomes visible. Better clocks mean smaller epsilon, so every write waits less. The paper measured a wait of about 5 ms, and Spanner overlaps it with replication so users do not pay all of it on top.
