## 2. Conventions used in this plan

**Positions.** Options are shown in the order written. Position 0 is the first option (A), 1 is B, 2 is C, 3 is D. Every quiz item has four options; every predict has two (position 0 or 1).

**Length classes (key against the other options, counted in characters).**
- `S`: the key is the shortest option.
- `M`: the key is neither the shortest nor the longest.
- `L`: the key is the longest option, and never more than 10 percent longer than the longest distractor (aim for 5 percent or less).
- In every item all four options are written at the same level of detail and within about 15 percent of each other, so the class cannot be read from a glance. A writer who cannot hit the class without padding changes the options, not the class. If a class and a position are both unreachable for one item, swap the class with another item in the same quiz and tell the orchestrator.

**Scenarios.** Every quiz item and quick check puts a named, fictional actor in a role on a project and asks for a decision. Names, roles and projects were chosen so none repeats a scene person (Bashar, Ruqaya, Tawfiq, Sireen, Moath, Waed, Amjad, Diala, Ismail) or their exact case, none repeats Areej's numbers, and no actor name appears anywhere else in `courses/` or `agent-ip/` (grep, 2026-10-25), so none matches a name used in courses 13 and 14. Each chapter marks its actors once as fictional. Predicts keep the brief's seeds (Bashar, Areej and others) because they are built on the chapter's own worked example, and the quiz never reuses them.

**Opinion.** No item grades opinion. Every key follows from something the chapter teaches (the chapter and beat are named in each spec) or from arithmetic (checked by script, see section 9).

**Explanations.** Every option of every MCQ gets its own one-line reason (one per line, standing alone), as `04-interactive-playbook.md` requires. The plan names the real mistake behind each wrong option; the writer turns it into the reason.

**Dated facts.** Items that rest on a platform fact (Google sign-in, LinkedIn rules, free-tier behaviour, Google and duplicate copies) say "as in this chapter, checked 2026-10" in the stem and are re-checked by the re-check owner before publish (open decision 17).

**Quiz chapters hold activities only** (five `quiz` fences, no lesson text, no "Quiz:" in the title). Titles are the module topic, as in the roadmap.

## 3. Quiz chapters (9 chapters, 5 items each, 45 items)

Each item header reads: `Q<chapter>#<n> | pos <0 to 3> | len <S, M or L> | <level> | <outcome>`. Item 5 of every quiz is the "check the AI's answer" item (the brief asks for one per quiz; the pack requires at least one in modules 1, 3, 6, 7 and 8, and all nine have one). In each spec: **Skill** (with the chapter and beat that teaches it), **Seed** (actor, role, project, facts the writer may use), **Decision** (what the learner chooses), **Key**, and **Wrong options** (the real mistake behind each; the writer gives each one its own reason line).

### 3.1 Chapter 1.5, What a reader sees first (O1)

**Q1.5#1** | pos 2 | len M | application | O1
- Skill: pick the arrangement that puts the strongest checkable proof on the first phone screen (1.2 B3).
- Seed: Nasser, a data analyst. Two-page resume: an objective paragraph, a skills list of 24 tools, experience, and on page 2 a dashboard project that cut his team's weekly reporting time (he kept a timing sheet). One labelled link exists, at the bottom.
- Decision: which order of blocks for the first screen.
- Key: name and one labelled link to the dashboard write-up, one line with the result, then experience; skills shrink to one line at the end.
- Wrong options: (A) the objective paragraph stays first because a summary frames the reader; (B) the skills block stays first, trimmed to his ten best tools, because a reader scans for tools; (D) cut everything to one line because readers give "six seconds" (the myth).

**Q1.5#2** | pos 0 | len S | application | O1
- Skill: tell a line a stranger can check from a line that is only a claim (1.3 B3).
- Seed: Rola, a mobile developer, drafts four profile lines. The founder has five minutes and will not message her first.
- Decision: which one line can the founder check without asking Rola.
- Key: a line with a before and an after, a window and a named source (for example a crash rate in the store console, 30 days either side).
- Wrong options: (B) a number with no base, window or source ("improved app speed by 40 percent"); (C) a "we" line that credits a great team and says nothing checkable; (D) a tool list with years, which looks like proof and is only a claim.

**Q1.5#3** | pos 3 | len L | application | O1
- Skill: keep the useful habit and drop the false number (1.2 B2).
- Seed: Hind, a QA engineer. A classmate says recruiters give a resume six seconds, so Hind should cut hers to one line.
- Decision: the honest reply.
- Key: nobody has a reliable number, but employers do say they get more applications than they can read closely, so put the strongest proof and one labelled link first and cut what does not help this reader.
- Wrong options: (A) agree, six seconds is proven; (B) swap it for a "better" number such as 7.4 seconds, which has the same problem; (C) readers read everything, so order does not matter (overcorrection).

**Q1.5#4** | pos 1 | len M | application | O1
- Skill: place a certificate and a shipped project (1.3 B4).
- Seed: Sufian, a backend developer. One shipped project with a measured result, three course certificates, a one-page resume.
- Decision: where each goes.
- Key: the project, with its result and a link, near the top; the certificates as one small line near the end.
- Wrong options: (A) a certificate block as the headline, above the project; (C) drop the project because it is "only personal work" and list the certificates (checkable and claim reversed); (D) both side by side with equal space (avoids deciding).

**Q1.5#5** | pos 2 | len S | analysis | O1 | CHECK THE AI'S ANSWER
- Skill: spot advice that fixes the wrong thing (1.4 B3, B4, B5).
- Seed: Maysaa, a frontend developer who writes English as a second language, asks an AI assistant how to avoid AI detectors. It says to remove words like "delve" and "realm" and add a few spelling mistakes.
- Decision: what is wrong with the advice.
- Key: word lists date, detectors often misjudge second-language writing, and the real fix is specifics only she could write.
- Wrong options: (A) nothing, detectors read word choice so removing the words works; (B) only the spelling mistakes are wrong, the word swap is sound; (D) wrong because detectors are accurate and no edit can fool them.

### 3.3 Chapter 3.7, Honest numbers and proof of work (O3)

**Q3.7#1** | pos 3 | len S | application | O3
- Skill: choose the complete claim card (3.2 B3).
- Seed: Idris, a backend developer at a retail platform, shortened a search page's load time. Seed numbers: median load 9 s before, 2 s after, 14 days either side, team dashboard owned by his lead; he changed the query, his lead reviewed it; a banner release landed mid-window and traffic was flat.

**Q3.7#2** | pos 1 | len M | analysis | O3
- Skill: question an average (3.4 B1, B2).
- Seed: Fidaa, a support engineer, is shown a tile "average response time 120 ms" on a service she does not run.
- Decision: the most useful next question.
- Key: which statistic is that, and what do p95 and p99 show over the same window.
- Wrong options: (A) can she round it to 100 ms for her resume (rounding is deception); (C) is it quicker than other companies' services (a benchmark, no definition); (D) who is the most senior person who reads the dashboard (authority instead of definition).

**Q3.7#3** | pos 2 | len L | analysis | O3
- Skill: percentiles do not average (3.4 B5).
- Seed: Louay, an SRE intern, sees "average p95 across 3 servers: 220 ms", built from p95 values of 180, 200 and 280 ms (arithmetic checks: 660 / 3 = 220).
- Decision: what is wrong and what to ask for.
- Key: percentiles cannot be averaged; ask for the p95 over all requests together, or for the bucket counts summed first.
- Wrong options: (A) nothing, averaging smooths out noise per server; (B) take the highest of the three (280 ms) as the real p95; (D) take the median of the three (200 ms) because it ignores the outlier.

**Q3.7#4** | pos 0 | len M | application | O3
- Skill: choose the before-and-after design that supports "my change did it" (3.5 B1, B2).
- Seed: Salah, a backend developer, added a cache to an events endpoint and wants to say it made the endpoint faster.
- Decision: which test design.
- Key: same load script, same data, same machine and same arrival rate before and after, with the cache as the only change.
- Wrong options: (B) compare this month's dashboard with last month's (five other changes inside); (C) run the new version once and quote it (one run misleads); (D) compare it with a friend's similar service (different data and machine).

**Q3.7#5** | pos 1 | len S | analysis | O3 | CHECK THE AI'S ANSWER
- Skill: time saved is net, not gross (3.3 B4; 3.5 B5).
- Seed: Tamara, an operations analyst, wrote a script for a weekly report. Seed numbers: the task runs 12 times a week; 9 minutes each before, 2 minutes each after (including the check); 15 minutes a week of upkeep. An AI tool says "9 x 12 = 108 minutes saved a week".
- Arithmetic: (9 - 2) x 12 = 84; 84 - 15 = 69 minutes a week.
- Decision: what is wrong.
- Key: it is gross, not net; with the 2-minute check and the upkeep the saving is 69 minutes a week.
- Wrong options: (A) 108 is right, her own upkeep time does not count; (C) 84 is right, upkeep never belongs in a saving; (D) 93, which is 108 minus the upkeep and forgets the 2-minute check.

## 6. Gates (9)

### How every gate works

- **One open question per gate** (blueprint rule): `submissionMode: text`, `allowSpeechInput: true`. Where the gate has an AI drill (1.6, 3.8, 5.8, 6.8), the written option is a clearly marked optional part of the same prompt ("If you did not finish the AI drill, also answer..."), so the written path completes the gate and the chapter keeps one open question.
- **Learner-facing text** shows the rubric parts with their Met, Partly and Not yet descriptors in plain words ("How your mentor will read it"), as the gate example does, so the learner can check their own answer first. The mentor key, Areej's model answer and the follow-up bank are server-only (the `modelAnswer` field and mentor notes), never in the chapter body.
- **Mentor follow-up.** The mentor reads the answer part by part, then marks it `NEEDS_REVISION` with one to three short questions; the learner answers by resubmitting. The reply is part of the gate and its time is inside each gate's estimate. A gate is complete when its pass rule holds and any follow-up is answered.
- **Review depth** (roadmap open decision 23): full review for gates 1.6, 3.8, 6.8 and the capstone; spot-check (read the rubric parts that carry the pass rule, sample one more) for 2.7, 4.7, 5.8, 7.7 and 8.8.
- **Learning logs and checklists** are never graded and never required to contain personal hardship. Nothing in a gate rubric asks for, or scores, hardship.
- **`minWords` is set at about 60 to 70 percent of the expected answer length**, not at it, so a short, complete answer is not padded. The briefs said 100 for every gate; this plan lowers it where the expected answer is shorter than 150 words. The gate prose says the expected length in words as guidance ("about 120 words").
- **Flags.** The five flags are vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation. The mentor key says which apply to Areej's model answer, and which a mentor should watch for in learners' answers.
- **Areej.** Every Areej fact below is canon (`module-briefs/00-areej-and-cast.md`). Where canon has no fact (for example the result of her second ten-second test), the key says so and the orchestrator adds the fact to the canon before the chapter is written.

## 7. AI drills (gates 1.6, 3.8, 5.8, 6.8 and the defence in 9.4)

### How the drills work

- Five uses of the existing `AI_INTERVIEW` block (`docs/13-ai-interview.mdx`). All are `required: false` because the block needs the learner's own OpenRouter key. The written option completes the same gate at 1.6, 3.8, 5.8 and 6.8; at 9.4 a recorded walkthrough or a written defence plus a short live call completes the chapter. - **What the learner pastes** comes from the career arc, the claim ledger, the case study or the resume, with names and contact details removed. The gate prose lists what never to paste: full name, phone, email, address, ID numbers, passwords, keys, client or employer names, private company data, other people's details.
- **There is no numeric score.** The feedback card gives strengths, what to do better with a fix, a note per focus area, "Things to watch" (flags with the learner's own words) and three next steps. - **Comparability.** The 1.6 and 6.8 rubrics use the same probes and flag names on purpose, so the baseline and the new resume can be compared. The 9.4 rubric adds the first real user, the sleeping demo and "what next".
- **Rubric rules met by every rubric below** (checked by script on 2026-10-25): length 600 to 1,400 characters; one line, no line breaks, no double quotes, no placeholder markers; (1) says what a strong answer shows for the module; (2) probes how each number was measured ("How did you measure it? ## 9. Blind key check plan

**Scope.** Every option-based item is re-answered blind: the 45 quiz items, the 28 quick checks and the 28 predicts (101 items), after the chapter writers have written them from this plan. The plan's keys are the drafters' keys; they are not checked until this step is done.

**Who.** A reviewer from the second model family (`routing.yaml`: `blind_key_check`, keyless batches only; the drafting family is not independent, even a smaller model). A person checks too, because the drafters and critic B share a family (recorded risk R1).

**How.**
1. `node agent-ip/scripts/blind-keys.mjs extract courses/career-brand <out-dir> --batches 2` writes `batch-1.md`, `batch-2.md` (questions and lettered options, no keys, no explanations) and `keys.json`.
2. Give the reviewer only the batch files. The reviewer writes `answers-1.md`, `answers-2.md`, one line per item: `<item id> | <letter> | <high, medium or low> | <note>`.
3. `node agent-ip/scripts/blind-keys.mjs compare <out-dir>` prints mismatches, missing answers and every item not marked high, and exits 1 on any mismatch.
4. Any disagreement is a defect until the stem, the options or the key is fixed; ambiguity counts as a disagreement. Open every medium or low item in its chapter: if the context the reviewer needed sits in the prose just above the question, record that and keep the item; otherwise rewrite it.

## 10. Integrity, canvas pairing, issues for the orchestrator, and weakly assessed outcomes

### 10.1 Integrity risks and what changes the risk

| Risk | Mitigation in this plan |
|---|---|
| A learner pastes a gate prompt into a model | Every rubric part asks for the learner's own source, place and share (where the number lives, what they did apart from the team); the follow-up asks "where would I click?"; the capstone defence asks about lines the mentor picks |
| Guessing or sharing quiz answers | Scenario items with specific facts; four plausible options; per-option reasons; platform quiz signals go to mentors only and never change a score; quizzes are formative and carry no weight beyond completion |
| Inventing a number to look strong | An honest "not measured, here is how I would" is Met in every rubric that asks for sources; the drills and rubrics never push for a guess; invented reason risk is a named flag |
| Self-reported peer tests (ten-second test, stranger test, say-it-back) | The answer must state who, which device, the time and what was said; 7.7 recommends a different viewer than 1.6; the mentor opens the public link themselves |
| Using Areej's material as one's own | The kit index says which parts are Areej's (open decision 1); the arc, project plan, resume, profile and website plan must be the learner's own |
| Drill flags that are wrong | A flag survives only if its quote is in a learner answer; 9.2 teaches the learner to notice wrong feedback; flags are feedback, not a pass criterion |
| Dated facts go stale | Each platform item says "as in this chapter, checked 2026-10"; section 9 step 6 names the items and the re-check owner |

### 10.2 Canvas pairing (prediction before, transfer check after)

### 10.5 Outcomes I could not assess well

- **O5 (show your work).** The deployable unit, the instant access and the short demo are plans, not running things; a mentor cannot verify from a plan that a demo account is safe, and the stranger test is self-reported. Mitigations: the six-control list must be written out, the case study link is opened by the mentor, a deployed demo is a should-have. Residual risk stays.
- **O7 (LinkedIn used within the rules, measured rhythm).** Only the profile, drafts and a plan are visible; the eight-week log and real outreach happen after the course. The rules are checked by the learner's own statement and the drafts. Residual risk stays; a mentor check-in at week eight outside the course would close it.
- **O8 (a site that is clearly theirs; readers judge them by their writing).** A website plan and one post are evidence; whether readers judge them well, and whether a site is clearly theirs, cannot be tested in the course. The say-it-back listener is unseen.
- **O2 (the career arc).** The gate sees one pillar, two proofs and a privacy list, not the nine-part document or every role card. The rest is checked only indirectly, through the ledger mapping at 6.8 and the profile at 7.7.
- **O9 on the written path.** Without a key there are no flags; the comparison is the learner's own account, and the fix at 9.4 is self-reported. Mentor judgement carries more weight here than on the drill path.
- **O1 and the ten-second test.** The test is a course method, not a research finding, and depends on the viewer. It is used as a baseline and a before-and-after, not as proof.
- **O3 (read numbers you did not set up).** Quizzes use written scenarios; only the optional no-install path (Areej's export) practises a real dashboard export, and it is not separately graded.

### 10.6 Feedback plan

- **Quiz, quick check and predict:** immediate, auto-graded on the server, one reason per option shown after answering; a wrong key teaches the real mistake, not just the right letter.
- **Gates:** delayed and specific. The mentor names the rubric part, quotes the learner's words as evidence and gives one next action; follow-up questions are answered by resubmitting.
- **Drills:** the feedback card (strengths, fixes, level words, flags with quotes, three next steps) is immediate; the mentor reads it with the transcript.
- **Fading:** modules 1 to 3 give Areej's worked answers and templates for each gate; modules 4 to 6 ask for the same parts with shorter worked examples; modules 7 to 9 remove the template and ask the learner to find their own gaps (the capstone asks the learner to choose the next step). The gate prose says so, so the learner sees it as progress.
