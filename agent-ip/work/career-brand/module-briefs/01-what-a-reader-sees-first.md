# Module 1: What a reader sees first

## Outcome
The learner can say what a busy founder or hiring manager is likely to check first, tell a line a stranger can check from a line that is only a claim, explain why fluent AI-made writing now says little, and has recorded a baseline of their own kit: a ten-second test result and either the baseline AI drill flags or written answers to "how was that measured?" and "what was your part?" for three resume lines (O1, start of O9).

## Why this module exists
Fellows apply and hear nothing back. Before rebuilding anything, they need to see their current kit as a reader does and have a baseline to compare with at the capstone. This is diagnosis only (debug-first): no practice chapter, no new kit piece. Why before what: the reader's situation (too many applications, cheap fluent text) comes first; the arrangement habit follows from it.

## Prerequisites
Entry: work-history, resume-draft (or Areej's old resume, canon), linkedin-account. Nothing earlier in the course.

## Cross-links and limits
- Course 7, "Professional & Business Communication", for first LinkedIn basics: mention once in 1.1 for fellows who do not yet have a profile.
- Never teach as fact (unverified table, research.md): "six seconds" or "7.4 seconds"; "75 percent of resumes rejected by robots"; "99 percent of Fortune 500 use an ATS"; any vendor survey percentage about hiring managers and AI ("80 percent distrust", "a third can spot AI in 20 seconds"); "42 percent prefer problem solving over certificates" or "73 percent prefer a portfolio"; "68 percent can identify AI content (HBR)"; GitHub contribution squares as a hiring signal.
- Credits for this module (at most two or three names a chapter): Marlow, Dabbish and Herbsleb, and Capiluppi, Serebrenik and Singer (1.2); Wiles, Munyikwa and Horton, Galdin and Silbert, Cui, Dias and Ye (1.4, pick two or three); Jakesch, Hancock and Naaman; Liang and colleagues (1.4). Employers are described without names: "a software company that publishes how it hires".

## 1.2 The first look is short (Learn, 20 min, stepType Learn, format: beats)
Outcome: arrange material so the strongest proof is seen first, one link away, without relying on the six-second myth.

| # | Heading | Claim the picture must show | Visual | GoDeeper fold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | More applications than time to read | One reader, a pile of applications far taller than a working day; only a few get a close read | Watch-only scene | One software company that publishes how it hires says it gets too many applications to review work from all of them, screens by cover letter and resume, and gives a small work test to about 1 to 3 percent [S8]. One company, so an example, not a rule. Phrase: "Nobody has a reliable number for how long a reader looks at a resume. What employers do tell us is that they receive far more applications than they can read closely, so the first look is quick and your best proof must be easy to find." |
| B2 | The famous six seconds has no source | The "6 seconds" claim traced back: a company eye-tracking study, sample not disclosed, measuring the decision to keep reading | Static MermaidDiagram (claim to company study 2012 and 2018 to "sample and jobs not disclosed") | [S4, S5]: the 2018 report does not say how many recruiters, which jobs or how long the resumes were; the 7.4 second figure is about deciding to keep reading, not judging. Say: treat it as an arrangement habit, not a statistic. |
| B3 (predict before; PLAYABLE) | Your first screen decides what they see | A phone frame; the learner drags blocks (objective paragraph, skills list, best proof line with link, experience, links) into order and the frame shows what is visible before scrolling; verdict "Best proof seen" or "Best proof hidden" | Playable canvas (block-map or sorter primitive) | The habit (research A, design implication 1): best proof first, one link, no scavenger hunt. Mechanism: a phone shows a fraction of a page; whatever fills it is what a quick reader keeps. Failure mode: a long objective paragraph or a skills block takes the first screen. |
| B4 | One labelled link, not thirty repositories | A reader faced with 31 repositories gives up; a reader given one link labelled with what they will see opens it | Watch-only scene | People who judge developers online look at activity over time and who you worked with, not a count of green squares [S1, S2] (S1 read at abstract level: do not quote counts). Phrase: "People who judge developers online look at what you actually did, over time, and who you worked with. They can also tell when a profile was arranged to look good, so keep it real." |
| B5 | The ten-second test | A partner looks at the learner's phone for ten seconds, then says what they remember; the learner writes it down and compares it with their best proof | Watch-only scene | Course method, labelled as such: the ten seconds are a practice limit, not a research finding. How to run it alone (a timer, then write what you remember) and with a partner. This is the gate 1.6 task. |

- Predict before B3: "Bashar's best work is on page 2. When a founder opens the file on a phone, what do they see first?" Two options in everyday words.
- Quick check after B5 (one or two questions): two orders of the same resume; which puts checkable proof on the first screen; why the other fails.
- Worked example: Bashar's resume reordered: the queue import line (with "stopped the nightly import from timing out" and no invented number) moved under his name with one labelled link; the objective paragraph cut; the skills block shortened to one line at the end. No numbers are added that the canon does not give.
- Avoid: any number of seconds as fact; GitHub squares; "the robot rejects you".

## 1.3 What founders and hiring managers look for (Learn, 25 min, stepType Learn, format: beats)
Outcome: name the signals employers who publish their hiring describe, and say where a certificate fits.

## 1.4 When writing is cheap, proof counts (Learn, 20 min, stepType Learn, format: beats)
Outcome: explain why fluent AI-made text says less, why detectors misjudge second-language English, and what only the learner can write.

| # | Heading | Claim the picture must show | Visual | GoDeeper fold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | Fixing mistakes helped in one study | A resume with error marks turning clean; a hire counter rises slightly | Watch-only scene | A field experiment with nearly half a million jobseekers on one platform: algorithmic help that corrected common errors raised hiring by about 8 percent with no drop in employer satisfaction; the tool did not write new text; two authors have ties to the platform [S12]. Phrase: "Writing help that fixes mistakes has been linked to more hires in one large study. The help in that study corrected errors. It did not invent claims." |
| B2 | When everyone writes smoothly, smooth proves less | A stack of identical polished letters; the reader's attention moves to the one line with a checkable result | Watch-only scene | After an AI cover-letter tool arrived on one platform, the link between how well a letter matched the job and callbacks fell (reported as 51 percent) and employers leaned on other signals such as work history [S14]; a second paper finds the premium for customised applications disappeared [S13]. Both read at abstract level; S13's 19 and 14 percent figures come from a model, so do not quote them. Teach the direction only. Phrase: "When anyone can produce a smooth cover letter in a minute, a smooth letter proves less. Readers lean on things they can check: what you built, what the numbers were, who vouches for it." |
| B3 (predict before) | People cannot reliably spot AI text | Readers guessing "human" or "AI" for profiles; their hits land near a coin flip | Watch-only scene | Six experiments with 4,600 participants: people could not reliably detect AI-written self-presentations, and relied on cues like pronouns and contractions that are unreliable [S16]. A related experiment found distrust of AI-labelled profiles only in mixed sets [S15]. Vendor surveys disagree with each other and are company claims [S22]: do not quote them. Phrase: "Nobody can reliably tell if a profile was written with AI, not even the people who try. So the question is not 'will they catch me'. The question is 'does this say anything only I could say'." |
| B4 | Detectors flag second-language English | A detector stamps "AI" on a careful second-language essay and passes a native speaker's essay | Watch-only scene | Several widely used detectors misclassified more than half of essays by non-native speakers as AI-generated, while classifying US eighth-grade essays accurately [S17] (61 percent is from press coverage: say "more than half"). What to do if a screen says "AI": offer the working document, drafts and the evidence behind each claim. Phrase: "AI detectors often wrongly flag English written by non-native speakers. If someone tells you your plain English 'sounds like AI', that is a known weakness of the tools, not a verdict on you." |
| B5 (PLAYABLE) | Only you can write this | A short bio; the learner swaps each sentence between an empty version and a specific one; a counter shows "things a reader can check" rising from 0 to 3 | Playable canvas (beat-choice style toggles on the picture) | Patterns that make text empty, for orientation only [S20]: inflated significance, unnamed "experts", trailing "-ing" phrases, "not just X but also Y", lists of three, promotional adjectives. Word lists date by model era and one word proves nothing [S19, S20]: no banned list. The full edit pass is 6.4. AI may help edit; numbers, ownership and judgment must be the learner's (hard constraint 4). |

- Predict before B3: "In experiments, how well did people tell AI-written profiles from human-written ones?" (well, or about as well as guessing).
- Quick check: which of three bio lines says something only the writer could say.
- Worked example: Areej's old objective paragraph (canon) against her later USP line "A full-stack developer who measures before and after, and writes it down"; only the second can be followed by a question with an answer.
- Avoid: "recruiters spot AI in seconds"; any vendor percentage; teaching to sound less like AI; "AI screeners prefer AI resumes" as a real-market fact [S21].

## 1.5 What a reader sees first (Check, 10 min, stepType Check)
Activities only: five MCQs, one reason per option, varied correct positions, comparable option lengths. Test skills, not recall:
1. Choose the arrangement of a given resume that puts the strongest checkable proof on the first phone screen.
2. Sort a set of lines: which one can a founder check without asking (application of B3 in 1.3).
3. A classmate says "recruiters give you six seconds, so cut everything to one line". Choose the honest reply that keeps the useful habit and drops the false number.
4. A fellow has one shipped project and three course certificates: where each goes on a one-page resume.
5. **Check the AI's answer:** an AI assistant advises "to avoid AI detectors, remove words like 'delve' and 'realm' and add some spelling mistakes". Choose what is wrong with this advice (word lists date, one word proves nothing, detectors misjudge second-language writers, and specifics are the real fix).

## 1.6 Checklist and learning log (Gate, 50 min with the drill, 45 written, stepType Gate)
Purpose: baseline. Complete on submission when every part is present; nothing about the current kit's quality is graded.

Checklist:
- I ran the ten-second test on my current resume (or Areej's old resume) with a partner or a timer, and wrote down what was remembered.
- I ran it on my LinkedIn profile, or noted that I have none yet.
- I marked where my best proof sits (first screen, later, or missing).
- I removed my phone number, email, address and any client or employer secrets before pasting anything into the AI interviewer.
- I finished the baseline AI drill and copied its flags into my learning log, or I wrote the written baseline.
- I saved my baseline where I can find it in module 9.

Learning log (four prompts; the personal ones offer Areej's case):
1. What did the viewer remember first, and was it your best proof? (Or: what did Areej's classmate remember, canon.)
2. Name one line in your kit a stranger can check and one that is only a claim.
3. Which flag or written answer surprised you most, and why?
4. What is the first thing you will move or cut when you rebuild?

Written task (one openquestion, submissionMode text, minWords 100, allowSpeechInput true): report the ten-second test (what was seen, where the best proof sits, one change you will make). Optional section in the same answer, marked "If you did not finish the AI drill": for three lines of your current resume, answer "how was that measured?" and "what was your part?" (about 150 words); "I did not measure that" is a full answer when true.

Rubric parts (feedback only; the gate passes when every part is present):
| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test reported | Names at least two things the viewer remembered and the time limit used | Names one thing | No test result |
| Best proof located | Names the best proof and whether it was on the first screen | Names the proof but not where it sits | Neither |
| One change | One specific change tied to what the test showed | A change not tied to the test | None |
| Baseline recorded | Drill finished with flags copied, or all three lines answered with how-measured and own part (honest gaps allowed) | Drill started but not finished, or one or two lines answered | Missing |

Pass rule: all four parts at least Partly. Mentor follow-up: one question at most, on the change chosen.

AI drill (aiinterview fence): `context: resume`, `focus: [resume, impact, ownership]`, `maxTurns: 8`, `whyDepth: 3`, `required: false`. Opening question idea: "Pick one line on your resume you are proud of. What did you do, and how do you know it worked?" Rubric intent (server only, written by the assessment designer): probe every number for its source, separate "I" from "we", accept "I did not measure that" as honest. The block's flags (vague ownership, unsupported number, pseudo-story, invented reason risk, jargon without explanation) are the baseline. If the key fails or a request is refused: do the written section; never use a VPN.

ChapterRecap: three lines (the first look is short; checkable beats fluent; your baseline is saved).
