# Curriculum roadmap: Your Career Brand: Resume, LinkedIn, Personal Site and Proof of Work

## In one paragraph
Fellows apply and hear nothing back, because their best work is invisible: a GitHub link nobody runs, a resume full of "responsible for", numbers they cannot defend. Employers who publish how they hire say they get more applications than they can read closely, and fluent writing is now cheap, so a reader trusts what they can check. In this course each fellow builds an honest career kit piece by piece, evidence first: a private career arc document, a proof-of-work inventory where every number is a claim card with a source, one strong project plan for the AI era, a showcase a stranger can open on a phone (with a case study that survives when a free demo sleeps), a resume written from the ledger, a LinkedIn profile used well (posts, comments, outreach, slow honest growth), a blog post with a plan for about ten channels, and a website plan. Module 1 diagnoses the fellow's current kit with a ten-second test and a baseline AI drill; the capstone defends the finished kit in a full AI interview or a recorded walkthrough, and compares the result with that baseline. Every AI practice has a written option that completes the same gate, and a provided fellow covers every practice for anyone with no paid work yet or who prefers not to share their own.

## Learner and entry prerequisites
- **Learner:** Dev Weekends fellows in Gaza, early career to mid level, aiming at remote work with international founders, teams and clients. Mostly backend, frontend, mobile and data engineers, with some product, design and operations fellows. Arabic first, English second. Mostly phones on weak, expensive data, with power cuts.
- **Prior knowledge:** at least one job, internship, freelance gig, study project or volunteer role; usually a resume draft and a LinkedIn account; Git and GitHub. No training in writing, branding or measuring impact is assumed.
- **Time available:** about 5 to 6 hours a week for about 5 weeks. This plan is about 30 hours (see the five-week shape).
- **Entry diagnostic:** gate 1.6. A partner (or the fellow with a timer) runs the ten-second test on the current resume and profile, and the fellow takes a baseline AI drill on three resume lines or answers the same "how was that measured, and what was your part?" questions in writing. The flags or written answers are the baseline the capstone is compared against. Nothing in it is graded.
- **Repair paths:** no resume draft yet: start from the provided fellow's draft structure in 6.6 and replace her lines with your own. No laptop: every measurement task has a no-install path (read a supplied dashboard export and answer the six reading questions). No paid AI key: the written option.
- **Equipment:** a phone is enough for every chapter and every gate. A laptop helps for the optional "measure it yourself" runs in 3.5 and 3.6.

## Where it sits
- **Course 7, "Professional & Business Communication":** basic LinkedIn and outreach etiquette. This course cross-links it in 7.4 and goes deeper; it does not reteach it.
- **Course 13, "Learn and Build with AI":** turning a week of commits into an article and saying when AI helped, and the six-month weekly learning plan. Chapters 4.4 and 8.2 cross-link it by name and do not repeat it.
- **Course 14, "The Founder Interview":** defending numbers and stories aloud, the why chain, and reading feedback critically. This course builds the proof and the profile that come before it. Overlaps, kept short and cross-linked: course 14 module 2 ("Impact with honest numbers") with chapter 3.2 here, course 14 chapter 5.7 ("A demo they can open") with module 5 here, and course 14 chapter 7.2 (reading feedback) with 9.2 here. Here the numbers become claim cards in a ledger with measurement methods, and the demo becomes a full showcase; the interview defence stays in course 14. See open decision 6 on course order.

## Constructor profile
**Debug-first.** Fellows arrive with a resume and a profile that are not working. Module 1 diagnoses that kit the way a reader sees it (ten-second test, baseline drill), then the course rebuilds it from evidence: the arc and the proof first, then the project and showcase, then the prose. The risk of debug-first (fixing without understanding) is handled by order: no prose artifact is written until the claim ledger it draws from exists. Every set-the-scene chapter is a short case (a fictional fellow's kit failing a reader), so the rhythm is case, then concept, then the fellow's own piece of the kit.

## Module and chapter sequence
Types: **Read** (set-the-scene story, one or two beats), **Learn** (visual-first beats: four to eight beats, 300 to 700 visible words, at least 900 with folds, a predict before the key beat, one or two quiz questions at the end, at least one playable visual), **Build** (practice chapter, stepType `Build`, at least 1,200 prose words, builds one piece of the kit), **Quiz** (activities only, five MCQs), **Gate** (checklist, learning log, written task, and the AI drill where the module has one; at least 700 prose words). The "Playable beat" column is the planned interactive idea for each Learn chapter; the interaction designer turns it into a canvas spec or an existing renderer at the blueprint stage. Every numbered chapter maps to a lesson in `course.yaml`.

## Capstone
- **What the learner ships:** the finished kit: career arc document, proof-of-work inventory with claim ledger, one project plan, showcase plan and one case study, resume (plus one tailored version), LinkedIn profile, one blog post with a channel plan, and a website plan. Should-haves (not required): the project started and deployed with a demo account, the post published on at least one channel, the website live.
- **Five-minute demo:** a mentor opens the resume, profile, case study, post and project plan, picks any line, and the learner shows where its number came from (the ledger row and its source) and what part was theirs.
- **Defense questions:** "How was that measured?" "What was your part, and the team's?" "What else changed in that window?" "Why this project, and who is the first real user?" "If the demo is asleep, what does a reader see?" "What would you build or measure next?"
- **Kit requirements** (in place of production requirements): every number in the resume and profile maps to a ledger row or is an honest "not measured" line; no private data, client names or secrets in anything public; every public link opens on a phone without setup; anything shown is safe for a stranger (demo data, no real keys); no step needs a paid plan, a paid domain or LinkedIn Premium.
- **Review:** the mentor reviews the kit in under 30 minutes against a rubric (evidence, ownership, clarity, access and safety, defence) and sends one to three follow-up questions, answered by resubmitting.

## Variants
Variants are not planned for the first cohort (as in course 14). The kit, the outcomes and the order stay the same; what changes is where the proof comes from. Listed so instructors can say whether they serve these fellows.
| Direction | Who | Main proof sources | Hardest constraint | Capstone difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Backend and infrastructure | Backend, DevOps engineers | Dashboards, load tests, migrations, uptime, cost reports | Employer dashboards are private | Case study on a performance or reliability change |
| Frontend and mobile | Web and app developers | Page performance field data, crash rates, app store data, design handoffs | One lab score is weak proof | Case study with field data or a median of runs |
| Data and AI | Data and AI engineers | Evaluation numbers, pipeline run times, cost per run | Numbers move with the data | Case study with a fixed evaluation set |
| Product design | Designers | Usability tests, task completion, design decisions | Few numbers; decisions carry the proof | Case study built on a usability test |
| Freelancers on client platforms | Freelancers | Client results, repeat work, reviews | Client permission | Case study with a client's approval |
| Operations and non-engineering | Operations, support, coordinators | Time saved, error counts, process changes | Baselines rarely recorded | Case study on a process change with a measured baseline |

## Out of scope
- Interview technique (course 14), salary negotiation, visa and contract law, and legal, tax or sanctions advice.
- Basic professional English, email etiquette and first LinkedIn basics (course 7).
- Building the project in code; the course plans it, sets the bar and plans the showcase.
- Deep load testing, tracing or cost optimisation; one honest number, not a performance platform.
- Paid advertising, design tools in depth, video editing in depth.
- The "six-second", "75 percent rejected by robots" and "everyone uses an ATS" claims as facts; certificate shopping (a certificate is at most a small resume line).
- Keyword stuffing, white text, copying postings, inflated numbers, bought followers, engagement groups, bots and automation that break platform rules.
- AI detection as a skill, a fixed list of "AI words", humaniser tools, writing to sound like or unlike a model.
- Hard-coded LinkedIn limits and algorithm recipes (posting hour, hashtag count, first-hour rule).
- Any step that needs LinkedIn Premium, a paid domain or a paid key; VPN or fake-location workarounds.
- Real people's profiles, named founders or investors, and company case studies presented as fact. The sponsor's own website is never named, linked or described.
- A case study for every project (one is required; more are a stretch), and a deployed website (planned, deploying is optional).
