# Research dossier, platforms part: career-brand (course 16)

This file covers topics G to K of the research pack: G LinkedIn, H outreach and comments, I writing and distribution, J the career arc document, K access from Gaza. Topics A to F live in `research.md` (source IDs S...). Source IDs in this file are P1, P2, and so on, so the two files never clash.

How to read it. Each topic has findings with a source ID, then "Phrase it this way" lines for chapter writers, then misconceptions to correct. Every official page was fetched on **2026-10-25** (the "checked" date); the source table says how much of each I read. LinkedIn pages show only a relative "Last updated" label, such as "6 months ago"; those labels are copied into the source table because the pages give no calendar date. Where the fetch showed me only an abstract, the table says "abstract only". Where I could not confirm something on an official page, it is in the Unverified claims table and not in a "Phrase it this way" line.

## Learner and context (for these topics)

Gaza fellows, English as a second language, on phones, weak data, aiming at remote work with international founders and teams (brief, `00-course-brief.md`). They already have a LinkedIn account and a resume draft. What matters for topics G to K is therefore not "how to open an account" but: which fields founders actually see first, what is allowed and not allowed on the platform, how to reach strangers without breaking rules, where to publish writing so it lasts, and which tools they can reach from where they live.

## Hard constraints (platform topics)

1. **Platform facts expire.** LinkedIn changed its own limits between pages that are only a year apart (see G2). Chapters quote only what an official page says, name the check date ("checked 2026-10"), and tell the learner to trust the editor on screen over any list.
2. **No rule-breaking growth.** LinkedIn's User Agreement and community policies forbid bots, automation of posts and comments, fake engagement and agreeing in advance to like each other's posts [P22, P23, P24]. The course teaches slow, honest growth only.
3. **Honesty carries into every field.** LinkedIn's policies forbid misleading information about qualifications, work experience or achievements [P23]. Every number on the profile must be one the fellow can source (course outcome 3).
4. **Phone-first, low data.** Every task in these chapters must be doable on a phone: writing a post, a comment, a connection note, a profile edit. No tool that needs a desktop or a paid plan to finish a gate.
5. **Access is not equal.** Some paid or verified services are harder from Palestine for payment reasons, not legal ones (see K). Every chapter task has a free path and a written fallback.

**Phrase it this way.**

### G2. The connection note limit: LinkedIn's own pages disagree

This is the clearest example of why chapters must not hard-code limits.

- The help page on personalising invitations says free members can add a personalised message to "up to three connection requests per month", each "up to 200 characters"; Premium members can send unlimited personalised invitations; invitations sent from "People You May Know" in the mobile app "can't be personalized" [P9]. Label read on 2026-10-25: "Last updated: 6 months ago".
- The help page on invitation limits says Basic and free members can include a personalized message to "five connection requests per month" [P10]. Label read: "Last updated: 1 year ago".
- Both pages were read in full on the same day. They do not agree (three versus five). The newer page (three) is the more likely current one, but I cannot confirm it from LinkedIn.
- Same invitation-limit page: all members, Basic and Premium, have invitation limits; hitting one typically restricts sending for about a week; "withdrawing pending invitations" does not remove it; LinkedIn tells people to "only send invitations to people you know and trust" [P10].
- The weekly invitation numbers that practitioner blogs quote (around 100 or 200 a week) are **not** on LinkedIn's page [U-G2].

**Phrase it this way.**
- "On a free account, LinkedIn lets you add a note to only a few connection requests each month. LinkedIn's pages say three on one page and five on another (checked 2026-10), so plan for three, and count them. Use them for the people who matter most."
- "The note is short, 200 characters. Write one clear reason to connect, not a pitch."
- "A better use of your scarce notes: connect without a note when you are already in a conversation, and keep the note for cold, high-value people. Then send the real message after they accept." (Course method, not a LinkedIn instruction.)

**Misconception.** "You can send unlimited notes if you stay under 100 requests a week." No official page says that.

### G3. How the feed ranks posts: what is first-party and what is folklore

First-party sources I read in full:

- **LinkedIn Engineering, "Engineering the next generation of LinkedIn's Feed" (listed dated 12 March 2026)** [P17]. It says the Feed ranks from signals such as information members "have chosen to share" on their profile (industry, experience, skills, geography), and how a member engages over time ("what you've read, liked, commented on, returned back to, or simply scrolled past"). Retrieval now uses LLM-generated embeddings of members and posts (post text, author headline and company, format, engagement counts); ranking is a sequential model over "more than a thousand" of the member's past interactions. It says the system uses "professional signals and engagement patterns, never demographic attributes". It also says suggested content from outside a member's network is part of the feed and that new members get relevant content from limited history.
- **LinkedIn's Help Center, "Distribution of your content on LinkedIn"** [P13]. Your content appears in your Activity section; it is shown to "a subset of your connections and followers" depending on "connection strength", their notification settings and state. People outside your network can follow you. Tips from LinkedIn: write content your audience relates to, avoid being overly promotional, complete and verify your profile, share frequently, and engage by reacting and commenting on others' posts.
- **LinkedIn Help, "LinkedIn Feed, Overview"** [P16]. Recommended content is "determined by LinkedIn's algorithms and editorial team".
- **LinkedIn newsroom, January 2019** [P19]. The ranking frame was "People You Know, Talking About Things You Care About"; LinkedIn said no content format is favoured; it recommended no more than three hashtags and said niche hashtags start better conversations. This is seven years old and the 2026 post describes a different system.
- **LinkedIn Engineering, "Understanding dwell time", 12 May 2020** [P20]. Time a member spends on an update is one measured signal. Also five years old.
- **LinkedIn authors, arXiv 2510.14223 (16 October 2025)** [P18], abstract only. Describes a fine-tuned LLaMA 3 dual-encoder for feed retrieval of content from outside the network; the paper reports online A/B gains in engagement, largest among newer members who have few connections.

What this supports: the feed is personalised for each viewer, built from what that viewer engaged with and what is on their profile and in the post. The text of your post and your headline are inputs [P17]. LinkedIn tells you, in its own help pages, to be relevant, avoid heavy promotion and comment on others' posts [P13].

What is **folklore** (no first-party source found): that the first hour decides everything; that links in the post are penalised; that "dwell time" tricks like long posts with line breaks beat other formats; that a certain number of hashtags, emojis or words "unlocks" reach; that a comment of at least N words counts more. Third-party posts on LinkedIn state these confidently; none of the LinkedIn pages above say them [U-G3]. The 2019 newsroom post says that format is not favoured, and the 2026 post describes models that read the text itself, which makes pure format tricks less likely to matter, though I cannot prove either way.

**Phrase it this way.**
- "LinkedIn builds a different feed for every person, based on what they read and engage with and what is on their profile. LinkedIn's own engineering team published how this works in March 2026. Nobody outside LinkedIn can tell you an exact recipe."
- "LinkedIn's help page gives plain advice: write something your audience can relate to, do not be too promotional, keep your profile complete, share regularly, and react and comment on other people's posts."
- "Treat any guide that gives an exact number (a magic hour, a magic word count, a magic hashtag count) as a guess."

**Misconceptions to correct.** "The algorithm punishes links." "Post at 8 am Tuesday." "Engagement groups boost you" (see G5: they are against the rules).

### G4. Creator features as they are now

- LinkedIn removed the creator-mode on/off toggle in March 2024 and profile hashtags in February 2024; the Connect button on a profile changes to Follow by default for people on that setup, with a choice of making Follow or Connect the primary action; LinkedIn says members keep the same sharing tools and analytics, and those who had Live, Audio Events and Newsletter access under creator mode keep it [P14]. Page "Last updated: 2 years ago".
- The Follow link lets anyone follow you with one click from another channel; access requires "a history of abiding by our Professional Community Policies and a good standing record" and a location outside mainland China [P15]. "Last updated: 2 years ago". Not tested for Palestine specifically; Palestine is not named on the page.
- Newsletters and long articles are available as publishing formats; articles have SEO fields [P12, P25]. I did not read a current LinkedIn page that states newsletter eligibility rules, so the course should not state any [U-G4].

**Phrase it this way.** "LinkedIn used to have a switch called creator mode. It was removed in 2024; the tools stayed. On your own profile, choose whether the main button is Follow or Connect. Follow lets people read your posts without waiting for you to accept."

### G5. What the rules forbid: automation, fake engagement, pods

Read in full on 2026-10-25:

- **User Agreement, effective 3 November 2025** [P22], section 8.2 ("Don'ts"). You agree not to: use "bots or other unauthorized automated methods" to add contacts, send messages, or comment on, like or share posts, or otherwise "drive inauthentic engagement"; use scripts or plug-ins to "scrape or copy" profiles; use more than one account or a false identity (section 2.1 requires one account in your real name); "manipulat[e] algorithms"; or use the service in any way that is "misleading" or "deceitful". Section 8.1 requires accurate identity information and your real name.
- **Prohibited software and extensions** [P24] (page label "Last updated: 2 years ago"): no third-party "crawlers, bots, browser plug-ins, or browser extensions" that scrape or automate activity; fake accounts and fake engagement are also not permitted, including tools that "try to manipulate LinkedIn's content algorithms". Penalty described: accounts may be "restricted or shut down", and tools may stop working without notice.
- **Professional Community Policies** [P23]. Under spam: members must not "artificially increase engagement" with their content, and must not "agree with others ahead of time to like or re-share each other's content". That sentence is what rules out engagement pods (groups that agree to like and comment on each other's posts). The same section forbids using invitations "to send promotional messages to people you don't know". Under Be Trustworthy: do not post "misleading or deceptive information" about your qualifications, work experience, affiliations or achievements.
- **Post and share updates help page** [P11]: LinkedIn may "limit the visibility" of posts if it detects excessive post creation that indicates inauthentic activity, or use of an automation tool.
- **Generative AI in the User Agreement** [P22], section 3.6: LinkedIn's generated content "might be inaccurate, incomplete, delayed, misleading"; you must review and edit it, and you are responsible for it complying with the community policies "including not sharing misleading information". This applies to any AI-written post or profile text.

The pods rule needs care: the policy sentence is about agreeing in advance to like or re-share each other's content. LinkedIn does not use the word "pod" on the pages I read. The honest wording for learners is "the policy forbids agreeing in advance to like or re-share each other's posts, which is what a pod does". Tools that auto-comment or auto-connect are covered by the automation clauses.

**Phrase it this way.**
- "LinkedIn's rules say you may not use bots or tools to connect, message, comment, like or post for you. They also say you may not agree in advance with other people to like or re-share each other's content. Accounts have been restricted for this. The honest way is slower, and it is the only one we teach."
- "If an AI helps you write a post, you read it, you fix it, and you are responsible for what it says. LinkedIn says that in its User Agreement."

**Misconception.** "Everyone does pods and automation, so it is safe." The pages say accounts "risk having their accounts restricted or shut down" [P24]. I have no data on how often that happens.

### G6. The weak ties field experiment

- **Paper.** Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson and Aral, "A causal test of the strength of weak ties", *Science* 377 (2022), 1304 to 1310 [P21]. Abstract read (via a university summary page), not the full paper.
- **What was done.** Multiple large randomised experiments on LinkedIn's "People You May Know" recommendations, which changed how many weak ties members got, over five years, covering more than 20 million people, about 2 billion new ties and 600,000 new jobs.
- **What it found.** Weak ties helped job mobility, but not in a straight line: the effect was an inverted U, so very weak ties did not help most. Moderately weak ties, by mutual connections, and the weakest ties by interaction intensity created the most mobility. Weak ties helped more in more digital industries, strong ties more in less digital industries.
- **Limits.** It tested connection suggestions, not messages or posts. It measured job mobility (as the abstract describes it), not remote work for people in a conflict zone or foreign hires. It tells you nothing about whether a specific message works.

**Phrase it this way.**
- "A large LinkedIn experiment (more than 20 million people) found that people you know a little help you find jobs more than people you know very well, especially in digital industries, but only up to a point. So the person who can change your path is often someone you have met once, not your closest friend."
- "It does not prove that sending a message to a stranger works. It is about who is in your network."

**Misconception.** "Weak ties means anyone; connect with everyone." The inverted U says the weakest are not best, and LinkedIn's own page tells you to send invitations to people you know and trust [P10].

### G7. Honest audience growth: what evidence exists and what is only advice

What I found: **no controlled study** (randomised or otherwise) that shows a specific posting habit, post length or format causes more followers or more job offers on LinkedIn. The strongest first-party material is LinkedIn's own advice [P13]: relatable content, not overly promotional, complete profile, regular sharing, comment on others' posts. The strongest research is on tie structure (weak ties, G6), not on posting. The weak ties study speaks to connection mix, not to content strategy.

So the course can honestly say:
- Post about real work, with evidence (numbers with a source, a screenshot, a short story), because that is the thing a founder can verify; and because LinkedIn itself asks for relatable, not promotional, content [P13].
- Comment usefully on other people's posts (see H); LinkedIn lists commenting as a way to build reputation [P13].
- Keep a steady, small rhythm. LinkedIn says "frequently sharing"; it gives no number [P13].
- Expect slow results. No source supports a promise about followers or replies; the brief says outcomes are tracked by mentors, not promised.

**Phrase it this way.** "No one has a proof of what makes a LinkedIn audience grow. What we do know: LinkedIn tells you to share things your audience finds useful, not to sell, and to comment on other people's posts. We also know that bots, buying followers and engagement groups break the rules. So we post real work and comment well, and we measure our own results over eight weeks."

## H. Outreach and comments

### H1. What the evidence is, honestly

I searched for a controlled study that compares short, specific, personalised messages with templates when a job seeker writes to a founder or hiring manager. **I did not find one.** What exists falls into five groups. Chapters must keep them apart.

| Group | What it is | What it can support | What it cannot support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed experiments on asking for help | Flynn and Lake (2008) [P28]; Brooks, Gino and Schweitzer (2015) [P29]; Zhao and Epley (2021/22) [P34] | People expect refusals more than they get; asking an expert for advice makes the asker look more competent in lab tasks; genuine compliments land better than expected | Reply rates to a job seeker's message on LinkedIn |
| Network studies | Levin, Walter and Murnighan (2011) on dormant ties [P30]; Rajkumar et al. (2022) [P21] | Whom to contact: people you once knew well, and moderately weak ties | What to write |
| One personalisation trial in another setting | Short, Rebar and Vandelanotte (2015) [P31], abstract only | Personalised invitation emails got 1.5 times the responses (95% CI 1.18 to 1.93) of generic ones among 344 people invited to a health study; quasi-randomised | A job-seeker message; the abstract does not say what "personalised" meant |
| Company data, different direction | LinkedIn Talent Solutions blog on recruiter InMails [P32] | Recruiter to candidate: InMails under 400 characters had a 22% higher response rate than the average, and individually sent ones about 15% higher than bulk ones; candidates marked Open to Work responded 37% more than others (all as LinkedIn states them) | Candidate to founder; any causal claim (no method shown); labelled **company claim** |
| Vendor blogs and tool sellers | "Template X gets 40% replies" posts | Nothing | Do not cite; see Unverified claims U-H1 |

Also relevant to fairness: a randomised field experiment on LinkedIn (Mill, Evsyukova and Rusche, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2024) sent connection requests from fictitious profiles that differed only in AI-generated profile photos signalling race; requests from Black-presenting profiles were accepted at 23% against 26% for white-presenting profiles, in a sample of nearly 20,000 US users [P33]. I read only a news-service summary of the paper, not the paper, so numbers should be checked before a chapter quotes them. It says that who accepts a request is shaped by cues the sender cannot fully control. It does not measure anything about Arab or Palestinian senders, and I found no study that does [U-H3].

### H2. Findings a chapter can use

1. **A direct, polite, specific ask is more likely to be granted than the asker expects.** Flynn and Lake: across six studies including field settings, people underestimated by "as much as 50%" the chance a stranger would agree to a direct request for help, because askers focus on the cost of helping and miss the social cost of saying no [P28]. (Abstract read in full, via Europe PMC.) This is the best evidence for "just send the message" and against waiting until you feel ready.
2. **Asking for advice can raise how competent you look, with conditions.** Brooks, Gino and Schweitzer: advice seekers were rated as more competent than non-seekers; the effect was stronger when the task was difficult, when the person was asked personally, and when asked of an expert [P29]. (Abstract read in full.) Conditions matter: the abstract says the boost is larger for difficult tasks, so ask about the hard part you tried first.
3. **People you used to know are a good place to start.** Levin, Walter and Murnighan: executive MBA students who consulted dormant contacts on a real work project got outcomes that "compared favorably" with their current ties, with efficiency and novelty (usually linked to weak ties) plus trust and shared perspective (usually linked to strong ties) [P30]. (Abstract read in full; a sample of Executive MBA students, not Gaza fellows.) For fellows: former teachers, team leads, clients and classmates are a better first list than strangers.
4. **Moderately weak ties help most with jobs** (see G6) [P21].
5. **Compliments are underrated.** Zhao and Epley: people who wrote genuine compliments underestimated how positive recipients would feel and overestimated how awkward it would be [P34]. (OSF preprint abstract read; the published version is in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.) Supports a short, true, specific compliment in a note or comment.
6. **Short and individual beat long and bulk, in recruiters' data.** LinkedIn's own recruiter data says the shortest InMails and individually sent ones do better [P32]. That is a company claim about the opposite direction, with no method. Safe to use as "LinkedIn reports", not as proof.
7. **Free accounts cannot message people they are not connected to.** LinkedIn's help page: InMail is a Premium feature; with a Basic account "you can only directly message LinkedIn members that you're connected to" [P27]. So for a fellow without Premium, the first move is a connection request (limited notes, G2), a comment, or a message through another channel (email, GitHub, a community). This shapes the outreach chapter.
8. **A note on AI-written outreach.** One preprint (Chu and Huang, arXiv 2601.10726, December 2025) used AI to rewrite job referral requests to strangers and scored them with a trained model, not with real users; it reports a 14% rise in *predicted* success for weaker requests, no help for strong ones, and the authors warn real results may differ [P35]. Preprint, abstract only, not peer reviewed. Use only as "early evidence that AI help is uneven".

### H3. What is only practitioner advice

The following appear in many guides and are **not** supported by any source I read: the best day or hour to send; "always mention a mutual connection"; the ideal word count; "send a follow-up exactly three days later"; that a video message raises replies; that a particular opener ("I came across your profile") is bad or good. Chapters can offer these as the course's own method, labelled "our suggestion; test it and keep your own log", never as findings.

### H4. How to write a useful comment (course method with first-party anchors)

There is no study I could find on what makes a comment on a professional post useful. The anchors are:

- LinkedIn's help page tells members to build reputation by "engaging with other members by reacting to and commenting on their posts and articles" [P13].
- LinkedIn's community policy says to "respond authentically" to others' content and not to "agree with others ahead of time" to like or re-share each other's content [P23].
- LinkedIn's user agreement bars bots that "comment on, like, share" for you [P22].
- LinkedIn's 2019 feed engineering post says its candidate-selection model predicts a "probability of contribution", the chance a member will share, comment or react, and gives "timely feedback to content creators" weight [P36]. This is old and describes one stage of the system; it does not say that your comment ranks your own posts higher.
- Zhao and Epley's result on compliments [P34].

A comment that a founder values, as a method: it adds one thing the post did not say (a number, a small example, a question that shows you read it), it is short enough to read on a phone, it is true, and it is written by you. A comment that only agrees ("Great post!") costs the author nothing and teaches the reader nothing. Disagreement is allowed if it is polite and specific; LinkedIn's policy requires civility [P23].

**Phrase it this way.**
- "Send the message. In studies of asking for help (Flynn and Lake), people underestimated, by as much as 50% in some studies, how likely others were to say yes."
- "Start with people you once knew, then people you know a little. A study of MBA students found that advice from old contacts compared well with advice from current ones."
- "On a free LinkedIn account you can message only people you are connected to. So your connection note or your comment is often your first and only sentence. Make it count."
- "A good comment adds one useful thing. It is not a way to be seen. LinkedIn's rules ban tools that comment for you, and ban agreeing in advance to like each other's posts."
- "LinkedIn's own data on recruiters says shorter and individual messages get more replies. That is LinkedIn's claim about recruiters, not a law for job seekers."

**Misconceptions to correct.** "A template with a first name inserted is personalised." (The only evidence of personalisation helping [P31, P32] concerns a real message to a real person; mail-merge is bulk sending.) "More messages is better." (LinkedIn limits and restricts accounts that send many invitations [P10].) "A hook that tricks the reader to reply works." (Course honesty rule; LinkedIn also forbids deceptive content [P23].)

## I. Writing and distribution

### I1. Canonical URLs and cross-posting: what Google says (checked 2026-10-25)

- **What a canonical is.** When the same content is at more than one URL, Google picks one as canonical. You can state a preference by redirects (a "strong signal"), `rel="canonical"` (a "strong signal"), or sitemap inclusion (a "weak signal"); these stack, and none is required [P37]. Google also says plainly that "indicating a canonical preference is a hint, not a rule" [P38]. Pages carry "Last updated" dates of 2026-07-10 [P37] and 2026-08-20 [P38].
- **Syndication is a special case.** Google's troubleshooting page says the canonical link element "is not recommended" for avoiding duplication by syndication partners "because the pages are often very different", and that "the most effective solution is for partners to block indexing of your content" [P39] (updated 2026-08-21). A 2023 Google office-hours answer says the same: if you do not want syndicated copies in Search, make sure they include a `noindex` robots meta tag, and `rel=canonical` is "a signal ... as a hint" [P40].
- **What this means for a fellow.** A fellow cross-posting to a platform they do not control cannot make that platform add `noindex`. So the honest summary is: set the canonical where the platform lets you, publish on your own site first, and accept that Google may still show another copy. Google also says that without any canonical preference your site "will likely do just fine" [P37]. Cross-posting is for readers, not a ranking trick.

Platform support (checked 2026-10-25; always verify on screen):

| Platform | Canonical support | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| DEV (dev.to) | Front matter field `canonical_url: link for the canonical version of the content` in the editor guide | P43 (editor guide, full text) | High for the front matter field. The "rich + markdown" editor setting was reported by a search summary, not read on a DEV page |
| Medium | Official page: tools for cross-posting "add the source it is importing from as the canonical link automatically"; manual path in editor under More settings, Advanced Settings, "This story was originally published elsewhere"; only the author can set it | P44 (full text) | High. A separate help page may label the option differently |
| Hashnode | A search summary of a Hashnode support article described an "Add Original URL" button under "Are you republishing?"; I could not open the page (HTTP 404 on both support and docs hostnames) | none | **Unverified** (U-I1) |
| Substack | No official page found that states a canonical setting for imported or cross-posted posts; third-party writers disagree | none | **Unverified** (U-I1) |
| LinkedIn articles | The SEO help page describes an SEO title (truncated over 60 characters) and SEO description (suggested 140 to 160 characters) only [P25]; I read no LinkedIn page describing a canonical field | P25 | Absence of evidence only |

**Phrase it this way.**
- "Publish the full post on your own site first. Then copy it to other places, and where a platform has a 'canonical' or 'originally published at' field, put your own URL there. Google calls this a hint, not a rule, so expect it to help, not to be guaranteed."
- "At the top or bottom of every copy, write one honest line: 'First published at [your site].' with a link. Readers use it even if search engines do not."
- "DEV and Medium have an official canonical setting (checked 2026-10). For LinkedIn, put a short version in the post and link to the full one."

**Misconception.** "Duplicate content gets you a Google penalty." Medium's help page says sites with "an overabundance of duplicate content without indicating a canonical link may be penalized" [P44]. Google's own pages describe canonicalization as choosing one version, not as a penalty [P37, P38]. Chapters should say "Google picks one version and may pick the other site's copy", not "penalty".

### I2. POSSE: publish on your own site, syndicate elsewhere

IndieWeb community wiki: POSSE is "Publish (on your) Own Site, Syndicate Elsewhere", the practice of posting on your own site first and then publishing copies or links to third parties "with original post links" back to the original [P45]. The wiki lists reasons (ownership, links back to the original) and notes that copies linking back help search engines find the original. This is a community wiki with CC0 content, so treat it as a named practice and its rationale, not as research. It also happens to match the order that Google's canonical guidance favours. For fellows, the practical reading: the website plan (course outcome 8) is the home, LinkedIn and others are doorways. A fellow without a site yet can start with a GitHub repository README or a single page.

**Phrase it this way.** "Your own page is the one you control. LinkedIn, DEV and Medium can change their rules or lock your account. Keep the original on a page of yours, and use the other places to bring readers to it."

### I3. Plain language guidance

The US government's Digital.gov plain language guides (the successor to plainlanguage.gov, which now points to them; checked 2026-10-25) [P46]:
- Writing for understanding: the page says research shows content is easier to understand with shorter words, short sections, active voice and present tense. Use active voice ("You must do it", not "It must be done"); avoid "hidden verbs" ("We analyze data", not "We conduct an analysis of the data") [P46]. The page does not cite the research inside the passage I read.
- Principles: define your audience; organise; keep it short and simple; avoid jargon [P46].
- Testing: test early, "plan to test at least twice"; **paraphrase testing** (ask a person to explain it back in their own words) suits short documents; usability testing suits longer ones [P46].

Fit for fellows: second-language readers and phone screens both reward short sentences and plain words. Paraphrase testing is cheap: ask a friend to say your headline back. This is guidance from a government publisher, not a randomised study.

**Phrase it this way.** "Write the way you would explain it to a colleague who is tired. Short words, active verbs, one idea per sentence. Then ask someone to say it back to you. If they cannot, rewrite."

**Misconception.** "Plain means childish." The guide says plain language is for the specific audience [P46]; a founder reading on a phone is the audience.

### I4. Case study structure for engineering work

I found no standard or study that defines how a portfolio case study should be built. The closest first-party model is Google's SRE book example postmortem, whose fields are Status, Summary, Impact, Root Causes, Trigger, Resolution, Detection, Action Items, Lessons Learned, Timeline and Supporting information [P47]. The SRE chapter on postmortem culture adds that the blameless style "must focus on identifying the contributing causes" without blaming people [P47]. This is an incident format, not a career format, so use it only as an analogy for structure.

A defensible course structure, labelled "our method, adapted from the postmortem idea": the problem and who felt it; the numbers before (with source and window); what you personally did and decided (and what the team did); the numbers after (same source and window); what you would do differently; where to see the work. This lines up with brief outcome 3 (before, after, window, source, your share).

**Phrase it this way.** "A case study is a postmortem for a success. It says what was wrong, what it cost, what you changed and how you know it worked. Google's engineers write incident reports in this shape, and so can you."

### I6. What makes a site feel hand-made and not AI-made

Evidence is thin; the first-party anchor is Google's guidance. Google does not ban AI content but warns against producing many pages "without adding value", says generative models "predict a likely sequence of words" and may hallucinate, so "it is critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content", and suggests disclosing how content was created [P41] (updated 2026-10-01). Its people-first content page asks whether the content gives "original information, reporting, research, or analysis", whether it shows "first-hand expertise", whether bylines and author pages exist, and warns against "deceptive authorship information" [P42] (updated 2026-10-05). For readers' reactions to text they think is AI-written and for LLM vocabulary, see topic B in `research.md`.

One result belongs here because of who the learners are: Liang et al. (2023) found that several widely used GPT detectors "consistently misclassify non-native English writing samples as AI-generated" [P52] (arXiv abstract only). So "pass an AI detector" is not a goal worth teaching, and a fellow's own plain English may be flagged. A hand-made site is shown by specifics: real numbers with sources, real screenshots, names of real tools, a date, a photo of a real working place if safe, and an honest "what went wrong". That is the course's method, supported by the originality questions above.

**Phrase it this way.**
- "Make it yours by being specific: your number, your screenshot, your mistake. Google's own advice asks for first-hand experience and original information."
- "If AI helped you draft, you check every fact and you say so. Do not try to hide it from a detector. Detectors have been shown to mislabel second-language English."

**Misconception.** "Use a humaniser to beat AI detectors." The reliable fix is content only you can write; and see U-I2.

## J. The career arc document

Kept short, as the pack asks: the document is mostly course method. Evidence found, and its limits:

1. **Reflected Best Self.** Roberts, Dutton, Spreitzer, Heaphy and Quinn (2005, *Academy of Management Review*) propose that people build a "reflected best-self portrait", a self-knowledge structure about "who one is at one's best", through social experiences, including feedback from other people [P53]. This is a theory paper; the abstract (as returned by search summaries, not read at the publisher) does not report a field test of a resume or career outcome. A search summary describes the same group's practitioner article (*Harvard Business Review*, January 2005) as a feedback exercise that gathers input from others; I did not read that article [P53]. The "ask three people for a real story" step below is the course's own adaptation, a way to find material, not proof that the method raises hiring chances.
2. **Using signature strengths.** A meta-analysis of 14 articles (29 effect sizes) found that interventions built on using one's signature character strengths, compared with control conditions, had small to moderate effects on happiness (Hedges' g 0.32), life satisfaction (0.42) and a smaller one on depression (0.21); the authors note low power for other outcomes and that more research is needed [P54] (abstract read in full). The outcomes are wellbeing, not hiring or credibility. Safe use: naming and using strengths on purpose may help a learner feel more engaged; it says nothing about how an employer reads a "strengths" line.
3. **Personal branding research is fragmented.** A systematic review (Gorbatov, Khapova and Lysova, 2018) found more than 100 scholarly papers with "a diversity of definitions and conceptual boundaries", proposes a model and discusses ethical implications [P55] (abstract read in full). So no single, tested definition of a personal brand or "pillar" exists to cite.
4. **What makes a pillar credible?** I found no study that tests what makes a claimed strength believable to a founder. The course's own rule, consistent with the brief, is: a pillar counts when each claim can be backed by one piece of proof and one number a stranger could check (the proof-of-work inventory). Mark this "course method".

**Phrase it this way.**
- "Ask three people who have worked with you: 'Tell me about a time I did something well.' Look for what repeats. Researchers call this a reflected best-self exercise. It is a way to find your material, not a test."
- "A strength you cannot show is only a word. For each strength, write the proof and the number."
- "Skip personality-type labels in your kit. We have not checked evidence for them, and a founder cannot verify them."

**Misconceptions to correct.** "A personal brand is a logo and a slogan." (The research is about reputation and identity and is fragmented [P55].) "An ikigai diagram finds your career." (No source read; U-J1.)

## What not to teach (topics G to K)

1. **Hard-coded LinkedIn limits.** No headline or About character counts, no weekly invitation numbers, no note quota as fact. Teach "read the counter, count your notes, date the screenshot".
2. **Algorithm recipes.** No posting hour, hashtag count, first-hour rule, link penalty or comment-length trick. First-party sources describe a personalised system built from profile and engagement history [P17], not a recipe.
3. **Pods, bots, auto-DM, scrapers, "growth hacks".** Forbidden by the User Agreement and policies [P22, P23, P24]. Also no scraping of profiles to build contact lists.
4. **Hiding AI use or beating detectors.** Teach checking facts and owning the text [P22, P41]; do not teach humanisers.
5. **VPN or fake-location workarounds** for sign-up, payment or model access [P62].
6. **Premium-dependent advice.** InMail and unlimited notes are Premium [P27]; no gate may need them.
7. **Mass-mail "personalisation"** and any outreach that deceives (fake mutual connection, fake referral).
8. **Uncited personality or strengths labels** (personality types, ikigai charts) as career evidence [U-J1].
9. **Claims of guaranteed reach, followers or replies.** The brief tracks outcomes without promising them.
10. **Legal or tax advice** on payments from abroad, and detailed sanctions guidance. Point to official pages and mentors.
