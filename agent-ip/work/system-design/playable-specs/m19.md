## sysdesign-m19-add-zones (19.2, beat 2: "Add zones and watch the gain stop")

- **Beat text:** "Drag to choose how many zones the app uses, then tap a zone to fail it. One zone means any zone fault is an outage. Two zones survive a zone fault. The third and fourth add almost no uptime, because shared parts now cause most of the downtime. Watch the capacity bill too: it is what you pay to carry the load after a zone is lost."
- **Claim:** More zones raise uptime only until the shared parts (load balancer rules, deploy tool) dominate the downtime, while the capacity needed to survive a zone loss is n/(n-1) times the peak.
- **Model:** n zones, n from 1 to 4. Zone availability a = 99.9% (made up). Shared layer availability s = 99.95% (made up). Zone faults are independent. A year has 525,600 minutes.
  - Availability A(n) = s x (1 - (1 - a)^n). Downtime a year = (1 - A) x 525,600 minutes.
  - Capacity needed so that the app survives the loss of any one zone: each zone holds peak / (n - 1), total n / (n - 1) times peak for n at least 2; 1.00 times peak for n = 1 (no zone may fail).
  - Load on a live zone after f zones fail: (n - 1) / (n - f) of that zone's capacity, so 100% after one failure; the app is down when no zone is live, or when f is at least 2 and the load passes 100%.
  - Worked values (computed with the formulas, one decimal): n = 1: A = 99.8500%, downtime 788 min (13.1 h), capacity 1.00. n = 2: 99.9499%, 263 min (4.4 h), 2.00. n = 3: 99.9500%, 263 min (4.4 h), 1.50. n = 4: 99.9500%, 263 min (4.4 h), 1.33.
  - Bad release toggle: on, every zone fails at once whatever n; downtime shown is the shared layer's 263 min.
- **Inputs and snaps:** drag a handle on a rail with four snap points 1, 2, 3, 4 (default 3). Tap a zone card to fail it (tap again to restore). One toggle outside the picture: "Bad release to all zones: on / off" (default off). Reset restores everything.
- **What moves:** zone cards in a row (`SvgNode`, icon server) that appear or vanish as n changes; request arrows from a user icon into each live zone; a load bar on each live zone (0 to 100%, amber at 100%); a failed zone greys and its arrows move to the live zones; two readouts in the picture: "Downtime a year" and a capacity bar "Capacity needed: 1.50 x peak".
- **Verdicts:** n = 1: "One zone: any zone fault is an outage" (37). n = 2: "Survives a zone; costs 2.00 x capacity" (38). n = 3: "Survives a zone for 1.50 x capacity" (35). n = 4: "A 4th zone adds cost, not uptime" (32). After a zone fails, n = 1: "Zone down: the app is down" (26); n at least 2: "Zone down: the others carry the load" (36). Bad release on: "Same bug everywhere: all zones down" (35).
- **Primitive:** `picture-slider` with snap points for the zone count and `BeatToggle` for the release switch; zone cards with `SvgNode`; the load bars and readouts are custom drawing. Keep the model in a tested `model.ts` (the worked values above are the test cases).
- **Limits text:** "Made-up availability for each zone (99.9%) and for the shared layer (99.95%), and zone faults that never happen together. Real zones have correlated faults too, and cross-zone data transfer costs money."
- **Sources:** AWS "Fault isolation boundaries" whitepaper (zones, deployments separated in time; not yet in the dossier); [S3] for the round trip inside a region; the availability arithmetic is derived and labelled as a model in the chapter.

## sysdesign-m19-cells (19.2, beat 4: "Cut the system into cells")

- **Beat text:** "A cell is a complete copy of the app that serves only some customers. Drag to choose how many cells, then tap the poison customer. Their bad request breaks the cell it lands in. With one cell, all 100 customers are hit. With 10 cells, 10 are. The price is a router in front and a more careful way of running things."
- **Claim:** The share of customers hit by one bad request or release is 1 / (number of cells), because each cell shares no state with the others.
- **Model:** 100 customers numbered 1 to 100 in a 10 by 10 grid. N cells, N in {1, 2, 4, 10} (default 4). Customer c belongs to cell ((c - 1) mod N) + 1, so cells are interleaved, not blocks (keep this so the picture shows that a router, not geography, decides). The poison customer is customer 37. Tapping it fails the cell it belongs to. Affected customers = 100 / N.
  - Worked values: N = 1: 100 affected. N = 2: 50. N = 4: 25. N = 10: 10. The poison customer 37 is in cell 1 for N = 1, 2 and 4 and in cell 7 for N = 10 (cell = ((37 - 1) mod N) + 1). Use the formula in code and tests.
- **Inputs and snaps:** drag a handle on a rail with four snap points 1, 2, 4, 10 (default 4). Tap the poison customer dot (marked with a skull-free warning icon, labelled "poison request") to send the request. One outside control: Reset (44 px).
- **What moves:** dots recolour by cell as N changes; a thin router bar above the grid shows "cell router" once N is at least 2; after the tap, the affected cell's dots turn red with a small cross and the rest stay neutral; a counter "customers affected: 25 of 100" grows as the dots turn.
- **Verdicts:** before the tap: "Tap the poison customer" (23). N = 1: "100% of customers affected" (26). N = 2: "50% of customers affected" (25). N = 4: "25% of customers affected" (25). N = 10: "10% of customers affected" (25).
- **Primitive:** `block-map` (a grid of blocks recoloured by group) plus `picture-slider` snap points. The router bar is custom drawing.
- **Limits text:** "Equal cells, one poison customer and no shared state. A shared database or config store that every cell uses would put all 100 customers back in one blast radius."
- **Sources:** AWS Well-Architected, "Reducing the Scope of Impact with Cell-Based Architecture" (10 cells and 100 requests leaves 90% unaffected; not yet in the dossier); [S93] bulkhead pattern.

## sysdesign-m19-failure-time (19.3, beat 1: "Pick the moment it fails")

- **Beat text:** "Drag the failure marker along the day, and switch what protects the database. With a nightly backup, you lose everything since 02:00, up to 24 hours. With the log of changes saved every 5 minutes, you lose at most 5 minutes. With a copy in another region, you lose about a second. The time to get back changes too, and the cheapest plan is not the fastest."
- **Claim:** The data lost is the gap back to the last recovery point, and the time to recover is the sum of copy-back, replay and switch-over. A plan with a log loses less data but can recover more slowly; a live copy in another region does both well and does not protect against a wrong write (19.3 beats 3 and 4).
- **Model:** h = hours since the last nightly backup at 02:00, from 0 to 24 in steps of 0.5 (the bar is labelled 02:00 at both ends). Constants, all made up: database 1 TB; restore speed 250 MB/s; switch the app 10 min; replay runs 10 times faster than real time; the log is uploaded every 5 minutes; copy lag 1 s; write rate 300 writes a second; promote a copy 1 min [S107 for the under one minute Aurora figure].
  - Copy-back time = 1,000,000 MB / 250 MB/s = 4,000 s = 66.7 min, shown as 67 min.
  - Mode "Nightly backup": data lost = h hours; time to recover = 67 + 10 = 77 min.
  - Mode "Backup plus log (5 min)": data lost = up to 5 min (show as a 5 minute bracket, "at most"); replay = h x 60 / 10 min; time to recover = 67 + replay + 10.
  - Mode "Copy in Region B": data lost = 1 s (300 writes); time to recover = 1 + 10 = 11 min, independent of h.
  - Worked values: nightly: h = 12: lost 12 h, back in 77 min; h = 24: lost 24 h, 77 min. Log: h = 12: lost at most 5 min, back in 67 + 72 + 10 = 149 min; h = 24: 67 + 144 + 10 = 221 min; h = 0: 77 min. Copy: lost 1 s, back in 11 min for every h.
- **Inputs and snaps:** drag the failure marker along the 24 hour bar (snap every 0.5 h, default 12.1 rounded to 12). Three mode chips inside the picture (shared `BeatSegmented` chips). Outside the picture: Reset.
- **What moves:** the day bar with a flag at 02:00 (last full backup) and, in the log mode, small ticks every 5 minutes; the marker; a red span from the last recovery point to the marker (data lost); under the bar a stacked bar of the recovery steps (copy back 67, replay r, switch 10, or promote 1 plus switch 10) with their minutes written on the segments; a total time label. The same two clocks as scene `sysdesign-m19-two-clocks`.
- **Verdicts:** nightly: "12 h lost; back in 77 min" (25; hours formatted without decimals when whole, with one decimal otherwise). Log: "5 min lost at most; back in 149 min" (35, up to 3 digits). Copy: "1 s lost; back in 11 min" (24). At h = 0 in nightly mode: "Nothing lost yet; back in 77 min" (32).
- **Primitive:** `picture-slider` (drag along a rail with a step) plus `beat-segmented` chips for the mode; the stacked bar is custom drawing on `BeatFrame`. Keep the model in a tested `model.ts`.
- **Limits text:** "Made-up size, speeds and a nightly backup at 02:00. Real restores depend on the storage, the engine and the load, and a drill (19.3 beat 5) is the only way to know yours."
- **Sources:** [S108] RPO and RTO definitions; [S38] replicas typically under a second behind; [S107] Aurora Global Database latency under a second and promotion under a minute; Amazon RDS documentation (transaction logs uploaded every five minutes; not yet in the dossier).

## sysdesign-m19-fail-region (19.4, beat 2: "Fail a region, compare the four")

- **Beat text:** "Pick a plan on the ladder, then tap Region A to fail it. The picture shows how long users are down, how many writes are lost and what the plan costs. Compare the left end with the right end. Then look at active-active: users barely notice, but both regions accept writes, and that makes a new problem."
- **Claim:** Down the ladder from backup and restore to active-active, downtime and lost writes fall and cost rises; active-active removes the failover step and adds write conflicts.
- **Model:** four rungs (backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, active-active). Constants, all made up unless noted: a person decides in 10 min; rebuild the infrastructure from code 30 min; restore 1 TB 67 min (19.3); promote a copy 1 min [S107, Aurora promotes in under a minute]; start and scale the app servers 10 min; move users 2.5 min (health check 30 s x 3 failures plus a 60 s TTL, 19.5); write rate 300 a second; copy lag 1 s; backups copied hourly.
  - Backup and restore: users down = 10 + 30 + 67 + 2.5 = 109.5 min (about 110); writes lost up to 1 hour of writes (backups copied hourly); cost 1.1 regions' worth.
  - Pilot light: down = 10 + 1 + 10 + 2.5 = 23.5 (about 24); lost = 300 x 1 = 300 writes; cost 1.2.
  - Warm standby: down = 10 + 1 + 2.5 = 13.5 (about 14), serving at reduced capacity; lost 300; cost 1.5.
  - Active-active: no decision and no promotion; Region A's half of the users are down 2.5 min (about 3), the other half 0; lost = 150 (A took half the writes); cost 2.0 (each region sized for 100%, 19.5 beat 4). A standing note under this rung: "Same record written in both: one edit is lost."
- **Inputs and snaps:** drag a handle on a ladder with four snap points (default warm standby); tap the Region A card to fail it (tap again or Reset to restore). No other outside control.
- **What moves:** Region A and Region B cards; what runs in B per rung is drawn as small icons (copy only, database only, small stack, full stack); users as dots on A; on the fail tap, A turns red, a stacked bar of steps fills left to right (decide, rebuild, restore, promote, start, switch) with minutes on each segment, and the users' dots turn grey until the bar ends and then green on B; a chip "writes lost: 300" appears; a cost bar shows the multiple.
- **Verdicts:** before the tap: "Pick a plan, then fail Region A" (31). Backup and restore: "Down about 110 min; up to 1 h lost" (34). Pilot light: "Down about 24 min; 300 writes lost" (34). Warm standby: "Down about 14 min; 300 writes lost" (34). Active-active: "A's users back in 3 min; 150 at risk" (36).
- **Primitive:** `picture-slider` with four snap points; cards and the step bar are custom drawing on `BeatFrame` (or `design-stepper` for the steps). Keep the model in a tested `model.ts`.
- **Limits text:** "Made-up step durations, write rate and costs. The order of the rungs comes from the AWS guide; the numbers do not."
- **Sources:** [S107] the four plans, Aurora promotion under a minute, typical cross-region lag under a second; [S108] RTO and RPO; 19.5 for the 2.5 min DNS move.

## sysdesign-m19-route-users (19.5, beat 2: "Route users, then fail a region")

- **Beat text:** "Pick a routing policy, then tap a region to fail it. Latency routing sends each user to the nearest healthy region. Location routing sends them by country. Failover keeps everyone on A until it fails. Anycast has no DNS cache to wait for. Watch where each group of users lands and how long they are dark."
- **Claim:** For DNS-based policies, the time users are dark is the health check time plus the DNS TTL; anycast removes the TTL. The survivor receives the failed region's users.
- **Model:** three user groups (Europe, North America East, Singapore) and two regions (EU West, NA East). Policies and the region each group uses while both regions are healthy: Latency: Europe to EU West, NA East to NA East, Singapore to EU West. Location (by country, a course assignment): same as latency. Failover (EU West primary, NA East secondary): all groups use EU West. Anycast: same as latency. Round trips: Europe to NA East 80 ms and NA East to Europe 80 ms [S3]; Singapore to EU West 160 ms [S3]; within a region 0.25 ms [S3]; Singapore to NA East is not in the table, so show it as "farther" with no number, or label a made-up 220 ms.
  - When a region fails at second 0, the groups on it are dark until: DNS policies: 90 s (health check 30 s x 3, made up threshold) + TTL, where the TTL is 60 s or 300 s; anycast: 90 s only (a course assumption: the same detection time).
  - Groups not on the failed region are not affected. After the move, the failed region's groups use the other region.
  - Worked values: Latency, EU West fails, TTL 60: Europe and Singapore dark 150 s, then use NA East. TTL 300: 390 s. Anycast: 90 s. Failover, EU West fails: all three groups dark 150 s (TTL 60). Failover, NA East fails: no group is affected (it is the secondary).
- **Inputs:** four policy chips inside the picture; tap a region card to fail it. One toggle outside the picture: "DNS cache (TTL): 60 s / 300 s" (default 60 s). Reset.
- **What moves:** user dots grouped at the left; region cards at the right; a line from each group to its region (light when active); on the fail tap the failed region turns red, a timeline under the picture counts 0 to the move time, dots of affected groups turn grey ("dark") and then their line jumps to the other region; a load label on the survivor, "serves 2 of 3 groups" in amber.
- **Verdicts:** before the tap: "Pick a policy, then fail a region" (33). Latency, EU West fails, TTL 60: "Europe reaches NA East after 150 s" (34). Same with TTL 300: "Europe reaches NA East after 390 s" (34). Anycast: "Moved after 90 s; no cache to wait" (34). Failover, NA East fails: "Nobody was on it; no one moves" (30). Any policy with both regions failed: "No region left to route to" (26).
- **Primitive:** `traffic-playground` (groups of users sending to nodes, tap a node to fail it) with a time bar under it; the policy chips use the shared chip row. Keep the model in a tested `model.ts`.
- **Limits text:** "Two regions, three user groups, a course health check of 30 s x 3, and a TTL you can switch. DNS routing also depends on where the resolver is, not the phone (chapter 3.3)."
- **Sources:** [S111] Route 53 policies; [S112] Global Accelerator anycast; [S107] Global Accelerator avoids DNS caching issues; [S3] round trips; Amazon Route 53 health check intervals of 10 or 30 seconds (not yet in the dossier); 3.3 for the TTL floor.

## sysdesign-m19-write-latency (19.6, beat 3: "Write latency across regions for each choice")

- **Beat text:** "Drag the customer between regions and switch the design. One leader in North America East is fast for nearby users and slow for far ones. A home region per customer is fast everywhere and keeps data in its region. A global database is slower for everyone and keeps every confirmed write. Watch the delay and the rule check."
- **Claim:** The write delay depends on how far the data's leader and its confirming copy are from the user. A single far leader makes far users pay; a global majority makes every user pay the trip to the nearest other region; a home region makes the write local and also meets a residency rule.
- **Model:** three regions: NA East, NA West, EU West. Round trips [S3]: NA East to NA West 60 ms; EU West to NA East 80 ms. The EU West to NA West round trip is not in the table and no design here needs it. One convention for all designs: the leader's local work is 0.55 ms (disk write 0.3 ms plus a 0.25 ms round trip to one follower in its own region, as in 8.3) [S3]. Write time = trip from the customer to the leader (0 when the customer is in the leader's region, otherwise the round trip between the two regions) + 0.55 ms + (Design 3 only) the round trip from the leader to the nearest other region. Customer location L in {NA East, NA West, EU West} (default EU West).
  - Design 1, one leader in NA East, async copies elsewhere: write = trip(L, NA East) + 0.55. NA East 0.55; NA West 60.55; EU West 80.55.
  - Design 2, a home region per customer (the leader is in the customer's region): write = 0.55 for every L.
  - Design 3, a global database with one copy in each region, majority 2 of 3, leader in the customer's region: write = 0.55 + RTT to the nearest other region. NA East: nearest is NA West, 60, so 60.55; NA West: nearest is NA East, 60, so 60.55; EU West: nearest is NA East, 80, so 80.55.
  - Displayed, rounded to whole milliseconds above 10 and one decimal below: Design 1: 0.6 / 61 / 81 ms (NA East / NA West / EU West). Design 2: 0.6 / 0.6 / 0.6. Design 3: 61 / 61 / 81. These match the table in chapter 19.6.
  - Residency check for EU rows: Design 1 breaks (EU rows stored in NA East); Design 2 meets; Design 3 breaks unless all copies are inside the EU.
  - Region lost: Design 1 loses the newest writes if the leader's region fails; Design 2 loses the newest writes of that region's customers; Design 3 loses nothing confirmed.
- **Inputs and snaps:** drag the customer dot along a rail with three stops (NA East, NA West, EU West; default EU West); three design chips inside the picture (shared chip row). Outside: Reset.
- **What moves:** a small map with three region cards, the customer dot, and the leader marked with a crown icon in the chosen design; a packet travels customer to leader and, for Design 3, leader to the nearest other region and back; a wait ring on the leader counts to the write time; a result chip "Write: 81 ms"; a second chip "EU rows stay in the EU: no" (red) or "yes" (green).
- **Verdicts:** Design 1: NA East "0.6 ms: leader is next door" (27); NA West "61 ms: trip to the NA East leader" (33); EU West "81 ms: trip to the NA East leader" (33). Design 2, any: "0.6 ms: data lives where you are" (32). Design 3: NA East "61 ms: nearest other copy is NA West" (36); NA West "61 ms: nearest other copy is NA East" (36); EU West "81 ms: nearest other copy is NA East" (36).
- **Primitive:** `picture-slider` (three stops) with the shared chip row; the map and wait ring are custom drawing on `BeatFrame` (the `scene` wait ring is a model for the look). Keep the model in a tested `model.ts`.
- **Limits text:** "Steady conditions, one write at a time, round trips from the course latency table. Queueing, busy leaders and loss add to every figure. Copies kept inside the EU are not drawn, because the delay between EU regions is not in the table."
- **Sources:** [S3] round trips and disk write; [S57] PACELC; 8.3 for the wait for a copy; 12.3 for majorities; GDPR Chapter V, Articles 44 to 46 (not yet in the dossier); CockroachDB documentation on super regions (not yet in the dossier).
