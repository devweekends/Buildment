## sysdesign-m7-store-sorter (7.2, beat 6: "Sort eight workloads to a store")

- **Beat text:** "Eight Ticketline workloads arrive one at a time. Tap the store family that fits, or drag the card onto it. A wrong tap shakes and says why. Look for the clue words in each card: join, by key, all day, hops, words."
- **Claim:** The way data is read, not the product name, picks the store family. Clue words in a workload point to relational, key-value or document, wide-column or time-series, or graph or search.
- **Model:** the sorter model (`models/sorter.ts`): one card at a time in data order, a wrong bucket keeps the card and shows the reason for that card and that bucket, the round ends when the deck is empty. No numbers.
- **Buckets (4, the primitive's limit; the chapter teaches seven families in four groups):**
  1. "Relational" (sub: "joins, transactions")
  2. "Key-value or document" (sub: "fetch by key")
  3. "Wide-column or time-series" (sub: "flood of writes")
  4. "Graph or search" (sub: "links or words")
- **Cards (8) and the reason for each wrong bucket** (answer first):
  1. "Sell a ticket: lower the seat count, add an order row and a payment row, all or nothing." Answer: Relational. Key-value: "A key-value store protects one record, not three that must change together." Wide-column or time-series: "These stores take huge write streams. They give no all-or-nothing change across rows." Graph or search: "Neither engine is built for changing several rows as one step."
  2. "Keep a login session, read and written by session id, 50,000 times a second." Answer: Key-value or document. Relational: "It works, but a join and a transaction buy nothing here. A plain key lookup is the whole job." Wide-column or time-series: "Sessions are not a stream read by time range. They are looked up by id." Graph or search: "There are no links or words to follow, only an id."
  3. "Store gate scans: 18 a second, never edited, always read as 'gate 2, last hour'." Answer: Wide-column or time-series. Relational: "It can hold them, but this is an append-only stream read by time range, which time-ordered stores are built for." Key-value or document: "A key lookup cannot answer 'last hour at gate 2' without reading every key." Graph or search: "The question is a time range, not links or words."
  4. "Find 'friends of friends who bought a ticket', three hops out." Answer: Graph or search (graph). Relational: "Each hop is another self-join, and the work grows with every step." Key-value or document: "Following a chain of links would mean one lookup per hop, done in your own code." Wide-column or time-series: "It is built to append and range-read, not to walk links."
  5. "Search event titles for 'jaz' and rank the best matches first." Answer: Graph or search (search). Relational: "A LIKE with a leading wildcard cannot use a B-tree, and there is no ranking." Key-value or document: "A key lookup finds exact keys only. It does not rank or forgive typos." Wide-column or time-series: "Sorted by key or time, not by how well words match."
  6. "Venue report: tickets, events and venues combined and totalled by month." Answer: Relational. Key-value or document: "Combining three kinds of record means joins, which you would have to do in your own code." Wide-column or time-series: "These stores avoid joins by design." Graph or search: "It is a grouping and totalling question, not links or words."
  7. "A whole order, with buyer, seats and payment, read as one unit by order id." Answer: Key-value or document. Relational: "It works, but the whole order is read together by id, so one stored document is simpler and faster." Wide-column or time-series: "Orders are not a time-ordered stream." Graph or search: "There is no link or word question here, only a read by id."
  8. "Cooler sensors send one reading a second each, charted by time range, and old data is dropped after 90 days." Answer: Wide-column or time-series. Relational: "It can hold them at small scale, but dropping old data and range reads over a huge stream are what time-series stores are built for." Key-value or document: "A chart needs a time range, which a key lookup cannot give." Graph or search: "The question is time, not links or words."
- **Inputs:** tap a bucket (a real button under each bucket), or drag the card onto a bucket. "Sort again" after the round.
- **What moves, what to notice:** same as `sysdesign-m1-requirement-sorter`: a right card flies into its bucket, a wrong one shakes and shows its reason. Notice the clue words: join and all-or-nothing, by id, every second and last hour, hops, words and rank.
- **Verdicts:** no card needed a second try: "Eight for eight, first try". Otherwise: "N cards needed a second try" (N a digit). All fit in 38 characters.
- **Primitive:** `primitives/sorter.tsx` (`makeSorterCanvas`), data only. The data file holds buckets and cards.
- **Limits text:** "Real workloads mix kinds, and several stores can serve one. Four groups stand for seven store families here."
- **Sources:** the chapter's own folds; DynamoDB [S40]; Lucene [S100]; Bigtable, Cassandra, MongoDB, Neo4j and Tiger Data docs (read 2026-10-24).

## sysdesign-m7-lsm-stepper (7.3, beat 5: "Step through writes, flushes and merges")

- **Beat text:** "Press Next to put keys in, flush the memory table, and merge files. After each step, read the two counters: how many files a read must check, and how many bytes were written for each byte you wrote. More files make reads slower. Merging makes reads faster and writes costlier."
- **Claim:** An LSM tree gets fast writes by appending and pays later: more files mean slower reads, and merging them rewrites data (write amplification).
- **Model (all made up, toy sizes):** memory table limit 4 keys; each put is 1 log write; a full memory table flushes 4 key writes into a new sorted file (newest first); the script merges all files when asked, writing one disk write per distinct key in the merged file, and newer values replace older ones. Disk writes = log writes + flush writes + merge writes. Write amplification = disk writes divided by puts. A read of a probe key checks the memory table first (0 files if found there), then files newest to oldest, counting each file opened until the key is found. With the Bloom toggle on, a file is opened only if it holds the key (an exact toy filter with no false positives).
- **Script (18 steps, one per press):** steps 1 to 12 put seat-01 to seat-12 (flush after steps 4, 8 and 12). Step 13 overwrites seat-03 with "sold". Step 14 overwrites seat-07 with "sold". Step 15 merges the 3 files. Steps 16 and 17 put seat-13 and seat-14 (flush after step 17, which also writes the two overwrites). Step 18 merges the 2 files.
- **Counters after each step** (probe key seat-01; files checked without / with Bloom): step 4: puts 4, files 1, disk 8, write amp 2.00, checks 1 / 1. Step 8: files 2, disk 16, 2.00, checks 2 / 1. Step 12: files 3, disk 24, 2.00, checks 3 / 1. Step 14: puts 14, memory 2 keys, disk 26, 1.86, checks 3 / 1. Step 15 (merge): files 1, disk 38, 2.71, checks 1 / 1. Step 17: puts 16, files 2, disk 44, 2.75, checks 2 / 1. Step 18 (merge): files 1, disk 58, 3.63, checks 1 / 1.
- **Inputs:** Back / Next / Play from the stepper; one toggle outside the picture: "Bloom filters" on / off (default off).
- **What moves:** keys drop into the memory table box; a full table slides to the right as a new sorted file; at a merge, the files slide together into one; a read probe (a small arrow for seat-01) visits boxes and each opened file lights up and adds to the read counter; the two counters sit on the picture.
- **Verdicts:** step 12 without Bloom: "3 files: a read opens all of them". With Bloom: "Bloom filter: opens 1 file of 3". Step 15: "Merged: 1 file, 12 more writes". Step 18: "Fewer files, but 3.63 writes per put". All 38 or fewer.
- **Primitive:** `primitives/design-stepper.tsx` with a pure model `models/lsm-stepper.ts` for the counters. A custom model is needed because the counters are computed, not fixed text.
- **Limits text:** "Toy sizes: 4 keys per memory table. Real engines keep levels, many files, a log and background threads."
- **Sources:** RocksDB overview: memtable, log, flush, compaction, leveled and universal styles [S37]; Bloom filter [S113].

## sysdesign-m7-bloom-bits (7.3, beat 7: "Drag the bits per key")

- **Beat text:** "Drag to change the bits per key. At 5 bits, about 9 lookups in 100 wrongly say maybe and open a file for nothing. At 10 bits it is under 1 in 100. Each extra bit costs a little memory and saves disk reads. The curve falls fast at first, then flattens."
- **Claim:** A few extra bits per key make a Bloom filter's wrong "maybe" rate fall quickly, at a small memory cost.
- **Model:** b = bits per key. Number of hashes k = max(1, round(b x ln 2)) (the best k is about (m divided by n) x ln 2 [S113]). False positive rate FP = (1 minus e to the power of minus k divided by b), all to the power k. Memory for a billion keys = b x 1,000,000,000 / 8 bytes. Wasted file reads per 1,000 lookups of absent keys = 1,000 x FP.
- **Input and snaps:** a rail inside the picture with 10 snaps for b: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20. Default 10. Arrow keys step along the ladder.
- **Worked values (b, k, FP, one wrong maybe in, memory per billion keys):** 2, 1, 39.35%, 3, 0.25 GB. 3, 2, 23.68%, 4, 0.375 GB. 4, 3, 14.69%, 7, 0.5 GB. 5, 3, 9.18%, 11, 0.625 GB. 6, 4, 5.61%, 18, 0.75 GB. 8, 6, 2.16%, 46, 1 GB. 10, 7, 0.82%, 122, 1.25 GB. 12, 8, 0.31%, 318, 1.5 GB. 15, 10, 0.07%, 1,344, 1.875 GB. 20, 14, 0.0067%, 14,895, 2.5 GB. The JavaScript toy in the chapter measured 9.18%, 0.73% and 0.06% at 5, 10 and 15 bits.
- **What moves:** a strip of 100 dots stands for 100 lookups of keys that are not stored. The dots that wrongly say "maybe" are amber: round(FP x 100) of them, and no dot when FP is under 0.5%, with the exact rate written beside the strip. Below the strip, a small curve of FP against b has a dot that rides it as the learner drags, and a memory bar shows the cost for a billion keys.
- **Verdicts:** FP above 5%: "Many wasted file reads". FP from 0.5% up to 5%: "About 1 wasted read in 100". FP under 0.5%: "Few wasted reads, more memory".
- **Primitive:** `primitives/picture-slider.tsx` (`PictureSlider`, `SliderRail`) with a small custom curve drawing; model `models/bloom.ts`.
- **Limits text:** "Whole-key Bloom filters help point lookups only, not range scans. Real filters add some overhead, so the memory is a floor."
- **Sources:** [S113] for the formulas and the best k; RocksDB Bloom filter page: about 10 bits per key gives about 1% false positives (read 2026-10-24).

## sysdesign-m7-isolation-lab (7.4, beat 5: "Interleave two transactions, change the level")

- **Beat text:** "Pick a story and an isolation level, then step the two transactions forward one statement at a time. The picture shows what each one sees, and says which anomaly appears. Try each story at each level, and find the weakest level where the story ends well."
- **Claim:** What can go wrong when two transactions overlap depends on the isolation level, and in PostgreSQL the stricter levels stop an anomaly by refusing one transaction with error 40001, which the app must retry.
- **Model:** a fixed script per story, with an outcome per level taken from runs on PostgreSQL 18.6 (2026-10-24). No randomness.
- **Stories and scripts (statements in step order):**
  - A. "Lost update (app adds)": balance 100. T1 BEGIN; T2 BEGIN; T1 SELECT balance (100); T2 SELECT balance (100); T1 UPDATE balance = 150; T2 UPDATE balance = 130 (waits for T1); T1 COMMIT; T2 result.
  - B. "Lost update (SQL adds)": same, but the updates are `balance = balance + 50` and `balance = balance + 30`.
  - C. "Write skew (gates)": north and south both open. T1 BEGIN; T2 BEGIN; T1 SELECT count open (2); T2 SELECT count open (2); T1 UPDATE north closed; T2 UPDATE south closed; T1 COMMIT; T2 COMMIT.
  - D. "Dirty read (seats)": left = 1. T1 BEGIN; T1 UPDATE left = 0; T2 SELECT left; T1 ROLLBACK.
- **Levels:** Read Committed (default), Repeatable Read, Serializable. For story D only, a fourth choice "Read Uncommitted (not PostgreSQL)", labelled "PostgreSQL treats this as Read Committed; this shows a database that allows dirty reads".
- **Outcomes** (from the runs; the chapter's tables match):
  - A: Read Committed: T2's UPDATE completes after T1 commits; final 130. Repeatable Read and Serializable: T2 fails, "ERROR 40001 could not serialize access due to concurrent update"; final 150.
  - B: Read Committed: T2 waits, then adds 30 to 150; final 180. Repeatable Read and Serializable: T2 fails with the same 40001 (retrying gives 180).
  - C: Read Committed and Repeatable Read: both commit, 0 gates open. Serializable: T1 commits, T2's COMMIT fails "ERROR 40001 could not serialize access due to read/write dependencies among transactions"; 1 gate open.
  - D: Read Committed, Repeatable Read, Serializable: T2 reads 1 (no dirty read). "Read Uncommitted (not PostgreSQL)": T2 reads 0, then T1 rolls back.
- **Inputs:** a story chooser and a level chooser (the two controls outside the picture, segmented controls); the scrubber is the picture itself: drag along the timeline or use Next and Back. Default: story A at Read Committed.
- **What moves:** three lanes (T1, T2, Database). Statements appear as bars in time order; an uncommitted write is drawn dashed and becomes solid at commit; a waiting statement shows a hatched bar until the other transaction ends; a refused statement turns red with its error code; the Database lane shows the value over time.
- **Verdicts:** A at Read Committed: "Lost update: 150 was erased". A at Repeatable Read or Serializable: "Refused with 40001: retry". B at Read Committed: "No loss: 100 + 50 + 30 = 180". B at the others: "Refused with 40001: retry". C at Read Committed or Repeatable Read: "Write skew: no gate stays open". C at Serializable: "Refused: one gate stays open". D at PostgreSQL levels: "No dirty read in PostgreSQL". D at Read Uncommitted (not PostgreSQL): "Dirty read: saw a change that vanished".
- **Primitive:** the `timeline` renderer (`renderers/timeline/`) on the sim core, with an outcome table as data.
- **Limits text:** "Outcomes are PostgreSQL 18.6 behaviour in a two-transaction script. Other databases differ, and so do other timings."
- **Sources:** PostgreSQL transaction isolation docs (read 2026-10-24); Jepsen snapshot isolation [S46] (read 2026-10-24); the course runs behind the chapter's tables.

## sysdesign-m7-two-writers (7.5, beat 4: "Two writers, one row")

- **Beat text:** "Two writers hit one row. Choose nothing, a lock or a version check, and set how long each writer thinks before it writes. The picture counts lost updates, retries and the finish time. With a lock or a version check nothing is lost. But one waits and the other repeats work."
- **Claim:** With no protection two writers lose an update when the think time opens a gap. A row lock makes the second writer wait for the first one's whole transaction. A version check makes the second writer repeat its work. Nothing is lost with either.
- **Model (times in ms, made up):** writer A starts at t = 0 and writer B at t = 2. A read takes 1 ms and a write 1 ms, think time T is the same for both. A's write finishes at 2 + T.
  - None: B reads at t = 2 to 3. If T > 0, B's read happens before A's write completes, so B overwrites with a value based on an old read: lost updates = 1. If T = 0, there is no overlap: lost updates = 0. Finish time of the last writer = 4 + 2T when there is no overlap, otherwise 3 + T + 1 = 4 + T.
  - Row lock: B waits until A commits at 2 + T (wait = T when T > 0). B then reads (1), thinks (T) and writes (1): finish time = (2 + T) + 1 + T + 1 = 4 + 2T. Lost updates 0, retries 0, waiting T.
  - Version check: A commits at 2 + T. If T > 0, B's compare-and-set at 3 + T is refused (A's commit came first): one retry, B reads again (1), thinks again (T), writes (1): finish = (3 + T) + 1 + T + 1 = 5 + 2T. Wasted work = T. Lost updates 0, retries 1. If T = 0, B's read at t = 2 already sees A's commit: retries 0, finish = 4.
- **Input and snaps:** think time T on a rail inside the picture with snaps 0, 10, 25, 50, 100 ms (default 25). A segmented control outside the picture: Nothing / Row lock / Version check (default Nothing).
- **Worked values at T = 50:** Nothing: lost updates 1, retries 0, finish 54. Row lock: lost 0, B waits 50, finish 104. Version check: lost 0, retries 1, wasted work 50, finish 105. At T = 0: all three finish at 4 with no loss.
- **What moves:** two writer lanes over a time axis; the row value in a third lane (100, then 150, then 130 for the lost update); bars for read, think and write; B's lock wait is a hatched bar; B's refused write is a red bar followed by a second read-think-write; counters for lost updates, retries and finish time sit on the picture.
- **Verdicts:** Nothing with T > 0: "One update lost". Nothing with T = 0: "No overlap, no loss (this time)". Row lock: "B waited, nothing lost". Version check with a retry: "B repeated its work, nothing lost". Version check without a retry: "No collision, no retry".
- **Primitive:** `renderers/timeline/` on the sim core (the roadmap names `[timeline]`), with the three rules as the model. The strategies could be shared with chapter 7.8's data.
- **Limits text:** "Two writers only, and made-up times. With many writers, retries grow fast: challenge 6 measures it on PostgreSQL."
- **Sources:** PostgreSQL explicit locking docs (read 2026-10-24); compare-and-set and conditional writes [S42], [S52]; retries and jitter [S89].

## sysdesign-m7-scaling-path (7.6, beat 6: "Grow the traffic, add the right step")

- **Beat text:** "Drag the traffic up and watch which part is full. When a part turns red, add the step that fixes it. A wrong step, such as read replicas for a write problem, leaves the red part red. Try to reach 12,000 requests a second."
- **Claim:** Each database scaling step removes load from one part. Adding a step that does not touch the part that is full leaves it full, so the order of steps follows the bottleneck.
- **Model (all made up; labelled illustrative):** traffic R requests per second, 90% reads (r = 0.9 R) and 10% writes (w = 0.1 R). One machine handles C = 4,000 units per second. A read costs 3 units without an index and 1 with one. A write costs 5 units. The cache has a 90% hit rate (reads reaching the database = 0.1 r). With n replicas (n at least 1), all database reads go to the replicas, each taking an equal share; with n = 0 they go to the primary. With p partitions, the writes (and the reads, when n = 0) are split equally over p primaries. A bigger machine doubles C to 8,000 and costs 2 instead of 1. Every machine (each primary, each replica) and the cache cost 1 unit per hour; the cost readout is only for comparison.
  - Primary load per partition = (w x 5 + (n = 0 ? database reads x read cost : 0)) / p. Replica load each = database reads x read cost / n. Utilisation = load / C.
  - Optional scenario chip "Add the search box": 10% of the reads are word searches (q = 0.1 r). Each costs 20 units on the database when there is no search index (they are not cached). With a search index, they cost 0 on the database and use a search store that handles 5,000 searches per second.
- **Worked values** (R, steps, primary utilisation): 3,000 with nothing: (300 x 5 + 2,700 x 3) = 9,600 units, 240%. With an index: 4,200, 105%. With index and cache: 1,770, 44%. 12,000 with index and cache: 1,080 + 6,000 = 7,080 units, 177%. Adding 2 replicas: primary 6,000 units, 150%, each replica 540 units, 14%. Adding 2 partitions instead of replicas: (1,080 + 6,000) / 2 = 3,540 units each, 89%. Index, cache, 2 partitions and 2 replicas at 12,000: primary 3,000 units, 75%. At 25,000 with index and cache: w = 2,500, r = 22,500, database reads 2,250: 2,250 + 12,500 = 14,750 units, 369%; with 4 partitions: 3,688 units each, 92%.
- **Input and snaps:** traffic on a rail inside the picture with snaps 500, 1,000, 3,000, 6,000, 12,000, 25,000, 50,000 requests per second (default 3,000). Steps are toggles drawn inside the picture on the architecture: Index (on / off), Cache (on / off), Replicas (tap to add, 0 to 3), Partitions (tap to cycle 1, 2, 4, 8), Bigger machine (on / off), and, when the scenario chip is on, Search index (on / off). Outside the picture: the scenario chip "Add the search box" and Reset (at most two controls).
- **What moves:** an architecture picture of cards: App, Cache, Primary (or P1..Pp), Replicas, Search store. Request dots flow along links at a rate that follows R (capped, one dot stands for many requests). Each database card shows its utilisation as a pill with a number and a word (under 70% "fine", 70% to 100% "busy", over 100% "full"), and turns amber then red. The cost per hour shows beside the picture.
- **Verdicts (by worst part; N is the percentage):** all under 100%: "Every part has room". Primary over 100% with no index and read cost above write cost: "Primary N%: add an index". With an index but no cache and read cost above write cost: "Primary N%: add a cache". With write units per partition at or above C: "Writes N%: partition the data". Reads on the primary over 100% with writes under C: "Reads N%: add a read replica". With the scenario chip on and search reads on the database over 100%: "Searches overload: add a search index". Each is at most 38 characters.
- **Primitive:** no single existing primitive fits several cards with utilisation pills. Build on `picture-slider` (traffic rail) and `svg-node` cards, with a pure model `models/db-scaling.ts`, or on the `sandbox` explore mode when it ships. The traffic-playground primitive covers one source and one server only.
- **Limits text:** "All numbers are made up to show the reasoning, not a benchmark. Real databases differ, and real traffic is not 90% reads all day."
- **Sources:** the load model is a course example. Replica lag [S38], [S39]; DynamoDB partitions [S40]; cache savings: Facebook leases [S24].
