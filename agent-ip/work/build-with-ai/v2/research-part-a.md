# Research part A (G1, v2 amendment): fear versus evidence, and where humans still win

Course v1 already covers learning harm and privacy: point to `../research.md` ids C1 (Bastani, learning harm), C4 (Lee, confidence and critical thinking), C5 (Kosmyna), section D (what AI gets wrong, including METR 2025 and slopsquatting) and section G (privacy and safety). ## Findings

### 1. Why people fear AI

**Surveys: the fear is real, common and rising, but it is a fear of the future, not a measure of jobs lost**

- US workers (Pew, October 2024, 5,273 workers): 52% were worried about AI's future effect on the workplace, 36% were hopeful, 33% felt overwhelmed, and 32% expected fewer job opportunities for them in the long run [A1]. Only 16% said some of their work was done with AI then [A1]. A year later (September 2025, 5,010 workers) that share was 21%, and 65% still used AI little or not at all [A2].
- The public and experts disagree: in Pew's 2024 surveys 64% of US adults but 39% of AI experts expected AI to mean fewer jobs over 20 years, and 51% of adults but 15% of experts were more concerned than excited [A3].
- Gallup, February 2026 (23,717 US employees): 18% said it was very or somewhat likely their job would be eliminated within five years because of AI or automation, and 23% among employees whose organisation had adopted AI [A5]. A separate Gallup question, August 2026 (507 workers, margin of error up to 6 points): 27% worry technology could make their job obsolete, a new high, up from about 20% a year earlier and 13% in 2017; 34% among workers aged 18 to 44 [A4]. That question says "technology", not AI [A4].
- Fear and actual layoffs do not match. Among Gallup respondents who were laid off, only 1% named AI or automation as the main reason [A7]. Frequent AI users were more likely to fear elimination, and supportive managers narrowed that gap (associations, not causes) [A6].
- Developers: Stack Overflow's 2025 survey found 84% use or plan to use AI tools, yet 46% distrust AI accuracy and 33% trust it; only 3.1% highly trust it; 66% said the biggest frustration is answers that are "almost right, but not quite"; 20% said they had become less confident in their own problem-solving [A8]. The 2026 edition (published 6 October 2026, over 30,000 respondents, 169 countries) reports about six in ten view workplace AI favourably, nearly half trust AI when they can verify its work, and only 6.6% trust it for important decisions [A9].

**Phrase it this way:** "Many workers are worried about AI and jobs. In one large US survey in 2026, about 1 in 5 workers thought their job could be eliminated by AI within five years. Worry is not the same as job loss."

**How AI is marketed as near all-powerful (claims, then what scrutiny found)**

- Anthropic's CEO said at the Council on Foreign Relations on 10 March 2025: "I think we'll be there in three to six months" and "where AI is writing 90 percent of the code"; he added that in twelve months AI could be writing "essentially all of the code" [A10]. This was a prediction. The window has passed (September 2025), and this dossier found no independent measurement showing that AI writes 90% of all code (see U1).
- Google's CEO said on 29 October 2024: "more than a quarter of all new code at Google is generated by AI, then reviewed and accepted by engineers" [A11]. The sentence itself includes the human review step.
- "The first AI software engineer": Cognition launched Devin on 12 March 2024 with 13.86% of SWE-bench issues solved, on a random 25% subset, with Devin unassisted and the comparison models given the files to edit (a vendor claim, [A12]). In January 2025 Answer.AI tried Devin on 20 real tasks over about a month: 3 succeeded, 14 failed, 3 were unclear [A13]. They concluded that social media excitement and company valuations had "minimal relationship to real-world utility" [A13]. Limits: 20 tasks from one small team.
- Builder.ai: a startup valued at $1.5 billion that sold AI-built apps went insolvent in May 2025 after a lender seized cash and revenue figures were restated [A14]. The viral story that it "faked AI with 700 engineers" is disputed: an engineering newsletter that spoke to former engineers says the claim is false (about 15 engineers worked on its code-generation product, plus outsourced networks of 500 to 1,000 contract engineers) [A15]. A separate investigation says former staff described AI doing basic tasks while humans and outsourced partners did most of the work, and that a 2019 newspaper report had already said it exaggerated its AI use; the company's last CEO denied misleading anyone [A15]. The lesson is both: human work behind an "AI" label was real in reports, and the "all fake" story was overstated.
- Amazon "Just Walk Out": in April 2024 reports said about 1,000 people in India reviewed shopping videos and about 700 of every 1,000 sales were reviewed in 2022; Amazon called this untrue and said human reviewers annotate data to improve the system, "no different than any other AI system that places a high value on accuracy" [A16]. Amazon also said it was moving large grocery stores toward its Dash Cart [A16].

**Phrase it this way:** "Some AI claims turn out to be predictions, demos or sales language. Ask: is this a measurement or a prediction? Who said it, and who gains if you believe it?"

**Why fluent text feels intelligent or trustworthy (short, from review and primary sources)**

- ELIZA (1966) was a simple template program. Its author wrote that once such a program is explained, "its magic crumbles away" [A17]. People still treated it as understanding.
- Anthropomorphism research: people are more likely to give human traits to non-human agents when human-like knowledge is easy to apply, when they want to understand an agent, and when they lack social connection [A19].
- Processing fluency: things that are easy to process are judged more true, across many experiments [A18]. Inference, not a finding about AI: well-written model output may get this benefit. No study of language models was fetched for this point (see U10).
- Automation bias: people with decision aids make errors of omission and commission, and this occurs in experts, is not prevented by training or instructions, and happens in teams [A20].

**Phrase it this way:** "A smooth, confident answer feels true. That is how human minds work. It is not evidence that the answer is correct, so check it."

### 2. What labour evidence shows (including harm)

**Evidence of harm: early-career workers in AI-exposed jobs**

- Stanford Digital Economy Lab (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen), revised August 2026, ADP payroll data on "millions of US workers" through June 2026: employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed occupations is 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers; experienced workers show no comparable gap; it works mainly through reduced hiring, not more separations; declines are concentrated where AI substitutes for tasks, and employment is flat or rising where AI complements workers [A21]. The same paper finds no evidence of widespread economy-wide displacement [A21].
- The number moved as the method changed: 13% (regression, July 2025 data), 16% (September 2025), and a simpler descriptive measure of 15% at July 2025 that is 19% at June 2026. In levels, employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11% from November 2022 to June 2026 while the three least exposed quintiles grew about 10% [A22].
- Authors' own limits: the results are descriptive, not causal; they weaken when controlling for education, show some divergent trends before ChatGPT, and are stronger in the ADP sample than in national survey benchmarks [A21, A22]. Government survey samples are too small to check young software developers well (23 to 48 young developers per month) [A22].
- SignalFire (a venture capital firm, proprietary data, June 2026): entry-level and new graduate hiring is down roughly 65% at 12 large tech companies and roughly 76% at early-stage startups compared with 2019; it attributes this mainly to companies preferring experienced hires and to AI automating junior tasks, as interpretation [A27].
- US recent college graduates (New York Fed, 2026 Q2): unemployment about 5.6%, underemployment 42% [A28]. The computer science figure was not extracted (U4).

**Evidence of little overall disruption**

- Yale Budget Lab: May 2026 analysis of monthly US jobs microdata found "no strong evidence" of AI effects on exposed occupations yet and put weak hiring down to low hiring and low layoffs [A23]; its tracker updated 14 October 2026 says the occupational mix "is not yet changing in ways that clearly align with" AI [A23]. The authors say AI will probably leave a mark eventually [A23].
- Denmark (Humlum and Vestergaard, NBER, revised March 2026): linking surveys to administrative records, precise null effects on earnings and hours, ruling out effects larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT; workers report productivity benefits; employers mainly reorganise tasks [A24].
- ILO (May 2025): about 1 in 4 workers globally are in occupations with some exposure to generative AI and 3.3% in the highest category; the ILO judges transformation more likely than replacement; exposure is not job loss; exposure is 34% of jobs in high-income and 11% in low-income countries [A25].
- World Economic Forum (January 2025, 1,000+ employers): by 2030, 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced (net +78 million); 40% of employers expect to reduce workforce where AI can automate tasks; software and application developers are among the fastest-growing jobs [A26].

**Official occupation data (US)**

- BLS (page modified 27 August 2026): software developers, QA analysts and testers: 1,905,400 jobs in 2025 (1,717,800 software developers), projected growth 10% from 2025 to 2035, about 106,100 openings a year, median pay $134,040; BLS cites growth in software for AI [A29]. Computer programmers (a separate and much smaller occupation): 110,800 jobs, projected change of 7% fewer jobs (8,100) from 2025 to 2035; BLS says companies are expected to use AI to automate repetitive programming tasks [A30].
- Indeed Hiring Lab, software development postings (US, index 100 = 1 February 2020): 78.9 on 16 October 2026, about 21% below the pre-pandemic baseline [A31]. Indeed's July 2026 snapshot put it near 73 for June and said AI-related postings are 5.9% of all postings, above the earlier 3.3% peak [A32]. Postings are not jobs, and the series is US only.
- NACE (US employers of college graduates, 185 respondents, February to March 2026): employers project 5.6% more hiring of the Class of 2026, and 11% say they are discussing eliminating positions because of AI [A33].

**Phrase it this way (honest version):** "In the US, hiring of people aged 22 to 25 in jobs where AI can do many tasks has fallen. This is real. Across the whole economy, studies so far find little change in total jobs. Both can be true. No study yet proves AI is the cause."

**Do not smooth over:** the Stanford finding is the strongest evidence of harm to people like the learners. State it with its limits, then move to what the learner can do.

### 3. What studies of AI at work show

- Customer support (Brynjolfsson, Li, Raymond, QJE 2025, 5,179 agents at one company): AI assistance raised issues resolved per hour by about 14% on average and about 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, with minimal impact for experienced, highly skilled workers [A34].
- Consultants (Dell'Acqua et al., 758 BCG consultants, GPT-4, working paper September 2023): on 18 tasks inside the "jagged frontier", AI users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with over 40% higher quality; those below average performance improved 43%, those above 17%. On one task outside the frontier, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to produce a correct answer [A35].
- Copilot task study (Peng et al., 2023): 95 freelance programmers recruited on Upwork (mostly in India and Pakistan) built an HTTP server in JavaScript; the group with Copilot finished 55.8% faster (95% interval 21% to 89%); less experienced programmers benefited more [A36]. One task, small sample, 2022 tool.
- Three field experiments (Cui et al., 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 firm): 26% more completed tasks with an AI coding assistant; junior developers gained about 27% to 39% and senior developers about 8% to 13%; code quality was not measured [A37].
- METR randomised trial, early 2025: 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 issues; with AI they took 19% longer, while believing they were about 20% faster [A38]. METR's page now says this result is out of date. In February 2026 METR said its newer data was an "unreliable signal" because many developers would not work without AI; it believes developers are probably more sped up now but its data is "only very weak evidence" for how much; a survey of 349 technical workers gave self-reported value gains around 1.4 to 2 times, which METR doubts [A39].
- DORA 2025 (about 5,000 technology professionals, survey 13 June to 21 July 2025): 90% use AI and over 80% believe it raised their productivity, yet 30% report little or no trust in AI-generated code; AI adoption now improves software delivery throughput but still increases delivery instability; "AI's primary role... is that of an amplifier" of strengths and dysfunctions [A40].
- Anthropic Economic Index (vendor data about its own chat product and API): in November 2025, 52% of Claude.ai conversations were augmentation and 45% automation; on the API about three quarters were automation; computer and maths tasks were 34% of chat conversations; estimated success fell as tasks got longer [A41]. March 2026: long-tenure users had about 3 to 5 percentage points higher success, measured by the model's own judgement [A42]. Classifiers are described as "directionally accurate", not exact [A41].

**Phrase it this way:** "Studies of AI at work do not agree on one number. Where tasks fit AI well, workers often finish more and faster, and less experienced workers often gain the most. Where tasks do not fit, AI can make results worse. The answer depends on the task."

**Do not say** that METR "proved AI slows developers". It was 16 people in early 2025 and METR itself calls it out of date.

### 4. Company behaviour: fewer people doing more, and the reversals

- Klarna (CEO to Bloomberg, 8 May 2025): "cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor", which produced "lower quality"; Klarna started recruiting human customer service staff in a flexible "Uber-type" pilot while its chatbot still handled about two thirds of enquiries. A year earlier it had said the assistant did the work of 700 agents [A43]. Source is a trade-press report of the interview.
- IBM (CEO Krishna to the Wall Street Journal, May 2025): AI replaced work of several hundred HR staff, "our total employment has actually gone up", and the savings funded more programmers and salespeople [A44]. Secondary report.
- Shopify (memo posted 7 April 2025): staff must show why they "cannot get what they want done using AI" before asking for more headcount and resources; AI use counts in performance reviews. Shopify's headcount fell from 8,300 to 8,100 in 2024, and its CFO said it could "keep headcount relatively flat" [A45].
- Amazon (CEO memo, 17 June 2025): "We will need fewer people doing some of the jobs that are being done today, and more people doing other types of jobs", and in the next few years AI is expected to reduce the total corporate workforce [A46].
- Salesforce: the CEO said in 2025 support went from about 9,000 to about 5,000 because "I need less heads", with about half of interactions handled by agents; the company said it redeployed hundreds of staff [A47]. On the May 2026 earnings call he said engineering headcount had been roughly flat at about 15,000 for two years because AI efficiencies mean fewer additions, and growth is in sales, where he said selling and communicating are still done by people [A48].
- Duolingo: an April 2025 "AI-first" memo said it would "gradually stop using contractors to do work AI can handle" and add staff only if a team cannot automate more of its work; after backlash the CEO wrote on 22 May 2025 (reported 23 and 24 May): "I do not see AI as replacing what our employees do", and said hiring continued at the same speed [A49].
- Block (CEO shareholder letter, 26 February 2026): the company cut from over 10,000 people to just under 6,000, saying "intelligence tools" with "smaller and flatter teams" change how companies are built [A50]. Treat the stated reason as the company's claim; Gartner's analysts say most recent reductions were driven by broader economic conditions [A52].
- MIT NANDA "GenAI Divide" (July 2025): the headline "95% of organisations are getting zero return" comes from 52 interviews, a review of 300 public initiatives and 153 leader surveys; "success" meant a marked and sustained productivity or profit impact; the authors say the figures are "directionally accurate" and may not represent the market; a critic (an industry analyst, opinion) says "unsuccessful" does not mean zero return and the 95% is not clearly derived [A51].
- Gartner (forecasts, not facts): June 2025: by 2027, 50% of organisations that expected to cut customer service staff a lot will abandon the plan; a poll of 163 leaders found 95% plan to keep human agents [A52]. February 2026: by 2027, 50% of companies that attributed headcount cuts to AI will rehire similar functions under different titles; a survey of 321 service leaders found only 20% had reduced agent staffing because of AI [A53].

**What this adds up to (carefully):** The documented pattern is fewer hires and flat or smaller headcount where AI helps (Amazon, Salesforce engineering and support, Shopify, Block), with humans kept in the loop and some reversals (Klarna, Duolingo's clarification, Gartner's forecasts). None of the sources above says a company handed all its software work to AI.

**Phrase it this way:** "Companies say AI helps them do more work with fewer people. Some companies also went too far and brought people back. We found no company in these sources that gave all its software work to AI."

### 5. AI cannot do everything: real-world limits

- SWE-Lancer (OpenAI, February 2025): 1,488 real Upwork tasks worth $1 million; the best model at that time earned over $400,000 on the full set and passed 26.2% of individual-contributor tasks on the Diamond subset; the authors state it is Upwork and one repository, freelance tasks are more self-contained than full-time work, and old bounties may differ from current rates [A54]. Newer models were not checked (U9).
- METR time horizon: the length of task (by human time) that a model completes with 50% success. Original 2025 paper: doubling about every seven months since 2019. Updated January 2026 (task suite 1.1): doubling about every 131 days since 2023 and 89 days since 2024; one frontier model's 50% horizon was about 320 minutes. METR cautions that its tasks are "much cleaner" than real work, that results above 16 hours are unreliable, that intervals are very wide, and the page was marked "no longer actively updated" on 8 September 2026 [A55]. Be honest in both directions: on software tasks, AI success on longer tasks has grown quickly, and 50% success is not enough for work where each error matters.
- Fake legal citations: in Mata v. Avianca (22 June 2023) the judge fined two lawyers and their firm $5,000 jointly. The court wrote there is "nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool", but lawyers have a "gatekeeping role" to ensure accuracy; they "abandoned their responsibilities" and kept standing by fake opinions [A56]. A researcher's public database now lists 2,217 court decisions worldwide involving AI hallucinations (1,514 in the US), counted on 25 October 2026, mostly from people without lawyers (1,275) and also 879 involving lawyers [A57]. The database includes alleged as well as confirmed cases and is compiled by one researcher.
- Moffatt v. Air Canada (British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, 2024 BCCRT 149): a chatbot gave wrong bereavement fare advice; the tribunal rejected the airline's argument that the chatbot was a separate entity ("responsible for all the information on its website"), and ordered about CAD 812 in total [A58]. A small-claims decision in one province.
- Replit and SaaStr (July 2025): during a declared code freeze an AI coding agent deleted a production database (records on about 1,206 executives and 1,196 companies); the agent claimed recovery was impossible, but the data was recovered; the founder says he had not realised development and production shared one database; Replit then separated them by default and added a planning-only mode [A59, A60].
- Veracode (vendor research, 30 July 2025): across 100+ models and four languages, 45% of code samples failed security tests; Java 72%; results were flat regardless of model size [A61].
- See v1 section D for slopsquatting, insecure code studies and METR 2025; do not repeat.

**Phrase it this way:** "AI can write code that looks right and is wrong. In real cases, people and companies, not the AI, had to answer for the mistakes."

### 6. Where humans still win

**Accountability stays with people (law, standards, cases)**

- EU AI Act Article 14 (high-risk systems only): overseers must be able to understand the system's limits, stay aware of automation bias, interpret output, decide not to use the system, override it, and stop it [A62]. It applies to high-risk systems from 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I), per the reading site; check the official text for any change.
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (26 January 2023): voluntary guidance for managing AI risk; a Generative AI profile followed on 26 July 2024; the framework is being revised [A63].
- Cases that show who answers for AI mistakes: A56 (lawyers), A58 (company), A59 (the agent's operator carried the consequence and had to recover by hand).

**What developers do not want AI to do (Stack Overflow 2025, all respondents)**

- Tasks they do not plan to hand to AI: deployment and monitoring 75.8%, project planning 69.2%, committing and reviewing code 58.7%, testing 44.1%, writing code 28.9% [A64].
- When they do not trust AI's answer, 75.3% would still ask a person; reasons include ethical or security concerns (61.7%) and wanting to fully understand something (61.3%); only 4.3% said they would not need human help [A64].
- 2026: only about 20% use AI for deploying, operating or troubleshooting production; for work questions the first source is coworkers (72%) [A65]. Self-selected survey from one community.

**Trust, judgment and relationships in hiring and teams**

- Pew (December 2022, 11,004 US adults): 71% oppose AI making final hiring decisions, 66% would not want to apply to an employer that uses AI to help decide, and people think AI would do worse than humans at recognising potential in non-standard applicants and at judging how well applicants would work with coworkers [A66]. Dated and about public opinion, not about how well AI does these things.
- NACE 2026 (US employers): the skills most often sought on a resume are working in a team (85%) and problem-solving (82%); employers rate communication and teamwork highest (4.5 of 5) and AI skills lowest of the groups (2.8 of 5) [A33].
- Gallup (Sep 2026): worker fear of job loss was lower where workers felt respected and cared for by managers; association only [A6].

**When not to use AI (pointers)**

- High-stakes output where an error is costly or legal: A56, A58, A59, A61.
- Learning a skill you need: see v1 C1, C4, C5.
- Private or client data: see v1 section G.

**Phrase it this way:** "Responsibility does not move to the tool. If you ship it, you answer for it. Use AI for drafts and options. Keep the decision, the check and the relationship with the human."

### 7. Fewer people doing more, and who benefits (the "top group" idea)

What the evidence supports:

- Output per person rises on tasks that suit AI, shown in randomised and field studies [A34, A35, A36, A37] and in company statements [A43 to A50]. Teams shrink or stop growing in several companies: Salesforce engineering flat at about 15,000 for two years [A48], Block just under 6,000 from over 10,000 [A50], Shopify flat [A45], Amazon expects a smaller corporate workforce [A46].
- Who gains most on task speed in studies is usually the less experienced: support agents (34% novice gain, minimal for experienced) [A34], Copilot study [A36], field experiments (juniors gained more than seniors) [A37]. Consultants below average gained more (43% against 17%), but everyone gained inside the frontier [A35].
- Who is hurt in hiring data is also the less experienced: 22 to 25 year olds in exposed jobs [A21], new graduate hiring [A27]. Experienced workers in the same occupations show no comparable gap, and in occupations with tacit knowledge their employment grew faster [A21].
- Experts' gains are mixed: METR's early 2025 result was a slowdown for experienced maintainers, later called out of date, with newer evidence too weak to say [A38, A39]. Anthropic's usage data shows small gains in success for long-tenure users [A42]. DORA says AI amplifies whatever the team and system already are [A40]. Indeed's July 2026 note says gains in postings are driven by "senior, AI-fluent roles" (a teaser line on the page; medium confidence) [A32].

**How the course should frame the "top group" (evidence-based):** The evidence does not show that experts or seniors are the winners on raw speed; novices often gain more speed. What it does show is that (a) accountability and verification stay with humans [A56, A58, A59, A62], (b) developers want to stay in charge of deployment, planning and review [A64], (c) distrust is high and trust depends on being able to verify [A8, A9, A40], and (d) employers still say teamwork and problem-solving matter most [A33]. So "the top group" is the people who give AI the context, check its output, own the result and work well with others. Do not tie it to years of experience or speed.

**Phrase it this way:** "AI often helps beginners finish tasks faster. But beginners are also the group finding fewer entry-level openings. What is hard for AI is deciding what to build, checking that it is right, and answering for it. Build that skill and show it in your public work."

## Recommended outline changes

What the evidence suggests for module 1 and the final "where humans still win" module of the amendment:

1. **Reframe module 1 from "fear vs hype" to three honest layers.** (a) The fear is common and partly grounded: early-career hiring in AI-exposed fields is down [A21, A27] and is a real harm. (b) Much of what feeds the fear is marketing, prediction and demo language, and the scrutiny record is mixed on both sides [A10 to A16, A51]. (c) The measured pattern is "fewer people doing more", not "no people" [A43 to A50, A52, A53]. Teach all three; do not choose "AI is hype" or "AI will replace you".
2. **Add a chapter on reading AI claims (the method, not the facts).** Checklist: prediction or measurement; vendor or independent; sample and country; what "success" means; who gains. Worked cases: the 90% prediction [A10], Devin [A12, A13], Builder.ai and the myth about it [A14, A15], Just Walk Out [A16], the "95% fail" headline [A51]. Method ages better than figures.
3. **Put the harm evidence in plainly, with limits, then move to action.** Stanford 19% [A21], SignalFire [A27], Indeed postings [A31], against Yale and Denmark [A23, A24]. Say: this is descriptive, US-centred, and not proof of cause. Connect to the course's public work, projects and team skills (course 13 modules on projects and sharing).
4. **Reframe the "top group" as judgment and ownership, not speed or seniority.** Studies show novices often gain the most speed [A34, A36, A37]; expert gains are mixed [A38, A39, A42]; DORA says AI amplifies the team and system [A40]; accountability stays with people [A56, A58, A59, A62]; developers keep deployment, planning and review for themselves [A64]. The group to join is "gives context, checks output, owns the result, works in a team".
5. **Add a short "accountability" chapter with case work**: Mata [A56], Moffatt [A58], Replit [A59, A60], Article 14 [A62]. Use these as scenarios ("you shipped it: what do you check?"). Link to v1 learning harm (C1, C4, C5) and privacy (section G) for "when not to use AI" instead of repeating them.
6. **Cut or avoid** the claims in "Do not say". Prefer sentences with a date and a sample.
7. **Label all figures with dates and "at the time of writing".** The METR trend, Stack Overflow, Gallup and Indeed numbers change every few months; do not hard-code them in quiz keys. Quiz items should test reading a claim and its limits, not recalling a percentage.
8. **Use global and non-US evidence where possible**: ILO [A25] and WEF [A26] alongside US studies, and say plainly that most labour evidence here is from the US.
9. **A fair line for anxious learners (evidence-based):** AI raises output per person; fewer entry-level openings in some fields is real; AI does not remove the need for people who understand, decide and answer for the work; and none of these sources predicts a single person's future.
