### 1. Demos, UX and visuals

#### 1.1 Claude (artifacts, skills, Claude Design)

- Artifacts are content Claude makes beside the chat: documents, code, single-page sites, diagrams, dashboards, small tools. On Free, Pro, Max, Team and Enterprise you can create artifacts in chat and start from Docs, Slides or Design templates [C1].

- Claude Design is an Anthropic Labs product launched 17 April 2026 as a research preview, available to Pro, Max, Team and Enterprise, rolled out gradually [C3]. The support page for artifacts separately says Free users can start from a Design template [C1]. The two pages do not say the same thing about Free access, so chapters must not promise Claude Design on a free account.
- Skills are folders of instructions that Claude loads when a task needs them. The support page lists Free, Pro, Max, Team and Enterprise, and says code execution must be on [C4].
- Anthropic's public skills repository includes a `frontend-design` skill (design guidance: pick a direction from the subject, use deliberate type, avoid generic template looks, responsive layout, visible focus states, reduced-motion support). Also `canvas-design`, `web-artifacts-builder` and others. Many skills are Apache 2.0; the document skills (docx, pdf, pptx, xlsx) are source-available, not open source. The repo says skills are for demonstration and educational use and that behaviour may differ [C5]. The `frontend-design` licence text was not read (see U5).

**Phrase it this way**

### 3. Voice cloning (only your own voice) and the rules around it

**ElevenLabs (tool 1)**
- Instant Voice Cloning (IVC) works from short samples (under two minutes) and cannot be shared publicly. Professional Voice Cloning (PVC) uses longer training audio, needs a Creator plan or above, and uses "voice-captcha technology" to verify the clone comes from your own voice samples [C25].
- The pricing page shows no cloning on the Free plan; IVC starts at Starter; PVC starts at Creator [C26]. So a free account cannot clone a voice. Writers must not say "clone your voice for free".
- The Prohibited Use Policy (updated 9 October 2026) prohibits "cloning, synthesizing, or impersonating the voice, identity, or likeness of any living individual" without "explicit and informed consent or other valid authorization", prohibits fraud and scams, and prohibits impersonating political candidates or elected officials [C27].
- Terms: users must be 18 or older (or of legal majority where they live). There is no supported-countries page; the help centre lists restricted places (Belarus, Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia, Syria, Crimea, Donetsk and Luhansk). Palestine is not mentioned either way, so "Palestine appears on its supported countries page" is false because no such page exists [C28].

**Alternative (tool 2): OpenVoice** (MIT and MyShell). Open-source instant voice cloning, MIT licence for commercial and research use, V2 from April 2024; the README gives no usage warning and no hardware requirements, so it suits only fellows who can run Python locally [C29]. It has no consent step built in, which is why the course rule (own voice only, with disclosure) has to come from the learner, not the tool.

**Rules and risks**
- US FCC Declaratory Ruling (adopted 2 February 2024, released 8 February 2024, FCC 24-17): it "clarif[ies]" that the TCPA's rules on "artificial" voices cover AI-generated voices in robocalls, so such calls need the called party's prior consent unless an exemption applies [C30]. It is a US telephone rule about calls; it is not a general ban on voice cloning. Do not tell learners it is illegal to clone a voice.
- FTC consumer alert, 20 March 2023: scammers can "clone the voice of your loved one" from a short clip posted online; advice is to hang up and call the person on a number you already know, and to be wary of requests for wire transfers, crypto or gift cards [C31]. The FTC Voice Cloning Challenge was a contest on preventing and detecting malicious voice cloning, with three winners sharing US$35,000 [C32].
- Banks: in February 2023 a Vice reporter used a clone made from about five minutes of speech to pass Lloyds Bank's phone voice ID, after trying more than once and adjusting the audio; Lloyds said voice ID is optional and it had not seen this fraud against customers [C33]. On 22 July 2025 OpenAI's CEO said at a Federal Reserve conference that some financial institutions still "accept the voiceprint as authentication" and called that "a crazy thing to still be doing" [C34]. A BBC test at Santander and Halifax was reported only by secondary outlets (U1).
- EU AI Act Article 50(4): deployers who make or alter image, audio or video that looks like real people or events and could seem authentic (a deep fake) "shall disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated". For evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional works the duty is limited to disclosing it in a way that does not hamper enjoyment. Article 50 has applied since 2 August 2026; a grace period to 2 December 2026 covers only the provider marking duty (50(2)) for systems already on the market. The Commission FAQ says personal non-professional use is outside the Act, but regular economic benefit or freelance activity makes a person a "professional" deployer [C35]. Important for this audience: fellows who publish for clients or income may be "professional".
- Platform labels:
  - YouTube requires disclosure of realistic altered or synthetic content. Its list of what needs no disclosure includes script, outline and thumbnail help and "cloning your own voice for voice-overs or dubs"; repeated failure to disclose can bring a label added by YouTube, removal or loss of Partner Programme access [C36]. The course may still ask learners to disclose own-voice narration; say that this is the course's rule, not a YouTube requirement.
  - TikTok's newsroom (19 September 2023) says its synthetic media policy "requires people to label AI-generated content that contains realistic images, audio or video"; the current policy page could not be opened (C37, medium confidence).
  - Meta may require an AI label for "photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio" made or altered with AI; images are exempt from that requirement; "AI info" is the label; penalties exist but are not specified [C38].
  - LinkedIn's Professional Community Policies forbid sharing synthetic or manipulated media that shows a person saying something they did not say without clearly disclosing it [C39].

**Phrase it this way**
- "Clone only your own voice. Do not clone anyone else, even a friend, even as a joke. The tool's own rules ask for the person's explicit consent."
- "A cloned voice can fool some phone banking systems and some people. If a call asks for money, hang up and call back on a number you already know."
- "If you publish audio that sounds like a real person speaking and it was made by AI, say so in the first lines. Some platforms and some laws now require it."

### 4. CLIs, connectors, cloud cost views and secrets

**GitHub CLI**
- `gh auth login` uses a browser flow by default and stores the token in the system credential store (a plain text file if none exists). For token login the minimum scopes are `repo`, `read:org` and `gist`; `--scopes` requests extra ones; for a fine-grained token the docs say to prefer setting `GH_TOKEN` [C40].
- `gh issue list` shows open issues, 30 by default, with `--state`, `--label`, `--limit` and `--json`. `gh issue create` takes `--title`, `--body`, `--label`, `--assignee`. `gh pr create` takes `--title`, `--body`, `--draft`, `--fill`. `gh pr comment` takes `--body`. `gh pr review` takes exactly one of `--approve`, `--comment`, `--request-changes` [C41]. The first is read-only; the other four change GitHub.
- Fine-grained personal access tokens: GitHub recommends them over classic tokens; each is limited to one owner and can be limited to chosen repositories; permissions are set one by one and some are read-only; always add an expiration. They do not support every feature of classic tokens [C42].

**MCP and the GitHub MCP server**
- The Model Context Protocol spec (version 2026-07-28) says tools "represent arbitrary code execution and must be treated with appropriate caution", tool descriptions should be treated as untrusted unless from a trusted server, and hosts must get explicit user consent before invoking a tool [C43].
- GitHub's official MCP server is MIT licensed. Read-only mode: the `X-MCP-Readonly` header or `/readonly` path (remote) or `--read-only` / `GITHUB_READ_ONLY` (local); it disables all non-read-only tools even if requested. Default toolsets: context, issues, pull_requests, repos, users. "Lockdown mode" shows only content in public repositories from users with push access and is described as "a best-effort content filter", not an authorization boundary [C44].
- Documented risk: Invariant Labs (a security vendor) showed on 26 May 2025 that a malicious issue in a public repository could make an agent with access to both public and private repositories leak private data into a pull request. They call it architectural, not a bug in one server, and say model alignment alone did not stop it; they suggest least-privilege permissions such as one repository per session, and monitoring [C45].

**Cloud cost views**
- `aws ce get-cost-and-usage` needs `--time-period`, `--granularity` (DAILY, MONTHLY, HOURLY) and `--metrics`; it can filter and group by dimensions such as SERVICE [C46]. AWS charges for the Cost Explorer API: "$0.01" per request on the primary billing view, charged per paginated request; the API reaches up to 13 months of history [C46]. The console view carries no price statement on the page read.
- AWS Budgets can alert on actual and forecast cost by email or Amazon SNS. Budget data updates up to three times a day, and AWS warns alerts can lag charges so costs may pass the threshold before you are notified [C47].
- The managed policy `AWSBillingReadOnlyAccess` (edited 30 July 2026) does include `ce:GetCostAndUsage` and `budgets:ViewBudget`, but it is broad: it also lets the holder list payment instruments, view invoices and tax registrations, and read free-tier and contract data [C48]. "Read-only" does not mean "only costs". The course should show a minimal custom policy for cost reading (an exercise for learners; the exact action list should be checked in IAM documentation at chapter time).
- AWS accounts opened on or after 15 July 2025 choose a Free plan (ends after six months or when credits run out; US$100 credit plus up to US$100 more for activities; the account closes and data is kept 90 days unless upgraded) or a Paid plan. AWS's free-tier FAQ says "A payment method isn't required to sign up for most new customers", but may ask for one to verify identity. Older accounts stay on the legacy programme [C49].
- Google Cloud: the Free Trial gives a US$300 credit for 90 days and requires "a credit card or other payment method" (a temporary authorization hold, not a charge). Alert-only budgets "don't automatically cap" spending. `gcloud billing` has groups `accounts`, `budgets` and `projects` [C50]. Azure: `az consumption usage list` is in preview; the Azure free account is US$200 credit for 30 days plus 12 months of limited free services; whether a card is required was not stated on the page read (U15) [C51].
- Free ways to practise without a card: read a sample cost report or screenshot supplied by the course; use the AWS Free plan where no card is needed; use a local AWS emulator. LocalStack since 23 March 2026 ships as one image that needs an account and auth token, with a free plan for non-commercial use [C52]. Whether LocalStack emulates Cost Explorer or Budgets was not found (U16). So the safest practice path is a supplied sample report.

**Secrets**
- GitGuardian's State of Secrets Sprawl 2026 (a vendor report): 28,649,024 new hardcoded secrets in public GitHub commits in 2025, up 34%; 1,275,105 AI-service secrets; leaked secrets up 152% since 2021 [C53].
- GitHub: push protection for users is on by default and blocks pushes of secrets to public repositories; secret scanning runs for free on public repositories and notifies the provider for partner secrets [C54]. GitHub's guidance on removing sensitive data says that as a first step you "revoke and/or rotate that secret"; rewriting history may not be needed once the secret is dead [C54].
- OpenAI says never commit a key to a repository, use environment variables, never put a key in browser or mobile code, and rotate keys [C55]. Anthropic's help article says the same: do not share a key, keep it out of code, use environment variables, rotate, enable secret scanning [C55].

**Phrase it this way**
- "Start with read access. Give a tool the smallest permission that does the job, and a date when it expires."
- "A connector lets an AI tool act on your accounts. Text it reads, such as an issue written by a stranger, can contain instructions meant for the tool. Use read-only mode and one repository at a time."
- "Cloud budget alerts tell you; they do not stop the spending. Set one before you start, and delete what you create."

### 5. AI critique of your work

- GitHub's Copilot code review reviews pull requests and suggests fixes, but GitHub says "Supplement Copilot's feedback with a human review", that it is "not guaranteed to spot all problems" and that it "will make mistakes". It excludes dependency files, log files and SVG files, and the Free Copilot plan does not include it [C56]. So a chapter on critique should not rely on Copilot code review for fellows on free accounts; a chat assistant given the diff is the free route.
- One large study (arXiv 2310.01783) had GPT-4 comment on the full text of 3,096 Nature-family and 1,709 ICLR papers. GPT-4's points overlapped with human reviewers' points 30.85% (Nature) and 39.23% (ICLR) of the time, against 28.58% and 35.25% between two human reviewers. In a user study of 308 researchers, 57.4% found the feedback helpful or very helpful [C57]. Limits: scientific papers, one 2023 model, and overlap with human points is not proof that a point is correct.
- Sycophancy: Sharma et al. found five AI assistants showing sycophancy across four tasks, and humans and preference models sometimes preferred convincing sycophantic answers to correct ones [C58]. Part B may cover this in more depth; point to it rather than repeat.
- Method points the sources support: ask for the problems, not praise; a person decides which feedback to act on; check each suggestion against the code, tests or documentation; human review still matters [C56, C58]. The step "ask the tool to argue against your work" is the course's method, not a sourced finding.

**Phrase it this way:** "AI feedback is a second pair of eyes that wants to please you. Ask it for what is wrong. Then you decide which comments are true, and you test each fix."

### 6. Content and virality, honestly

- Berger and Milkman (Journal of Marketing Research, 2012) studied almost 7,000 New York Times articles over three months and also ran experiments. Positive content was more viral than negative content; high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) raised sharing and low-arousal ones (sadness) lowered it; surprising, interesting and practically useful content were also positively linked to virality; results held after controlling for how prominently an article was placed [C59]. Limits: one newspaper's most-emailed list, 2008 to 2009 era, US readers; "emailed" is not the same as "shared on LinkedIn". Writers may say useful content is linked to sharing; they must not say anger content is the goal.
- Attention is uneven and hard to predict: in Salganik, Dodds and Watts (Science, 2006), 14,341 participants downloaded unknown songs with or without seeing others' choices. More social influence meant more inequality and less predictability of success; the best songs rarely did poorly and the worst rarely did well, "but any other result was possible" [C60]. Limits: an artificial music market, not LinkedIn.
- LinkedIn's own engineering post (12 March 2026, "Engineering the next generation of LinkedIn's Feed") describes ranking with profile data, engagement history, post features and a sequential model over more than 1,000 past interactions, and says ranking excludes demographic attributes and that posts from different creators "compete on equal footing". It does **not** mention engagement bait, authenticity or AI-generated content [C61]. Writers must not say "LinkedIn says it demotes X" from this post.
- LinkedIn's VP of Product posted in November 2025 (reported by Social Media Today; the original post was not opened) that the goal is "to make engagement pods entirely ineffective", that artificially boosted content is flagged and its reach limited, and that LinkedIn will act against tools that automate mass commenting [C62]. Medium confidence. No official LinkedIn statement on "engagement bait" in 2024 to 2026 was found (U10).
- Rules against fake engagement and automation:
  - LinkedIn Professional Community Policies: "Don't do things to artificially increase engagement with your content", and "don't agree with others ahead of time to like or re-share each other's content"; true identity required. The User Agreement (effective 3 November 2025) bans "bots or other unauthorized automated methods" and fake identities [C63].
  - YouTube's fake engagement policy bans services that inflate views, likes or subscribers, "sub4sub" swaps and content that solely exists to incentivise engagement; repeated or severe violations can bring strikes or termination; hiring a third party does not shift responsibility [C64].
  - GitHub's Acceptable Use Policies (section 4) list "inauthentic interactions, such as fake accounts and automated inauthentic activity" and "rank abuse, such as automated starring or following" [C65].
  - X's platform manipulation policy could not be fetched (U11).
- Fake stars: the StarScout study (He et al., ICSE 2026 version, arXiv 2412.13459) found about six million suspected fake stars; fake-star activity rose sharply in 2024; most promote short-lived phishing or malware repositories; fake stars help for under about two months and become a liability [C66]. Limits: detection is "suspected", from metadata.
- Contribution graphs: GitHub's profile "uses the author date" to decide when a commit was made, and a commit counts only if its email is linked to your account, on the default branch, in a non-fork repository [C67]. Git itself lets the author date be set (`git commit --date`, `GIT_AUTHOR_DATE`) [C67]. That is why a graph is not proof of effort, and why the course says never to fake one. Faking dates would also be "inauthentic" in spirit and can be spotted by anyone who reads the history; do not give a how-to.

**Phrase it this way**
- "Useful content that makes people feel something real is shared more, on average. No one can promise a post will spread: even the best work can be missed."
- "Do not buy likes, trade comments in a group, or use a tool that comments for you. Every large platform's rules forbid it, and LinkedIn says it works against it."
- "A green graph can be faked in one command, so people who hire you read the work behind it. Let your history be true."

### 7. Posting with AI help without looking AI-made

- OpenAI withdrew its own AI text classifier on 20 July 2023 "due to its low rate of accuracy". At launch it labelled only 26% of AI-written text correctly and wrongly labelled human text as AI 9% of the time; it was unreliable under 1,000 characters and recommended only for English [C68].
- Liang et al. (Patterns, 2023): seven GPT detectors misclassified 61.22% of 91 TOEFL essays (written by non-native English speakers) as AI on average, and 97.8% were flagged by at least one detector, while essays by US eighth-graders were classified almost perfectly. A simple prompt asking for richer wording cut the false positives to 11.77%, and a second prompt on AI text cut detection from 100% to 13% [C69]. Limits: small samples, detectors mostly built on an older model (GPT-2), a pilot study. For this audience the lesson is plain: a real second-language writer can be falsely flagged. Do not teach fellows to "beat detectors"; teach them to keep their own drafts and notes as evidence of authorship.
- Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" is an advice page (not policy) with categories: inflated significance, promotional tone, vague attribution, overused "AI vocabulary", "not just X, but Y" patterns, forced groups of three, title case and heavy bold, chatbot phrases left in the text, broken or invented citations. It stresses these are signs, not proof, and that detectors have "non-trivial error rates" [C70]. Use it as a checklist for editing your own draft.
- Platform AI-disclosure rules (re-checked):
  - Medium: undisclosed AI-generated writing gets "Network Only" distribution; AI-generated writing may not be paywalled; AI assistance such as outlining or grammar checking "does not need to be disclosed", but text or images generated by AI must be; AI helps authors "express themselves in a second language"; AI images need a caption; "easily disprovable AI-hallucinated" content is banned. v1 summarised this as "any story with AI assistance must be labelled"; the FAQ draws a line between assistance and generated text, so use the narrower wording [C71].
  - DEV: authors pick Hand Written, AI-Assisted or Fully Autonomous; undisclosed autonomy or presenting synthetic output as firsthand experience "may result in account suspension"; "Disclosure is not a loophole around our core community standards"; readers get an "AI Content in Feed" setting [C72].
  - LinkedIn: the User Agreement says generated content "might be inaccurate, incomplete, delayed, misleading" and users should review it; no labelling rule for ordinary posts was found; the synthetic media rule applies to media showing a person saying what they did not say [C63, C39].
  - OpenAI's terms forbid representing that Output was human-generated when it was not [C23].

**Phrase it this way:** "AI detectors are not reliable, and they are worse for people who write English as a second language. So do not try to hide AI help. Say what the tool did, keep your drafts, and make the post yours with a real story, a real number and a real mistake."

### 8. Commit history that looks human because it is

- Pro Git's guidance for commits: make each commit "a logically separate changeset"; "don't code for a whole weekend on five different issues and then submit them all as one massive commit on Monday"; use `git add --patch` to split work; first line of about 50 characters or less, a blank line, then a body; write in the imperative ("Fix bug", not "Fixed bug") [C74].
- Conventional Commits 1.0.0 is one optional convention: `<type>[optional scope]: <description>` with types such as `fix` and `feat`, so tools can build changelogs [C75]. Present it as one convention among others, not as a rule of Git.
- GitHub's squash and merge combines all commits in a pull request into one commit on the base branch; the intermediate commits "are not preserved as separate commits" there. GitHub recommends it for one logical change with many small fixup commits [C76]. So on a project that squashes, the pull request (title, description, review comments) carries the story, not the commit list. Writers should not promise that many small commits will show on the main branch.
- A history is "human" because the steps are real: tests added, a bug found, a refactor, a note about a mistake. Do not suggest any trick to make history look natural [C67, C74].

**Phrase it this way:** "Commit when one small thing works. Write the message for the person who will read it in six months. In a team project that squashes pull requests, write the story in the pull request description."

## Recommended outline changes

1. **Teach jobs, not product names, in the toolbelt module, and rewrite the "visuals" chapter around what changed.** Claude artifacts are now template based and new legacy artifacts ended on 16 September 2026; Claude Design is a paid research preview; Codex is bundled into ChatGPT with a Free tier; Stitch's availability is unclear [C1 to C3, C6, C10, C11]. Pair every named tool with a second one and a no-account fallback (Gemini Canvas, paper sketch, a plain HTML file in a repository).
2. **Make voice cloning an optional demonstration, not a core step.** Free plans cannot clone; the verified route (Professional) needs a paid Creator plan; no supported-countries page exists to prove access from Gaza; the tool's own consent step does not exist in the open-source alternative [C25 to C29]. Keep the sponsor's rule (own voice, consent, disclosure) and add the risk case: bank voice ID demonstrations and the EU disclosure duty for professional use [C33 to C35]. Offer a default path that needs no cloning (record your own voice, or label a stock voice as synthetic).
3. **Reframe "AI text detectors" from threat to false-accusation risk.** For second-language writers, detectors flagged 61% of real essays [C69], and OpenAI withdrew its own for low accuracy [C68]. Replace "avoid looking AI-made" with "show authorship": keep drafts and notes, put a real story and a real checked fact in every post, use the Wikipedia tells list for self-editing, and use the narrower Medium wording [C70, C71].
4. **Build the cloud cost practice on a supplied sample report with read access first.** Google Cloud's free trial needs a payment method; AWS's new Free plan usually does not, but may ask; alert-only budgets do not cap spend; the managed billing read-only policy is broader than "costs"; the API costs a cent a call [C46 to C50]. Add a short chapter on MCP and GitHub prompt injection (read-only mode, one repo, no secrets) [C43 to C45], and keep secret handling in the same module [C53 to C55].
5. **Make the content chapters honest about luck and fake activity.** Use Berger and Milkman for "useful and emotionally real content travels", Salganik for "outcomes are unequal and unpredictable", StarScout for "fake stars help under two months and attract suspicion", and the Git author-date fact for why a graph is not proof [C59, C60, C66, C67]. Do not say LinkedIn demotes engagement bait; cite its community policy instead [C61 to C63].
6. **Critique chapter: do not depend on Copilot code review.** It is not on the Copilot Free plan and GitHub tells users to add human review [C56]. Use a chat assistant with the diff, require the learner to test each suggestion, and link to part B for sycophancy.
7. **Commit chapter: teach small real steps and the pull request as the story.** On squash-merge projects the main branch shows one commit [C74 to C76].
8. **Cut or park:** a hands-on Remotion, Motion Canvas or OBS lab (all need a laptop install; none has a browser path); LocalStack-based cost exercises (account and token needed, service coverage unknown) [C52, U16].
