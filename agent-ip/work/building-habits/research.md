The brief allows naming people where this dossier credits the idea to them; real companies, politics and war stay out of learner-facing text. Quotations are kept under 15 words and used only where exact wording matters. Popular books are summarised, never quoted at length. ## Learner and context

**Who.** Gaza fellowship fellows building careers in tech and remote work, from mixed backgrounds. Arabic first, English second. (Course brief.)

**What they already know.** Plain English reading. No prerequisite course. Many will have met popular habit advice on social media or from AI tools, much of it overstated (for example "21 days" or "just use willpower"). (Course brief; this dossier's findings 2, 10 and 20.)

**Device and connection.** Mostly phones, weak or expensive data, power cuts. Light pages, no images, interactions that save on their own. (Course brief.)

**Life context.** Displacement, shared homes, family duties and schedules that change often. Prayer times are part of many fellows' daily rhythm. The brief requires hardship to appear only as ordinary background and never asks fellows to share it. (Course brief.)

**Why the research matters for this audience in particular.** Three findings shape the course more than any other:

1. Habits are tied to stable contexts and are disrupted when the context changes [S1, S2, S26, S27]. For this audience context change is frequent. That is both a risk (habits break) and, according to the habit discontinuity research, an opening (old habits are weaker, so new ones can start) [S26].
2. People with good self-control mostly rely on habits and on avoiding temptation, not on constant resisting [S1, S24]. A course that teaches "try harder" would fail people whose days are already hard.
3. A single missed day did not materially affect habit formation in the best-known study [S4], and the most effective of 54 gym programmes in a very large trial rewarded people for coming back after a missed workout [S33]. Restarting is the skill to teach, not perfection.

**Time.** About 15 hours in the brief; the roadmap gives 945 minutes (about 16 hours). Second-language reading load at 1,200 words per Learn chapter is a real risk to completion (the decision log already lists it as risk R3).

## Domain model

### Actors

- **The fellow:** chooses, runs and reviews habits. Usually also a student, a freelancer or a remote worker.
- **The household:** family members, shared rooms, guests, children. They shape the context (noise, space, devices, times) and are often part of the habit (family meals, a walk together).
- **The fixed points of the day:** prayer times, meals, work shifts, school runs, the hours when power is on. These are the natural anchors.
- **Clients and employers:** set deadlines and time zones that move the day around.
- **The mentor:** reviews the 30-day plan and the tracking data.
- **AI tools and social media:** confident sources of habit advice, often overstated. The brief asks for "check the AI's answer" questions.

### The habit lifecycle (the spine of the course)

1. **Choose** one behaviour, small and specific, that the fellow wants and can repeat often [S9, S7].
2. **Tie it to a cue** that happens every day in a stable context: a time and place, or an existing routine (an anchor) [S9, S7, S17].
3. **Plan the moment** as an if-then plan: "If (situation), then I will (first step)" [S12, S13].
4. **Shape the context:** make the behaviour easier and the competing behaviour harder; prepare for days when the context breaks [S1, S29, S30].
5. **Repeat** in the same context. Expect automaticity to grow fastest at first and then level off, over weeks to months, very differently between people and behaviours [S4, S7, S8].
6. **Track** the action simply, with a note on misses [S15, S14].
7. **Miss, notice, restart.** One miss is not a reset; a long gap makes the next time less likely; restart at the next cue [S4, S5, S8, S33].
8. **Review** after about a month: keep, shrink, move the anchor, change the friction, or drop. One change at a time.

For unwanted habits the cycle runs differently: find the cue and what the habit gives you, remove or avoid the cue, add friction, plan a replacement for the same cue, watch for the slip in the moment, and expect slips [S1, S25, S29, S38].

## Hard constraints

1. **Reading load on phones.** Full prose minimums for second-language readers. Architecture consequence: one idea per chapter, numbers kept to the two or three that matter, worked cases in short steps. Every research number in this dossier has a "phrase it this way" line that avoids decimals and effect sizes.

2. **Other people's vocabulary.** The most familiar habit words are coinages by named authors (see the Credits table). The brief forbids presenting a book's framework as the course's method. Architecture consequence: chapters credit a coinage the first time it appears, then switch to the course's plain words ("anchor", "make it easier", "the smallest version", "restart at the next cue"). No chapter is built around a book's list of laws or steps.

3. **Numbers that are easy to overstate.** The famous figures are real but narrow: 66 days is a median among 39 people with a good model fit in one study [S4, S6]; 43 percent comes from hourly diaries of US college students [S3]; d = .65 for if-then plans is an average across 94 tests [S12]. Architecture consequence: graded questions test the shape of the finding (wide range, months not days, one miss is not a reset), never the exact number.

4. **Broken routines are normal.** Power cuts, weak internet, displacement and family duties break contexts often. Research shows habits weaken when context changes [S26, S27] and that habits formed in the new context carry on [S27]. Architecture consequence: every habit plan in the course has a backup version and an "if the context breaks, then..." line; disruption is taught as a normal design condition, not as a crisis, and personal prompts always offer a provided case.

5. **Rules of thumb with weak evidence.** "Start tiny", "never miss twice" and "anchor it to an existing routine" are useful but rest on indirect or exploratory evidence (findings 6, 7 and 13). Architecture consequence: chapters label them as the course's advice or as popular advice, say honestly what research supports, and graded keys test the reasoning taught (for example "which plan names a cue that happens every day?"), not "which rule is scientifically proven".

## Findings by topic

### 1. What a habit is, and how much of daily life runs on habit

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Habits are learned by repeating a behaviour in the same situation. Over time the situation itself starts the behaviour, with little thought [S1, S2].
- Wood and Rünger name two defining features: habits are started by recurring cues, and once formed they do not depend much on what you want at that moment, including how much you value the reward [S1].
- Cues can be places, times, other people, and the action you just finished [S1].
- Habits often form as a side effect of pursuing goals: you repeat what works, and the link to the situation is learned without meaning to [S1, S2].
- Habits are efficient. In diary studies, people often thought about something else while doing habitual actions, and habits came with less stress than new actions [S3].
- Once a habit is formed, intentions predict behaviour less well, because the situation starts it [S2, S14].
- How much of daily life is habitual: in two diary studies where US college students wrote down every hour what they were doing, between a third and a half of listed behaviours were done almost daily and usually in the same place (35 percent in one study, 43 percent in the other) [S3]. Wood's later review gives "about 43%" [S1], and her book page uses 43 percent [S57]. An earlier review described about 45 percent across student and community samples [S2].
- Habits mostly help start a behaviour. One study separated habits that start an action from habits that carry out every step, and found that the starting part predicted how often people did the behaviour [S18]. This matters for study and deep work: a habit can get you to the desk with the book open; the thinking itself stays effortful.

**Common misconceptions**
- "95 percent of what we do is unconscious." Wood, Quinn and Kashy noted their estimate was far lower than earlier speculation of about 95 percent [S3].
- "A habit is anything you do often." Frequency alone is not enough; the stable context and the cue are what make it automatic [S1, S3].
- "Habits are a sign of weak character" or "habits are laziness." Habits are how everyone saves effort [S1, S3].
- "Good people decide well each time." Most repeated behaviour is not decided each time [S1, S2].

**Phrase it this way**
- Definition: "A habit is something you do because a familiar situation starts it for you, with little thought. You learn it by doing the same thing in the same situation again and again." Credit: "Psychologists who study habits, such as Wendy Wood, describe them this way."
- The diary figure: "In one study, students wrote down every hour what they were doing. About a third to a half of their actions were done almost every day, in the same place." Do not say "43 percent of everything people do"; if a number is used, say "in one study of students".
- Do not give the 95 percent figure except as a myth.
- Habit versus decision versus goal: "A goal is what you want. A decision is a choice you make now. A habit is what the situation makes you do without choosing." (Course's wording.)

### 2. How long a habit takes, the 21-day myth, and missing one day

**The main study (Lally and colleagues, 2010)**
- 96 volunteers (mostly university students) each chose one daily eating, drinking or activity behaviour (for example a walk after dinner) and did it in the same context for 84 days, reporting each day whether they did it and how automatic it felt [S4, S6].
- Data from 82 people were usable. A levelling-off (asymptotic) curve fitted 62 people, and fitted well for 39 [S4, S6].
- Automaticity rose quickly at first and then levelled off: the curve's shape is the clearest finding [S4, S9].
- Time to reach 95 percent of each person's plateau ranged from 18 to 254 days [S4]. The median, among the 39 with a good fit, was 66 days [S6, S9, S5]. Many of these estimates go beyond the 84 days actually observed, so they are model predictions [S6]. About half of participants had not reached their plateau by the end of the study [S5].
- Exercise behaviours took about one and a half times as long as eating or drinking ones [S5]. The medians in a careful reading of the paper were 91 days for exercise (13 people), 65 for eating (10) and 59 for drinking (15), and the difference was not statistically significant [S6]. Say "exercise seemed to take longer", not "exercise takes 91 days".
- **Missing one opportunity** did not materially affect the habit-forming process [S4]. Automaticity gains resumed soon after one missed performance [S9]. Many missed repetitions, even spread out, lowered the level of automaticity people reached [S5]. The study did not test longer breaks [S6].
- Limits: self-reported automaticity, small sample, simple behaviours, mostly students; the authors themselves called the reliance on self-report a serious shortcoming [S5, S6].

**Later work**
- A 2024 systematic review (Singh and colleagues) found 20 studies with 2,601 adults on health habits. Only four reported time to habit formation: medians of 59 to 66 days, means of 106 to 154 days, and individual times from 4 to 335 days [S7]. Eleven studies were rated at high risk of bias, most were small, and the authors could not pool times [S7]. They conclude that health habits typically take about two to five months, with wide variation, and that the 21-day claim is not supported [S7].
- The same review reports that early repetitions give the largest gains, that morning practice formed stronger habits than evening practice in one stretching study, that habits people chose themselves were stronger than assigned ones, and that time-based and routine-based cues did not differ significantly [S7].
- A 2023 machine-learning study of more than 30,000 gym members and more than 3,000 hospital workers estimated that gym attendance took months to become habitual (median about four to seven months) while hospital handwashing took about two weeks (about 9 to 10 shifts) [S8]. The authors conclude there is no "magic number" of days [S8]. Limits: "habit" here means behaviour that is predictable from context, not self-reported automaticity, and the handwashing data started after monitoring was installed [S8].
- In the gym data, the time since the last visit was the most important predictor of whether a person went again, for 76 percent of members: the longer the gap, the less likely the next visit [S8].
- An older gym study estimated that going to the gym became automatic within about six weeks at four visits a week [S1, citing Kaushal and Rhodes 2015]. Different studies define "habit" differently, which is one reason the numbers differ.

**The 21-day myth**
- The 21-day figure appears to come from anecdotes about plastic surgery patients adjusting to their new appearance, not from habit research [S9, which cites Maltz's 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics]. Maltz's own wording was not read here (UNVERIFIED as a quotation).
- Gardner, Lally and Wardle suggest telling people to expect daily habits to take around 10 weeks [S9].

**Common misconceptions**
- "It takes 21 days to form a habit." [S7, S9]
- "It takes exactly 66 days." The 66 is a median from one small study, with a range of 18 to 254 [S4, S6].
- "Miss one day and you start from zero." [S4, S9]
- "Missing days does not matter at all." Many misses lowered the level reached [S5], and long gaps predicted stopping in the gym data [S8].
- "Once it is a habit, it is permanent." Old habit memories are not erased, and habits weaken when the context changes [S1, S27].

**Phrase it this way**
- "Researchers led by Phillippa Lally in London followed people who were building one small daily habit. For the typical person it took about two months before the habit felt automatic. But people were very different: some took under three weeks, and the model predicted that others would need most of a year."
- Prefer "about two months, often longer" to "66 days". If the number is used: "a median of 66 days in one study", never "it takes 66 days".
- "Think in months, not days. Easy habits (a glass of water) can come faster; harder ones (exercise) seem to take longer."
- "The habit grows fastest in the first weeks, then more slowly. Do not judge it in week one, and do not expect a sudden jump later."
- "Missing one day did not undo the progress in this study. What matters is getting back to it." Avoid "missing days never matters".
- 21 days: "The '21 days' idea comes from stories about how long patients took to get used to a new face after surgery, not from studies of habits."
- For the slider canvas in 1.4: the curve's shape (fast then levelling off) and the wide range are sourced. Any specific formula is a toy model and must be labelled as such.

### 3. Cue, routine and reward: what the "habit loop" gets right and where it oversimplifies

**The popular version**
- Charles Duhigg's book popularised the "habit loop": a cue, a routine and a reward, with a craving that builds as cue and reward become linked [S55]. His "golden rule" of habit change is to keep the cue and the reward and insert a new routine [S55].

**What research supports**
- Reward matters while a habit forms: rewarded responses are repeated, and the reward strengthens the link between the situation and the response [S1].
- As habits strengthen, behaviour depends less on the current value of the reward and more on the cue [S1, S2]. This is shown clearly in animals trained for a long time [S1].
- In people the picture is mixed. Five careful attempts to show that long training makes people keep responding after the reward has lost its value all failed [S20]. A large field study also did not find habits ignoring changes in reward in its gym and handwashing data, though the authors note this is hard to detect in field data [S8].
- Experiences of reward seem to matter more than beliefs about benefits: in one exploratory study, enjoyment and intrinsic motivation strengthened the link between repetition and habit, while believing the behaviour was useful did not [S19]. The authors did not manipulate reward, so this is not proof of cause [S19].
- A smartphone study found that positive reinforcement messages did not help habits form, while event-based cues did [S21].
- Habits form best with simple, frequent behaviours in stable contexts [S1, S9, S7].

**Where the popular version oversimplifies**
- The loop suggests every habit is driven by craving a reward. Research describes habits as cue-driven once formed, often running on even when you no longer want the outcome much [S1, S2]. Both "reward drives everything" and "reward does not matter" are wrong.
- Finding the reward by trial and error, as Duhigg suggests, is a reasonable practical method [S55], but it is his method, not a tested research procedure. UNVERIFIED as research.
- "Keep the cue, change the routine" fits research on replacing a response for the same cue [S1, citing Adriaanse et al. 2010; S47], but habit researchers also stress removing or avoiding the cue [S1, S25].

**Common misconceptions**
- "Find the reward and you control the habit."
- "Habits are about dopamine." The brief excludes neuroscience. Wood and Rünger describe dopamine as a learning signal that is strongest when the reward is new or surprising and weakens as the reward becomes expected [S1]; this is not the "dopamine hit" of popular talk, and chapters should not mention it.
- "If I stop enjoying it, the habit will stop." Strong habits can continue when enjoyment fades, which helps good habits survive low-motivation days and keeps bad habits going after they stop being fun [S1, S9].

**Phrase it this way**
- Credit: "The writer Charles Duhigg made this idea famous as the 'habit loop': a cue, a routine and a reward." Then use the course's words: "the situation, the action, and what you get from it".
- "A reward helps a habit form. Once a habit is strong, the situation can start it even when you no longer want the reward much. That is why good habits can carry you on tired days, and why bad habits keep going after they stop being fun." Keep "can", not "will".
- "Enjoying the habit itself seems to help it form more than knowing it is good for you." Credit: "one study of flossing and vitamins". Label it as a single exploratory study.
- No dopamine, no brain regions.

### 4. Motivation, goals and systems: why goals without habits fade

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Intentions and behaviour are linked, but loosely. Across many studies, intentions predict behaviour moderately well, yet a review concluded that intentions are carried out only about half the time [S14].
- Changing people's intentions changes their behaviour much less: across 47 experiments, a medium-to-large change in intention produced only a small-to-medium change in behaviour [S14, citing Webb and Sheeran 2006].
- The gap comes mostly from people who intend to act but do not [S14]. Main reasons: forgetting, missing the moment, not preparing, losing track, and old habits taking over [S14].
- Changed intentions worked for one-off behaviours but much less for behaviours that could become habits [S1, citing Webb and Sheeran 2006].
- Goals do help. In goal-setting research, specific, difficult goals led to better performance than "do your best" [S45]. Goals work better with feedback on progress [S45]. For tasks that are new and complex, learning goals (for example, find three ways to do it) can work better than a performance target [S45].
- Habits persist after conscious motivation fades [S9]. Everyone falls back on habits, good and bad, when they lack the energy or motivation to decide [S1, citing Neal et al. 2013].
- BJ Fogg's model says a behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt come together at the same moment [S56].

**Common misconceptions**
- "Get motivated first, then act." Motivation rises and falls; plans and cues carry the behaviour when it is low [S14, S9].
- "Goals are useless, only systems matter." Over-correction. Goals give direction and are well supported [S45]; habits carry them out day to day.
- "If I really wanted it, I would do it." Intending is not enough for most people [S14].

**Phrase it this way**
- "Wanting to change is the start, not the plan. Research on intentions finds that people carry out what they intend only about half the time."
- "A goal says where you want to go. A habit is the daily step that gets you there even on days you do not feel like it."
- Fogg credit: "BJ Fogg, a researcher at Stanford, describes it simply: a behaviour happens when you want to do it enough, it is easy enough, and something reminds you, all at the same moment." Do not present B=MAP as the course's own formula.
- Avoid the phrase "systems, not goals" as a slogan (it is associated with James Clear's book). Say "a goal plus a daily habit".

### 5. If-then plans (implementation intentions)

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Peter Gollwitzer named and studied "implementation intentions": plans of the form "if situation Y happens, then I will do X" [S11, S12].
- A 2006 meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that forming an if-then plan had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals compared with only intending (d = .65) [S12].
- A much larger 2025 meta-analysis of 642 tests found if-then plans effective for behaviour, thoughts and feelings, with effects between small and medium-to-large depending on the outcome [S13]. Effects were larger when plans were in if-then form, when people were strongly motivated, and when plans were rehearsed [S13].
- If-then plans help people remember to act, seize the moment, get started despite procrastination, and handle distractions, temptations and old habits [S14].
- They work by making the planned situation easy to notice and linking it to the response, so the response starts in a cue-driven way, like a habit [S14].
- Limits: if-then plans work less well against strong habits. In a smoking study they helped people with weak or moderate habits but not those with strong habits [S16]. Wood and Rünger also note they do not control many strong habits [S1]. They work mainly when people actually want the goal [S1, S13].

**Common misconceptions**
- "Any plan is an if-then plan." Effects were larger when the plan used the if-then form [S13].
- "If-then plans will break any habit." [S16]
- "If-then plans replace motivation." They help when motivation exists [S13].

**Phrase it this way**
- "A researcher named Peter Gollwitzer studied plans that name the moment: 'If it is after breakfast, then I open my notes.' Across many studies, people who made plans like this reached their goals more often than people who only intended to."
- "Many studies" or "more than six hundred tests" is fine; do not give effect sizes in chapters.
- "A strong if-then plan names a moment you will surely meet and a first step you can see." (Course's rule; follows from [S13, S14].)
- "If-then plans help most for new habits. Against a very strong old habit, add other tools: change the place, remove the cue."
- Common writing mistakes for quiz keys (course's rules derived from the above): a vague situation ("when I have time"), a vague action ("study more"), a situation that does not happen every day, two actions at once.

### 6. Anchors and habit stacking

**What exists**
- BJ Fogg's Tiny Habits method ties a new behaviour to an existing routine, which he calls an anchor [S51]. The recipe as widely described is "After I (anchor), I will (tiny behaviour)", followed by a small celebration (from secondary summaries of Fogg's recipe card; the book was not read; medium confidence).
- James Clear calls this "habit stacking", credits Fogg for the idea ("anchoring") and S.J. Scott for the phrase, and describes it as a kind of implementation intention that uses an existing habit as the trigger [S51].
- Habit researchers give the same advice independently: choose a cue that happens every day; an existing routine "provides a convenient and stable starting point" [S9]. Wood and Rünger note interventions that used tooth brushing as the cue for flossing [S1].

**Evidence**
- In an exploratory study of 50 people learning to floss, those told to floss after brushing formed stronger habits than those told to floss before brushing, and still flossed more often eight months later [S17]. The authors call it exploratory and ask for stronger designs [S17]. A later, larger thesis by the same first author reportedly found no effect of before or after placement (secondary report only; UNVERIFIED).
- The 2024 review found that time-based and routine-based cues did not differ significantly in the studies that compared them [S7].
- If-then plans have strong evidence [S12, S13], and an anchor plan is an if-then plan whose "if" is an existing routine [S51]. So the anchor idea borrows indirect support from if-then research.
- Reminders are not the same as cues. In a smartphone study, reminders helped people repeat a behaviour but did not help it become a habit; cues tied to an event in the day did [S21].
- No peer-reviewed randomised trial of the Tiny Habits method itself was found in this research (search on 2026-10-24). Fogg's own pages report helping thousands of people but give no study details [S56; tinyhabits.com, not cited as evidence].

**Common misconceptions**
- "Habit stacking is a scientifically proven technique." Its parts are well supported (stable cues, if-then plans); the named technique has not been tested as such.
- "Any routine is a good anchor." A good anchor happens every day, at a predictable moment, in the place where the new habit can happen (course's reasoning from [S1, S9, S7]).
- "A phone reminder is as good as an anchor." [S21]

**Phrase it this way**
- "Tie the new habit to something you already do every day: 'After I finish the dawn prayer, I will read one page.' The writer James Clear calls this 'habit stacking'; the researcher BJ Fogg, who came up with the idea, calls the existing routine an 'anchor'." Then use "anchor" as the course's plain word.
- "Habit researchers give the same advice: pick a cue that happens every day, in the same place."
- Evidence: "This builds on if-then plans, which have strong evidence. The idea of attaching a habit to a routine has been tested in only a few small studies, so treat it as good advice, not a proven law."
- "A reminder on your phone helps you remember. It does not build the habit. The habit grows from the situation."
- Moving anchors: "An anchor that moves (power hours that change every day) is weaker than one that stays (a meal, a prayer time, waking up)." This is the course's reasoning from the stable-context principle [S1]; label it as advice.

### 7. Starting tiny

**What exists**
- Habit researchers advise choosing a simple action that can be done daily and aiming for small changes [S9].
- A trial of a leaflet with ten small, simple diet and activity habits ("Ten Top Tips") in English primary care found more weight lost at three months than usual care (a difference under one kilogram), with the groups similar at 24 months [S10]. An earlier small trial of the same idea reported a larger short-term loss [S9].
- Simple behaviours seem to become automatic faster than complex ones: drinking water habits were stronger than exercise or fruit and vegetable habits in one study [S7], and exercise seemed to take longer in Lally's study [S5, S6].
- Fogg's "tiny" behaviours and Clear's "two-minute rule" (shrink a habit until it takes about two minutes to start; Clear credits David Allen's productivity rule as the origin) [S54] are popular practitioner methods. No controlled test of "start tiny" against "start at full size" for habit formation was found.
- Habits mostly start behaviour rather than run every step [S18], which gives a reason why a tiny start can lead to the full behaviour, but this is reasoning, not a test.

**Common misconceptions**
- "Tiny habits are scientifically proven to work best."
- "Tiny means you never grow." Fogg and Clear both present tiny as a start.
- "Small changes compound into huge results." See finding 20 on the 1 percent claim.

**Phrase it this way**
- "Start with a version so small that you can do it on your hardest day. Habit researchers advise choosing simple actions you can do every day. Popular writers such as BJ Fogg and James Clear go further and suggest starting with something that takes a minute or two. There are few direct tests of this, so think of it as sensible advice."
- "Grow the habit only after it starts by itself."
- The course's own term for this, "the smallest version" or "the backup version", avoids borrowing "tiny habits" (Fogg's trademarked method name per a secondary source) or "two-minute rule" (Clear's term).

### 8. Context, environment and habit discontinuity

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Habits are tied to their context. When the context changes, habits are disrupted. Students who moved to a new university showed disrupted habits (for example exercising), unless the new setting kept similar cues [S2, S25, citing Wood, Tam and Witt 2005].
- The habit discontinuity hypothesis says that big life changes (a move, a new job, a new phase of life) temporarily disturb habits, and that this is a good time to start new behaviour [S26]. In a field experiment with 800 people in an English city, a sustainability intervention worked better for people who had recently moved house than for people who had not, and the "window of opportunity" seemed to last about three months after the move [S26]. Outcomes were self-reported behaviours [S26].
- In a study of 283 adults during the 2020 lockdowns, physical activity habits dropped during lockdown, especially for people who had strong habits before; habits built during lockdown predicted activity at the end of lockdown [S27]. In plain words: a change of situation weakened old habits, and new habits built in the new situation carried on.
- Old habit memories are not erased; when old cues come back, old habits can return [S1].
- About 90 percent of regular exercisers in one study had a location or time cue for exercise, and exercise was more automatic for those cued by a particular place [S1, citing Tappe et al. 2013].

**Common misconceptions**
- "If my routine breaks, my habits are lost forever." They weaken, and can be rebuilt in the new context [S27].
- "Habits only depend on me." They depend heavily on the situation [S1].
- "A change of situation is only bad." It is also a window to start [S26].

**Phrase it this way**
- "Habits live in places and times. When your place or schedule changes, your habits are shaken, the good ones and the bad ones." Credit: "Researchers such as Wendy Wood and Bas Verplanken have shown this."
- "A change of situation is also a chance. When old cues disappear, it is easier to start a new habit. In one study, people who had just moved house changed their habits more easily, for about three months."
- Handle displacement gently and generally: "a new home, a new shift, a new term, a different household". Never "after displacement" as a case trigger; never ask what changed in the fellow's life.
- The COVID-19 lockdown study can be described as "a study during a time when many people had to stay at home". Do not use pandemic, illness or war as case material.

### 9. Friction, nudges and choice architecture

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Small changes in distance and availability change what people do. A Cochrane review found that placing food farther away moderately reduced how much people ate, and offering fewer options reduced how often people chose the targeted foods, but rated the certainty of the evidence as low [S29]. Most studies were small, in laboratories and in high-income countries [S29].
- A smartphone field experiment with 280 people found that an app that added a short pause and a choice to close the app before opening social media apps cut actual openings of those apps by 57 percent over six weeks; in a follow-up online experiment, the option to dismiss and the time delay worked, while a "think about it" message did not [S30].
- Wood's book uses "friction" for the small costs of effort, time and distance that push behaviour [S57].
- People high in self-control tend to arrange their lives to avoid temptation [S24, S1] (see finding 10).
- **The nudge controversy.** A 2022 meta-analysis (Mertens and colleagues) reported an average nudge effect of d = 0.43 [S31]. A reply by Maier and colleagues argued that after correcting for publication bias, no evidence remained that nudges are effective overall, with some evidence still possible for nudges that change the structure of choices [S31]. A study of 126 trials run by two large government nudge units found average effects of 1.4 percentage points, against 8.7 in published academic studies, and attributed about 70 percent of the gap to selective publication [S32].
- In a very large gym trial of 54 four-week programmes, 45 percent increased visits during the programme, but only 8 percent produced measurable change after it ended, and experts could not predict which would work [S33].

**Common misconceptions**
- "Nudges have big effects." Published effects are inflated; real-world effects are small on average [S31, S32].
- "Environment design guarantees behaviour change." It shifts the odds [S29].
- "Popular food studies prove it." Several famous food-psychology studies (plates, bowls, buffets) came from a researcher with 17 retracted papers and a misconduct finding by his university [S59]; Wood and Rünger's review cites one of his buffet studies [S1]. Do not use these examples.

**Phrase it this way**
- "Make the good action a few steps closer and the unwanted action a few steps farther. Small changes in distance and effort change what people do, though usually by a modest amount, not by magic."
- "In one study, a phone app that added a pause before social media apps opened cut how often people opened them by about half." This is a fair plain-words version of 57 percent [S30]; better still: "by more than half".
- Friction credit: "Wendy Wood, a psychologist who studies habits, calls these small costs 'friction'." Then use "easier" and "harder" as the course's words.
- Never claim a percentage for a fellow's own redesign. Never present nudges as large effects.
- Use the course's own everyday examples: phone charging in another room, book open on the table, course page downloaded while the power is on. Not plates, bowls or buffets.

### 10. Willpower, self-control and ego depletion

**Safe to say in plain words**
- "Ego depletion" is the idea that self-control is a limited resource used up by effort. Two large, preregistered, multi-lab replications did not find the effect: 23 labs with 2,141 people found an effect close to zero (d = 0.04) [S22], and 36 labs with 3,531 people also found no evidence in their preregistered tests [S23].
- People high in self-control mostly do not win by resisting more. Across six studies with 2,274 people, better self-control went with stronger beneficial habits (exercise, study, sleep timing), and habits explained part of the link between self-control and good outcomes such as grades [S24]. An experience-sampling study they cite found that people with better self-control were less likely to report trying to resist temptations [S24, citing Hofmann et al. 2012; S1].
- Wood and Rünger summarise: people with high self-control seem to reach goals by forming habits that let them avoid unwanted temptations, rather than by inhibiting desires [S1].
- Difficulty changing a habit is not mainly a failure of willpower; old habits keep being triggered by the same cues [S1].
- A separate claim, that people high in self-control choose environments with fewer distractions, comes from a study whose abstract was not available here (Ent, Baumeister and Tice 2015). UNVERIFIED in detail; the direction agrees with [S1, S24].

**Common misconceptions**
- "Willpower is a muscle that runs out." The replications did not support the depletion effect [S22, S23]. (A reviewer of the replication noted its task may have been weak; say "the large tests did not find it", not "proven false".)
- "People with discipline resist temptation all day."
- "You failed because your willpower is weak."

**Phrase it this way**
- "For years, a popular idea said willpower is like a battery that runs down. When many laboratories repeated the classic experiments carefully, they did not find the effect."
- "People who seem disciplined usually face fewer temptations. They have habits and surroundings that make the good choice the normal one."
- "Do not plan to fight the urge. Plan the place and the moment so the fight does not happen."
- Avoid "willpower does not exist". Effortful self-control is real; the battery model is what failed to replicate.

### 11. Breaking unwanted habits

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Old habits are not erased; they are triggered by the same cues, so changing them takes more than intending [S1].
- What helped in research:
  - **Vigilant monitoring in the moment.** In two diary studies, the strategy that best controlled strong bad habits was watching carefully for slip-ups and telling yourself "don't do it" [S25]. It worked by increasing control, not by weakening the habit [S25]. In those studies, people's spontaneous use of avoiding the situation helped with temptations but not with strong habits, possibly because habit cues are hard to spot [S25].
  - **Changing or avoiding the cues.** Changing the environment reduces interference from old habits; life changes that remove cues are a window [S1, S26].
  - **If-then plans tied to the cue**, naming a replacement: for example "after dinner, I'll skip dessert as usual and substitute fruit" [S1, citing Adriaanse et al. 2010]. Weaker against very strong habits [S16].
  - **A competing response.** Habit reversal training, a clinical method, teaches awareness of the habit, a competing response that is incompatible with it, and support from others [S47]. A meta-analysis of 18 studies found it effective for tics, nail biting and similar repetitive behaviours [S46]. The everyday idea of a replacement action for the same cue borrows from this; the clinical method itself is out of scope.
  - **Add a new behaviour rather than only stopping one.** Gardner and colleagues advise choosing a new behaviour to do rather than giving one up, because "it is not possible to form a habit for not doing something" [S9].
- What does not work well: trying not to think about it. A meta-analysis of 31 studies found that suppressed thoughts come back afterwards (rebound) [S48]. Distraction is also unlikely to stop a habit, because habits can run while attention is elsewhere [S25].
- Slips are normal; see findings 13 and 14.

**Common misconceptions**
- "Just stop."
- "Delete everything and use willpower." (One of the writer pack's AI-answer mistakes.)
- "If I do not think about it, I will not do it." [S48, S25]
- "Avoiding the place always works." Good for temptations and for removing cues in advance [S1, S25]; for strong habits in the moment, watch for the slip [S25].

**Phrase it this way**
- "First find what starts the habit and what it gives you. Then remove the start if you can, make the habit harder to do, choose something else to do at the same moment that gives you some of the same thing, and watch for the moment it begins."
- Monitoring credit: "In one study, people who controlled bad habits best watched closely for the moment the habit started and told themselves 'not this time'." Keep it short.
- "Trying not to think about something often makes it come back. Plan what you will do instead."
- Replacement: "Keep the cue, meet the same need, change the action." Credit the idea to Duhigg only if his "golden rule" is named; otherwise present as the course's advice supported by [S1, S47].
- One kind sentence (brief): habits close to addiction or health problems need a mentor, doctor or counsellor. Do not teach habit reversal therapy.

### 12. Tracking and self-monitoring

**Safe to say in plain words**
- A meta-analysis of 138 studies with nearly 20,000 people found that interventions that got people to monitor their progress increased monitoring a lot and improved goal attainment by a small-to-moderate amount (d = 0.40) [S15]. Effects were larger when progress was physically recorded (for example written down) or made public [S15].
- Most studies were about health goals [S15].
- People often avoid checking progress when they fear bad news; researchers call this "the ostrich problem" [S14].
- Feedback on progress makes goals work better [S45].
- Streaks (see finding 13) are one way to display tracking, with specific effects.

**Common misconceptions**
- "Tracking is the habit." Tracking supports the habit; it does not replace the cue.
- "Track everything." No research here supports tracking many things; keep it to the action (course's advice).
- "Track the result (weight, income), not the action." Effects were larger when what was monitored matched what people wanted to change [S14, describing S15]. For a habit, the action is what you control (course's reasoning).

**Phrase it this way**
- "Writing down whether you did it helps. Across many studies, people who kept track of their progress reached their goals more often, especially when they wrote it down or shared it."
- "Track the action you control (I read one page), not only the result."
- "Add one word when you miss: why. Those notes are the data for your 30-day review." (Course's advice.)
- Do not give d values. "Many studies" is enough.

### 13. Streaks, missing a day, and "never miss twice"

**Safe to say in plain words**
- One missed opportunity did not materially affect habit formation in Lally's study [S4, S9]. Many misses lowered the level reached [S5].
- In gym data, the longer the gap since the last visit, the less likely the next visit, for most members [S8].
- In the 54-programme gym trial, the most effective programme gave small rewards for returning to the gym after a missed workout [S33].
- **Streaks.** Across seven studies, people who saw an intact streak in their record kept going more than people who saw a broken one, even when their actual past behaviour was the same [S37]. The effect was stronger when people blamed themselves for the break and weaker when they could repair the streak [S37]. The authors suggest apps can show streaks without a cost when streaks break [S37]; the abstract does not compare a broken streak with showing no streak, so whether a broken streak lowers effort below baseline is not established here.
- **The trap after a lapse.** In relapse prevention (Marlatt), the abstinence violation effect is the reaction to a first slip: guilt and the belief that it shows personal failure, which makes giving up more likely [S38]. Seeing the slip as "a mistake in learning" that calls for better planning is the recommended reframe [S38]. This comes from alcohol treatment; for everyday habits it is an analogy.
- Restrained eaters who ate a forbidden food tended to eat more afterwards (the "disinhibition effect"), and a short self-compassion message reduced this [S40]. This is the pattern popularly called the "what-the-hell effect" (name origin not checked; UNVERIFIED). Eating is out of scope as case material.
- "Never miss twice" is James Clear's advice: one miss is an accident, two in a row start a pattern [S52]. The only research he cites is the Lally study's finding that one miss did not matter [S52]. No study tested a "twice" threshold.

**Common misconceptions**
- "You broke your streak, so start over." [S4]
- "Never miss twice is a research finding." It is advice [S52].
- "Back-to-back misses are worse than spread misses." Not tested as such; a longer gap predicting stopping [S8] and many misses lowering the plateau [S5] are what research shows. UNVERIFIED as stated.
- "Streaks are bad." They help while intact [S37]; the risk is how a break is interpreted.

**Phrase it this way**
- "Missing one day does not erase your progress. What matters is the next time the cue comes."
- "The longer the gap, the harder it is to start again, so restart at the very next cue, even with the smallest version." [S8, S33]
- "Count days done, not only days in a row. 24 of 30 days is a strong month even if your longest streak was 9." (Arithmetic and course advice.)
- "If you use a streak, allow a repair: a grace day, or 'done' for the smallest version." (Course advice consistent with [S37].)
- Never miss twice: "The writer James Clear gives a popular rule: never miss twice. It is good advice, not a research finding. This course's version is simpler: restart at the next cue."
- The trap: "After one slip, it is easy to think 'I have already failed, so why bother'. Researchers who help people avoid relapse call this a common trap, and teach people to see a slip as a lesson, not a verdict." Name Marlatt only if needed: "a psychologist named Alan Marlatt".

### 14. Relapse, restarting, fresh starts and self-compassion

**Safe to say in plain words**
- A lapse is not a relapse. Going from one slip to giving up is not inevitable, and what you tell yourself about the slip matters [S38].
- **Fresh starts.** Dai, Milkman and Riis found, in three archival studies, that searches for "diet", gym visits and commitments to goals all rose after temporal landmarks such as the start of a week, month, year or semester, a birthday or a holiday [S35]. They suggest landmarks make past failures feel like they belong to an "old self" [S35]. These are correlational field data.
- A field experiment with more than 6,000 university employees found that offering to start retirement savings on a "fresh start" date (a birthday, the first day of spring, the new year) raised sign-ups for that future date by about half compared with an ordinary date, with no drop in people starting immediately [S36]. A related paper reported that highlighting a future fresh start can make people already working toward a goal slack off [S36].
- No published replication failure of the fresh start effect was found in this research; no preregistered replication was found either. Status: supported by archival and some experimental field data, not yet widely replicated.
- **Self-compassion.** In four experiments, students prompted to be kind to themselves after a failure studied longer for a retest and were more motivated to improve than students given a self-esteem boost or no prompt [S39]. Students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before a first exam procrastinated less before the next one [S41]. A self-compassion message reduced overeating after a "forbidden" food among restrictive eaters [S40].
- Being kind to yourself is not the same as excusing: the self-compassion studies found more motivation to improve, not less [S39].

**Common misconceptions**
- "Wait for Monday (or the new month) to restart." A fresh start can help motivation [S35], but waiting extends the gap, and gaps predict stopping [S8]. Use the next cue; use a landmark as an extra push.
- "Being hard on yourself keeps you disciplined." [S39, S41]
- "Fresh start is a proven law." Mostly correlational plus a few field experiments.

**Phrase it this way**
- "A slip is one missed day. Giving up is a decision. You can have the first without the second."
- "Researchers have noticed that people start goals more often after a new beginning: a new week, a new month, a birthday. You can use this: a new week is a good moment to restart. But do not wait for it. The best time to restart is the next cue."
- "Be kind to yourself after a miss. In experiments, students who were kind to themselves after failing a test studied longer for the next one than students who were simply told to feel good about themselves." Credit: "researchers Juliana Breines and Serena Chen" only if a name helps.
- "Kind is not the same as careless: notice what went wrong, plan one change, then restart."

### 15. Plateaus and slow results

**What research shows**
- Habit strength (automaticity) grows fastest at the start and then levels off [S4, S7, S9]. Early repetitions produce the largest gains [S7]. This is the opposite of a long flat period followed by a breakthrough, at least for how automatic the behaviour feels.
- Skill practice curves also rise fastest early and then slow (Heathcote and colleagues found exponential curves fit individual learning data better than power curves; both shapes slow down over time) [S60]. Chapters do not need this source; it confirms the shape.
- Outcomes (fitness, weight, skill, income) can lag behind behaviour, and habit interventions showed modest early effects [S10]. No study was found that tests a general "plateau then breakthrough" pattern for habits.
- The "plateau of latent potential" is a popular image from James Clear's Atomic Habits (credit from secondary summaries; medium confidence), illustrated with an ice cube that does not melt until it reaches a threshold [S53 for Clear's site; the ice-cube quote appears on his quotes page]. It is a metaphor, not a research finding.

**Common misconceptions**
- "Nothing happens for weeks, then everything changes." For automaticity, the first weeks are when it grows most [S4, S7].
- "If I see no results, the habit is not working." Results such as fitness or grades can take longer to show than the habit itself (course's reasoning; no general research claim).

**Phrase it this way**
- "Two different things grow at different speeds. The habit itself gets easier fastest in the first weeks. The results you want, such as fitness, skill or grades, can take longer to show."
- "Judge the habit by how easy it feels to start, not only by the results."
- If Clear's image is used: "The writer James Clear compares slow results to an ice cube warming up before it melts. It is a helpful picture, not a research finding."
- Do not state "results come in a sudden jump" or give a time.

### 16. Identity

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Identity-based motivation (Daphna Oyserman): people prefer actions that feel like "what people like me do", and they read difficulty through their identity. When an action feels congruent, difficulty means it is important; when it feels incongruent, the same difficulty suggests the action is "not for people like me" [S42]. This research is mostly about school achievement [S42].
- Habits and identity are linked: people tend to see their habits as reflecting their true self, especially habits tied to their central values, but the studies are mostly correlational and the direction of cause is unknown [S44].
- A famous study found that asking people about "being a voter" (a noun) rather than "voting" (a verb) increased turnout. A large replication found no effect [S43]. (The topic is politics: do not use it in chapters. Its value here is as a warning that clever identity wording effects can fail.)
- James Clear's book promotes "identity-based habits": decide who you want to be and prove it with small wins; each action is a vote for that identity [S53]. This is his framing.

**Common misconceptions**
- "Change your identity first, then habits follow."
- "Saying 'I am a runner' makes you run." The noun-wording effect did not replicate [S43].
- "Difficulty means this is not for me." Oyserman's work suggests that reading is changeable [S42].

**Phrase it this way**
- "Each time you do the habit, you collect a little evidence about the kind of person you are becoming." Present as the course's framing; credit: "James Clear's book makes a similar point."
- "When a new habit feels hard, that does not mean it is 'not for people like you'. Hard can mean it matters." Credit, if any: "a psychologist named Daphna Oyserman studies how people read difficulty."
- Do not claim identity change must come first, or that a label changes behaviour. Do not use the voting study.

### 17. Daily rhythm: prayer times, sleep, health, family, study and remote work

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Good cues happen every day, at a predictable time, in a stable place [S1, S9, S7]. Fixed daily events (waking, meals, prayers, a work shift) fit this description.
- Time-based and routine-based cues did not differ significantly in the studies the 2024 review found [S7]. Morning practice formed stronger habits than evening practice in one stretching study [S7].
- **Prayer.** No controlled research was found on prayer times as anchors for other habits (search on 2026-10-24 found only descriptive education papers and unsourced web pages). The course can use prayer times as an example of a fixed daily event, because the general principle (stable, daily, predictable cues) is well sourced. No claim about prayer's effects on habits, health, discipline or wellbeing may be made.
- **Sleep.** CDC guidance: adults aged 18 to 60 need 7 or more hours a night; keep a regular bed and wake time; switch off electronics at least 30 minutes before bed; avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening [S49]. A large observational study linked more regular sleep timing with lower risk of death [S50]; do not use it in chapters (frightening, observational, and out of scope).
- **Study and focused work.** People with good self-control had stronger study habits, which helped explain better grades [S24]. Habits mostly help with starting [S18]; studying itself stays thoughtful [S3, S18]. Complex tasks need more thought than simple ones even when repeated [S3].
- **Family and shared homes.** No specific research was used. Course advice only.
- **Remote work across time zones.** No specific research was used. Course advice only: anchor to events that stay fixed in your own day, not to the client's clock.

**Common misconceptions**
- "Prayer makes habits easier" or any similar religious effect claim. Not sourced; not allowed.
- "Morning is always best." One study [S7].
- "Deep work can become automatic." The start can; the work stays effortful [S18].

**Phrase it this way**
- Prayer: "Many fellows' days already have fixed points: waking up, meals, prayer times, the start of a work shift. Fixed points make good anchors because they happen every day at a known moment." Keep prayer as one example among several (the roadmap's open decision 6 says the same).
- Prayer times shift through the year with the sun, so a habit anchored to a prayer moves with it. (General knowledge, safe to state without a source; phrase neutrally.)
- Sleep: "Health agencies advise most adults to sleep at least seven hours and to keep a regular time for going to bed and getting up." No risk numbers.
- Study: "A habit can get you to the desk with the right page open. The thinking is still work. Make the start automatic: the same place, the same signal, the phone in another room, and tomorrow's first step written down at the end of today." (Course's advice from [S18, S1, S29].)
- "Deep work" is Cal Newport's term (UNVERIFIED here; not read). Use "focused work" in chapters unless instructors approve the credit.

### 18. Habits under instability: power cuts, weak internet, moving, changing schedules

**Safe to say in plain words**
- Habits depend on stable contexts; when the context changes, habits are disrupted [S1, S2].
- In the lockdown study, strong habits fell the most when the context changed, and habits rebuilt in the new context predicted later behaviour [S27]. Continuing to do the behaviour and doing it for one's own reasons helped people keep going [S27].
- Across 53 studies from five continents during the pandemic, disruption of daily routines (especially activity, eating and sleep) was linked with more symptoms of depression and anxiety; the associations were small and the data correlational [S28]. This supports the general value of keeping some routine, not a claim that routines cure distress. Do not use in chapters as a mental health claim.
- Under stress or low energy, everyone falls back on habits [S1, citing Neal et al. 2013]. That is a reason to build good habits before hard days, and to have a very small version ready.
- If-then plans can name obstacles as well as opportunities and help people handle distractions and interruptions [S14, S12].

**Course design that follows (course's reasoning, label as advice)**
- Three versions of each habit: full, backup, smallest.
- "If there is no power, then..." and "if the internet is down, then..." plans.
- Download or print while power and data are available; keep a paper tracker.
- Anchor to events that survive disruption (waking, meals, prayers) rather than to power hours.
- Waiting for power is not failure; restart at the next cue.

**Common misconceptions**
- "A habit that broke during a hard week was never real." [S27]
- "Routine fixes stress." Overstated [S28].

**Phrase it this way**
- "When the situation changes, habits wobble. That is normal, not a personal failure. Shrink the habit to its smallest version and keep the cue."
- "Plan for the day the power is off before it happens: 'If there is no power at 8:00, then I read the printed page by the window.'"
- Never frame the reasons for power cuts or moving; they are background conditions.

## Technical landscape

No software tools are needed. Paper or a notes app is enough (brief). Notes for canvases and quizzes:

- **1.4 `habit-strength-curve` (slider):** the sourced facts are the levelling-off shape, fast early growth, a typical time of about two months, a wide range, and one miss not resetting progress [S4, S7, S9]. Any formula is a toy model; say so in `limits`. Do not make a missed day reduce strength to zero; do not show a sudden late jump.
- **1.3 `habit-loop-repeats` (state machine):** the claim that a strong habit runs from the cue even when the reward is weak should be phrased "can", with the human evidence noted as mixed [S1, S20].
- **5.3 `streak-or-done-count` (slider):** streak and days-done counts are arithmetic. "Back-to-back misses do more harm than spread ones" is a toy rule, not a research finding (see Notes on the writer pack).
- **Trackers:** recommend paper or any simple tracker; no app tutorials (brief non-goal). If a phone tracker is used, reminders support repetition but do not build the habit [S21].
- **Licences:** no templates from Clear, Duhigg or Fogg (their recipe cards, scorecards and flowcharts are copyrighted). The course's own tracker and plan formats only.

## Failure modes

### How habit plans fail in real life (sourced)

1. **The plan is an intention, not a plan.** People intend but do not act about half the time; forgetting and missing the moment are the main reasons [S14]. Fix: name the cue and the first step [S12, S13].
2. **The cue is unstable.** Habits need a stable context [S1, S9]; a cue that does not happen daily does not build automaticity.
3. **Reminders instead of cues.** Reminders keep people repeating but do not make the behaviour automatic [S21].
4. **Judging too early.** Habits take weeks to months, very variably [S4, S7, S8]; people quit before the curve levels off.
5. **One miss becomes giving up.** The abstinence violation trap [S38]; long gaps then predict stopping [S8]; harsh interpretation of a broken streak, especially with self-blame [S37].
6. **Fighting temptation instead of avoiding it.** People with good self-control rely on habits and avoid temptation [S1, S24].
7. **A strong old habit meets only an if-then plan.** If-then plans are weaker against strong habits [S16].
8. **Trying not to think about it.** Suppression rebounds [S48]; distraction does not stop habits [S25].
9. **The context changes and the habit is not rebuilt.** Strong habits fell most during lockdown [S27]; old habits return when old cues return [S1].
10. **Effects that last only while the programme runs.** Most gym programmes stopped working when they ended [S33]. Fix: a habit that does not depend on the programme (cue, context, tracking the fellow owns).
11. **Too much at once.** No direct research was used; course reasoning from "simple behaviours form faster" [S7] and the gap between intention and action [S14].

### When teaching this to beginners (the course's main risks)

1. **Numbers taught as rules** (21, 66, 43 percent). Guard: the "phrase it this way" lines; quizzes test the shape, not the number.
2. **Book frameworks taught as the method.** Guard: Credits table; one book idea per chapter; course's own words afterwards.
3. **Willpower blame.** Guard: module 4 opens with the replication story and the self-control-through-habits finding [S22, S24].
4. **Perfectionism through streaks.** Guard: days done plus restart; streak repair.
5. **Overclaiming small techniques** (tiny, anchors, never miss twice). Guard: honest evidence lines; label as advice.
6. **Faith or hardship handled badly.** Guard: prayer as one fixed point among several; no effects claimed; disruption as normal background; provided cases always.
7. **Food and diet cases** drift into eating-disorder territory (out of scope). Guard: use sleep, study, phone use, walking, reading, water, family time.
8. **Retracted examples.** Guard: no plate, bowl or buffet studies [S59].
9. **AI answers that sound right.** Guard: "check the AI's answer" items built from finding 20.

## Notes on the writer pack (claims to correct before writing)

1. **5.3 canvas: "back-to-back misses do more harm than spread ones".** Not a research finding. Sourced: one miss did not materially matter [S4]; many misses lowered the level reached [S5]; a longer gap predicted not returning in gym data [S8]. Phrase: "A long gap makes it harder to restart" (sourced); keep any back-to-back rule as a labelled toy rule, or drop it.
2. **1.3: "strong habits run from the cue even when the reward fades".** Phrase with "can" and avoid "even when the reward is missing" as a firm claim [S1, S20].
3. **5.4: "results often come later than effort".** Fine as an everyday observation, not a research claim. Pair it with the sourced point that the habit itself gets easier fastest early [S4, S7].
4. **2.4: "habit stacking" credit.** Clear credits the idea to Fogg and the phrase to S.J. Scott [S51]. Credit Fogg's "anchor" and Clear's "habit stacking" together once.
5. **4.2: "people who seem to have strong self-control mostly face fewer temptations".** Supported as "rely on habits and report fewer attempts to resist" [S24, S1]. Do not say they "never feel temptation".
6. **4.4: "the dossier's wording on monitoring".** Vigilant monitoring (watching for the slip, "don't do it") is the in-the-moment tool for strong habits [S25]; removing cues is the in-advance tool [S1]. Teach both, in that order.
7. **5.4: fresh start "replication note".** No replication failure was found; no preregistered replication either. Phrase: "seen in several large data sets and some experiments; not yet tested as widely as some other findings."
8. **6.4: sleep.** Only the CDC basics [S49]; no risk or mortality numbers.

## Open questions for instructors

1. **Naming people.** The brief allows credits. Is two or three names per chapter the right ceiling, and should Clear, Duhigg and Fogg be named as "writers" and "researchers" as this dossier phrases them?
2. **The 66 days.** Teach the number with its caveat in 1.4, or only "about two months, often longer, very different between people"? Recommendation: the second in chapters, the number only in a "where this comes from" aside.
3. **Back-to-back misses.** The 5.3 canvas uses an untested rule (U6). Keep it labelled as a toy rule, or change the output to "longest gap" only?
4. **Prayer anchors.** Which examples do instructors approve (for example "after the dawn prayer", "after the evening prayer")? Should chapters say that prayer times move through the year, so a habit anchored to one moves too?
5. **Grading a disrupted month.** What does a "Met" look like for a fellow whose tracking shows a month of power cuts and moves, and no habit forming? Recommendation: grade the review and the restart plan, not the outcome.
6. **"Never miss twice".** Credit and use it, or use only the course's "restart at the next cue"?
7. **Referral sentence.** Is one kind sentence in 4.1 enough for habits close to addiction or health problems, and who should fellows contact (mentor, programme staff)?
8. **"Deep work".** Name Cal Newport's term in 6.3, or use "focused work" only?
9. **Online courses.** Should a human reviewer check one or two existing MOOCs on habits for overlap before G2B? This dossier did not.
10. **Provided cases.** The writer pack's cases (Ziad's walk, Rasha's phone, Hamza's sweet drink) fit the research. Should the sweet-drink case stay, given the food-case caution? Recommendation: keep it as a purchase habit at a shop, never as weight or diet.
