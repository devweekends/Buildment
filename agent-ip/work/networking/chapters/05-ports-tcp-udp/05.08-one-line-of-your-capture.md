This is the gate for module 5. Your evidence is one line of your own capture, explained number by number. You will pick any line from the handshake in `handshake.txt`, the file you committed in challenge 3, part 1. You have met every piece in the last six chapters, so nothing here is new. The gate asks you to put the pieces together in your own words. Your answer saves as you type, so you can stop after a power cut and come back.

A mentor reads your answer within three days. You do not have to wait. You can carry on to module 6 as soon as you submit, and if the mentor has a question, it reaches you while you work. You answer it by editing and sending your answer again. Your committed files from challenge 3 are part of the evidence as well, so check that `bash test.sh` passes before you submit.

## What your explanation must cover

Your reader knows what an address is and has heard of a port. That is all. Four things make an explanation they can follow.

The first is the five numbers. Name both addresses, both ports and the protocol, and say which end each one belongs to. A line of tcpdump shows two addresses with a port after each, joined by an arrow. The protocol is the one the conversation uses, TCP here. Together these five numbers name the conversation, and no other conversation on the computer has the same five.

The second is the source port. The server's port is 8080, because the server promised to answer there. Say why the other port is not 8080. The laptop is the client, and its computer gave it a free port for this conversation. Say which range that port comes from on Linux, and whether IANA calls it dynamic. Your port may be below or above 49152, so check yours.

The third is the flag and the numbers. Say which step of the handshake your line is, and how the letters in `Flags` show it: `[S]` is SYN, `[S.]` is SYN-ACK, and `[.]` is the plain ACK. Then say what the sequence number and the acknowledgement number on that line are for. An ack names the next byte the sender of that line expects. In a SYN-ACK it is the other side's starting number plus one.

The fourth is the listening address. Say how your line depends on the promise the server made in chapter 5.3. If the server had listened on `127.0.0.1`, the laptop's SYN would have been refused, so the SYN-ACK and the ACK would not exist. Use one real number from your own capture in every part. About 250 words are enough, or about three minutes if you speak. A voice note is as good as text.

## A worked example, for a different line

Here is the same shape of explanation for the second line of the course's own recorded run. It is a SYN-ACK. Your capture has different numbers, and you may pick a different line, so use this only to see the shape. These are recorded numbers from the course lab, not yours.

```
10:39:05.171824 IP 192.168.1.80.8080 > 192.168.1.23.48158: Flags [S.], seq 1089803950, ack 557210666, win 65160, options [mss 1460,sackOK,TS val 2147467152 ecr 710427391,nop,wscale 7], length 0
```

A weak answer says: "It is a SYN-ACK from the server to the laptop, and it has some ports and numbers." It is not wrong, but it explains nothing. Your reader cannot check it, and it names none of the five numbers.

Another weak answer says: "The server uses port 8080 and the laptop uses 8080 too." This one is wrong. The laptop's port is 48158, and it is not the server's port. It also says nothing about the flag or the numbers.

A strong answer says: "This is the second packet of the handshake. It goes from the server, 192.168.1.80 port 8080, to the laptop, 192.168.1.23 port 48158, over TCP. Those are the five numbers. Port 8080 is the port the server promised to answer on. The server was listening on 0.0.0.0, so it answered. A server on 127.0.0.1 would have refused the laptop's SYN and sent no SYN-ACK. The laptop's port, 48158, is not 8080 because the laptop is the client. Linux gave it a free port from its range, 32768 to 60999, and it is below 49152, so IANA would not call it dynamic. The flag S dot means SYN-ACK, the second step. The seq 1089803950 is the server's own starting number. The ack 557210666 is the laptop's starting number, 557210665, plus one. It tells the laptop that the server expects byte 557210666 next." This answer names the five numbers, explains the port, ties the flag to its step, and ends with a fact you can check in the line. The weak answers do none of these.

The rest of the line, `win`, `options` and `length`, is not part of this module. You do not have to explain it.

## Where this goes next

In module 6 your home router will keep a table with one row for each conversation. Each row ties an address and a port inside the network to an address and a port outside [S18]. A router that shares one outside address between many devices must remember which conversation belongs to which device. The numbers in your line are the numbers that table is made of. So the explanation you write now is also the first half of how NAT works.

## If your result was different

Two results can look different from the recorded run. Neither is a mistake, and neither needs a new idea.

Your ports and numbers will not match the recording. The starting numbers, the laptop's port and the times are different in every run. Use your own. If your laptop's port is above 49152, it sits inside both ranges, and you can say so.

If the third line of your capture shows a number other than `ack 1`, do not worry. Check it against the rule that an ack names the next byte expected. It is the other side's starting number plus one, or a small number counted from the start of the conversation.
