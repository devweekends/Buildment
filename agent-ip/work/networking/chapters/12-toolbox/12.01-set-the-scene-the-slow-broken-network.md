A friend says "the internet is slow". You now know a dozen places where a network can break: the cable, a router, a name, a port, a full link. Which one is it this time? In this module one lab network hides four faults, and each chapter gives you one tool to find one of them. Today you plant the faults and try with the two tools you already have: `ping` and the trace from 4.4.

## Plant four faults with your seed

The script builds these five lab computers and hides four faults in them. Your seed is a word, such as your first name. It turns into your own numbers: which name breaks, which port, how much loss. The lab has no git, so always type `--seed` with your word, or the script picks a random one.

|---|---|---|---|

| 2 | lab | `bash faults.sh --seed YOUR_NAME` | Builds the network, plants the faults, and prints the service sheet and three complaints |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the seed, the service sheet and starting again">

The seed is saved in `fault-seed.txt` in the same folder. The same seed always gives the same numbers, so a mentor who runs your seed sees exactly your faults. Another learner with another word gets another port, another name and another amount of loss. That is why the tests in this challenge never check a fixed number. They read your seed and compare your answers with your own numbers.

The first block of the output is the service sheet. It says what should be true in a healthy network: five names that all mean `203.0.113.80`, which resolver the laptop asks, which ports the server has open, and that every cable is fast and loses nothing. Read it as a promise. Every fault you find later is a place where the network breaks that promise. Write down your app port now. You need it for complaint C, and again in 12.6.

The second block is three complaints, written the way a person would say them. A complaint is not a cause. "Copying a file is slow" tells you what someone felt, not what broke. Notice that the script warns you: two different faults make that first complaint. Only evidence will tell them apart.

Three options of `faults.sh` help you later. `bash faults.sh` with no option uses the seed already in `fault-seed.txt`. `bash faults.sh --clean` builds the network with no faults at all, so you can see what healthy output looks like. Run `bash faults.sh` again to bring the faults back. Each run builds the whole network again, so anything you fixed is broken again. Do not run `bash faults.sh --list`: it prints the answers, and it is there for mentors.

The addresses come from the three ranges kept for examples [S10]. No real computer uses them, and there is no NAT in this lab, so here every address plays a public one.

</GoDeeper>

## Ping and the trace see loss, not where

<MermaidDiagram title="What your two tools can answer" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["ping: 50 small packets to the server"] --> A1["Are some packets lost? Yes"]
  T["traceroute: three probes to each hop"] --> A2["Which routers are on the path? Three hops"]
  Q["Still open"] --> Q1["Where are packets lost?"]
  Q --> Q2["Why does one name fail?"]
  Q --> Q3["Is the app port closed or blocked?"]
  Q --> Q4["How fast is the path?"]
`} legend="Top: the two questions ping and traceroute answer. Bottom: four questions they leave open. Each of the next chapters answers one of them with a new tool." />

Ping counts lost packets but cannot say where they were lost. Traceroute lists the routers, but three probes per hop often miss a loss of one packet in ten. Neither tool tests a name, a port or the speed. Spend ten minutes with them and the commands below, then write one best guess for each complaint.

| # | Where | Type this | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | lab | `ip netns exec laptop ping -c 50 -i 0.2 203.0.113.80` | 50 pings, five each second. Read the `packet loss` line |
| 4 | lab | `ip netns exec laptop traceroute -n 203.0.113.80` | The routers on the way, as numbers |
| 5 | lab | `ip netns exec laptop curl -sS -m 5 http://www.lab.test/` | The web page by name. It works |
| 6 | lab | the same curl with your broken name, then `http://203.0.113.80:PORT/` with your app port | Complaints B and C, tried by hand. Stop after ten minutes |
| 7 | your computer | Fill in **Best guesses** in `faults.md`, and `results.md` | One sentence per complaint. A wrong guess is fine |
| 8 | lab, then your computer | `bash test.sh` in the lab. Then, on your computer, commit `faults.md`, `results.md` and `fault-seed.txt` | Checks your seed, your guesses and your results. The lab has no git, so the commit happens on your computer |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each output proves, and what it does not">

Start with the ping. `50 packets transmitted, 45 received, 10% packet loss` is a real fault: a healthy virtual cable loses nothing, and the service sheet promised that. But the line is a total from one end to the other. A packet can be lost on the way out or on the way back, on the first cable or the second. Ping cannot tell you which. Look also at `ttl=62` in each reply. The server starts at 64, as Linux does [S28], and each router takes one off [S30]. 64 minus 62 is two routers, which matches r1 and r2.

The times say little here. `avg 0.230 ms` is fast, because every cable is virtual. And ping sends tiny packets, 84 bytes each, one at a time. A path that is too slow for a large file still carries one tiny packet quickly. So ping cannot show speed.

Now the trace. It found the three hops you expected: `192.0.2.1`, `198.51.100.2` and `203.0.113.80`. Every line has three times and no star. Does that mean nothing is lost? No. Look at each line: it has three times, one for each of the three probes sent to that hop. With one packet in ten lost, three probes often all get through. The trace gives you the map of the path, not a measure of loss on it.

The two curl lines are clues you can already read. `www.lab.test` works, as the complaint itself says. But `docs.lab.test` fails with `Couldn't connect to server` after about three seconds. The service sheet says both names mean the same server. So is the server refusing that name, or is the name leading somewhere else? curl does not print the address it tried with `-sS`. The app port fails differently: `Connection timed out after 5002 milliseconds`. Nothing answered at all, not even a refusal. In 5.3 you saw that a closed port answers at once with a reset. Silence is a different signal. Ping, traceroute and curl cannot tell you why.

So four questions stay open. Where exactly are packets lost? Why does one name fail while another works? Is the app's port closed, or does something drop the packets on the way? And how fast is the path, really? Write your guesses with these questions in mind. The next six chapters answer them, one tool at a time.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Read this after your guesses: the fault matrix">

Open this only when your three best guesses are in `faults.md`. It tells you which tool finds which fault. It does not tell you your numbers. Those come from your seed, and only your tools can show them.

| Fault | Complaint | Best tool | What the tool shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Copying a file to the server is slow." | `mtr` | Loss that starts at one hop and goes on to the last hop. In iperf3: many retransmits (the `Retr` column) |
| 2 | "One name does not load. www.lab.test works." | `dig` | Two resolvers give two different answers for the same name |
| 3 | "The app on its port does not answer." | `nmap` | The app's port is `filtered`, while another port is `open` and one is `closed` |
| 4 | "Copying a file to the server is slow." | `iperf3` | A low, steady speed with almost no retransmits. mtr is clean |

Faults 1 and 4 make the same complaint on purpose. A file copy is slow in both, and only the evidence tells them apart. Chapters 12.2 and 12.3 come first, because Wireshark and tcpdump let you look at any packet. Then 12.4 to 12.7 each clear one fault, and 12.8 times a page with curl. The gate in 12.10 plants new faults and takes this table away.

</GoDeeper>
