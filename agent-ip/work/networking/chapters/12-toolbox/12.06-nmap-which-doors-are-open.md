Your teammate deployed the app and says: "It listens on its port, I checked." Your app on the laptop tries to connect and waits until it gives up. You both look at the same server. Who is right? Often both are. The program listens, and something on the way drops the packets before they arrive. To see that, you need a tool that knocks on the port from your side and reports exactly what came back.

## The rule comes first

<Callout type="warning" title="Scan only what you own">
Scanning a computer that is not yours can break the rules of your network, your school or your provider, and in many places the law. In this challenge you scan only the lab network, which lives inside your own laptop.
</Callout>

nmap knocks on many ports of a computer, one after the other. To the owner of that computer, a scan can look like the first step of an attack. The nmap project's own page on the law says the question has no simple answer, because it depends on where you are. Its advice is to get written permission first. It also says what happens most often to people who scan without asking: a complaint to their provider, and sometimes a closed account [S133].

So this course has one rule. Scan only computers that you own, or that you have written permission to scan. Graded scans run only in the lab. You may later scan your own home router from your own computer, but only if you run that router yourself. Never scan a campus, hostel, office or café network, even to "just check". Do not scan your home router from the lab either: the lab reaches the outside through Docker's own NAT, so the scan would test Docker, not your router.

Before your first scan, type this line into `results.md` in the part 5 folder, under **Consent**, word for word. The test checks that it is there, and that it comes before your first scan:

```
I scan only computers that I own or that I have written permission to scan. In this challenge I scan only the lab network.
```

**nmap answers one question: what comes back when I knock on each port?** It sends a packet to each port and sorts the ports by the answer. nmap is installed in the lab. On Ubuntu its package is about 6.9 MiB to download with what it needs [S141].

## Three answers, three states

<MermaidDiagram title="Three ports, three answers" chart={`flowchart LR
  L["laptop<br>nmap"] --> R1["r1"] --> R2["r2<br>drops the app's port<br>(fault 3)"]
  R2 -->|"port 80: SYN-ACK back"| A["server 203.0.113.80<br>80 open"]
  R2 -->|"port 81: reset back"| B["server<br>81 closed"]
  R2 -.->|"app port: nothing back"| C["server<br>app port filtered"]
`} legend="nmap sends the first packet of a TCP handshake to each port. Port 80 answers with SYN-ACK, so it is open. Port 81 answers with a reset, so it is closed. The app's port gets nothing back, because r2 drops the packets on the way, so it is filtered." />

nmap knows six states for a port [S134]. Three matter here, and you have seen two of them before.

| State | What nmap got back | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| `open` | A SYN-ACK, the second packet of the handshake from 5.4 | A program listens on that port |
| `closed` | A reset | The computer is there, and nothing listens. A closed port answers with a reset [S81] |
| `filtered` | Nothing at all, or an error from a router | nmap **could not tell** whether a program listens. Often a rule on the way drops the packets |

`closed` is an answer: the computer said no. `filtered` is silence. You met that silence in 12.1: curl to the app's port waited the full five seconds and timed out. A closed port would have answered at once with a reset, as in 5.4.

## Three commands

All of these run **in the lab**, from the part 5 folder. `-n` tells nmap not to look up names, so it is faster and makes no DNS traffic.

**Command one: the default scan.** Without `-p`, nmap tries the 1,000 most common ports [S135].

```
cd /work/challenges/challenge-9-toolbox/part-5-nmap
ip netns exec laptop nmap -n 203.0.113.80
```

```
Starting Nmap 7.94SVN ( https://nmap.org ) at 2026-10-27 16:27 UTC
Nmap scan report for 203.0.113.80
Host is up (0.00059s latency).
Not shown: 999 closed tcp ports (reset)
PORT   STATE SERVICE
80/tcp open  http

Nmap done: 1 IP address (1 host up) scanned in 0.21 seconds
```

One open port, 80. And `Not shown: 999 closed tcp ports (reset)`: every other port in the list answered with a reset. Where is the app? Where is iperf3 on 5201? Neither is among the 1,000 common ports, so nmap never knocked.

**Command two: a wider scan.** `-p 1-10000` knocks on ports 1 to 10000.

```
ip netns exec laptop nmap -n -p 1-10000 203.0.113.80
```

```
Starting Nmap 7.94SVN ( https://nmap.org ) at 2026-10-27 16:27 UTC
Nmap scan report for 203.0.113.80
Host is up (0.00042s latency).
Not shown: 9997 closed tcp ports (reset)
PORT     STATE    SERVICE
80/tcp   open     http
5201/tcp open     targus-getdata1
7630/tcp filtered hawk

Nmap done: 1 IP address (1 host up) scanned in 1.66 seconds
```

Now 5201 is open, and the app's port, 7630 with this seed, is `filtered`. This took 1.66 seconds. While a link loses packets, the same scan slows down and tries again: in the recorded blind round, with loss planted again, it took 16.90 seconds.

**Command three: three ports, three states.** With `-p` and a list, you knock only where you want: the web page, a port where nothing listens, and the app.

```
ip netns exec laptop nmap -n -p 80,81,7630 203.0.113.80
```

```
Starting Nmap 7.94SVN ( https://nmap.org ) at 2026-10-27 16:27 UTC
Nmap scan report for 203.0.113.80
Host is up (0.00019s latency).

PORT     STATE    SERVICE
80/tcp   open     http
81/tcp   closed   hosts2-ns
7630/tcp filtered hawk

Nmap done: 1 IP address (1 host up) scanned in 1.43 seconds
```

Now you can answer your teammate. The scan says nmap got no answer from 7630. It does not say whether the app listens. So look on the server itself. The command `ss -ltn` lists the TCP ports where a program listens, as in 5.3.

```
ip netns exec server ss -ltn
```

```
State  Recv-Q Send-Q Local Address:Port Peer Address:PortProcess
LISTEN 0      9                  *:7630            *:*
LISTEN 0      4096               *:5201            *:*
LISTEN 0      9                  *:80              *:*
```

The app listens on `*:7630`, on every address. So the packets stop somewhere between the laptop and the server. Two routers are on the way. Look at the packet rules in r2, the one next to the server, with `nft list ruleset`:

```
ip netns exec r2 nft list ruleset
```

```
table inet fault3 {
	chain forward {
		type filter hook forward priority filter; policy accept;
		tcp dport 7630 drop
	}
}
```

One rule in a table called `fault3`: TCP to port 7630 is dropped. Dropped means thrown away, with no answer sent back, which is exactly the silence nmap reported. Delete the table, and scan again:

```
ip netns exec r2 nft delete table inet fault3
ip netns exec laptop nmap -n -p 80,81,7630 203.0.113.80
```

```
Starting Nmap 7.94SVN ( https://nmap.org ) at 2026-10-27 16:27 UTC
Nmap scan report for 203.0.113.80
Host is up (0.00023s latency).

PORT     STATE  SERVICE
80/tcp   open   http
81/tcp   closed hosts2-ns
7630/tcp open   hawk

Nmap done: 1 IP address (1 host up) scanned in 0.25 seconds
```

`7630/tcp open`. The app answers.

## How to read an nmap report

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `Host is up` | Something answered, so nmap knows the computer is there |
| `Not shown: 999 closed tcp ports (reset)` | nmap hides long lists of one state. Here 999 ports answered with a reset |
| `80/tcp open http` | The port, the protocol, the state and a service name |
| `SERVICE` | nmap's guess from a list of usual ports. It did not ask the program. Ignore it for ports above 1024: `hawk` on 7630 is only a guess |

Read the state column first, and the service column last, if at all.

## The trap: the scan that did not knock

The most common mistake is in command one. The default scan showed only port 80, and it is tempting to say "the app's port is not there". But nmap only reports the ports it knocked on, and 7630 was not one of them. Absent from the list is not closed and not filtered. It is "not asked". When you check a port, name it with `-p`. `-p-` scans all 65,535 ports, and `-F` scans only the 100 most common ones [S135].

The second trap is the word `filtered` itself. It does not mean "closed", and it does not mean "a firewall, for sure". It means nmap got no useful answer. A drop rule is one cause. A router on the way that cannot deliver the packet, and drops it or sends back an error, also leaves nmap without a reset. So treat `filtered` as a question, and look for the cause on the computers along the way, as you just did.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what nmap sends, and the other three states">

The scan you ran sends the first packet of a TCP handshake, a SYN, to each port, as the laptop does when an app connects (5.4). An open port answers with a SYN-ACK. A closed port answers with a reset: the TCP standard says a computer with no connection for that port answers any arriving packet with a reset, except another reset [S81]. Nothing comes back from a port whose packets are dropped on the way, so nmap waits, tries again, and then calls the port filtered. That waiting is why a scan of many filtered ports is slow, and why loss on a link slows the same wide scan from under two seconds to many seconds: 16.90 in the recorded blind round, where fault 1 was planted again.

Look at the time of the three-port scan before and after the fix: 1.43 seconds with the drop rule, 0.25 seconds without it. Almost all of that second was nmap waiting for 7630.

nmap's manual lists three more states: `unfiltered`, `open|filtered` and `closed|filtered` [S134]. They appear with other kinds of scan, when nmap can rule out some answers but not others. You will not meet them in this challenge. If you do meet one later, read it the same way: nmap is telling you exactly how sure it is.

The `ss -ltn` check matters just as much as the scan. nmap looks from outside, and `ss` looks from inside. When the two disagree, the problem is between them. When both agree that a port is closed, the program is not running, or it listens on another port or another address, as in 5.3.

</GoDeeper>
