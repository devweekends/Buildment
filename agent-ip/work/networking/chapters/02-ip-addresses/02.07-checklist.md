## What your explanation must cover

Your friend has never looked at a network setting. They know that their phone has an address, and that is all. Four things make an explanation they can follow.

The first is the shortage. Say what "ran out" means and give one date from chapter 2.1. Take care with the words. The internet did not stop working. Say what is empty, and what that changes for a provider that asks for new addresses today. A friend who hears "the internet ran out of addresses" will believe something false.

The second is the ranges. Your phone's address starts with `192.168` or `10`, or less often with `172` and a number from 16 to 31. Say what is special about these ranges, why many networks can use the same numbers, and what such an address cannot do. Take care with one word. Private does not mean secret or safe. It means that the address works only inside its own network.

The third is the question. A phone with a private address still loads websites. In chapter 2.4 you stepped through one request and saw what the router did to it. Name the question that a router sharing one outside address has to answer when a reply comes back. You are not asked to say how it answers. Module 6 builds that answer. You only need to ask the question well.

The fourth is clarity. Write for your friend, not for a mentor. Use no word that you have not explained in the same sentence. Use one real number: your own phone's address. About 250 words are enough, or about three minutes if you speak. A voice note is as good as text.

## A worked example, for a different address

Here is the same shape of explanation for a different question: why a program on your laptop can be reached at `127.0.0.1` from your own laptop, but not from your friend's. It uses a different range from the one you will write about, so it shows the shape without giving away your answer.

A weak answer says: "127.0.0.1 is localhost, it is just for testing." It is not wrong, but it explains nothing. Your friend cannot repeat it, and cannot check it.

A strong answer says: "The block 127.0.0.0/8 is a special row in the IANA registry, and the registry marks it as not forwardable. Every computer treats 127.0.0.1 as itself, so a packet sent there never leaves the machine. A program that listens on 127.0.0.1 answers you. It refuses your friend, because on your friend's laptop 127.0.0.1 means your friend's laptop." This answer names the range, gives the reason, and ends with a consequence your friend can test.

Look at what the strong answer does. It states a fact that can be checked, in this case the registry row. It says why that fact leads to the behaviour. And it finishes with what the friend will see. Your answer has the same three moves, for a different range: the fact about the range, the reason it is allowed, and what it cannot do. The weak answer has none of them.

## If your result was different

Two results on a phone change the answer a little. Neither one is a mistake, and neither needs a new idea.

If your phone is on mobile data and not on Wi-Fi, its address may not start with `192.168` or `10`. It may start with `100`, or be a long address with colons. Connect to Wi-Fi for this gate if you can, and use that address. If you cannot, use the address you have and say which kind it is. An address from `100.64` to `100.127` is shared space, which you met in chapter 2.4.

If your phone shows two addresses, one short and one long with colons, write the short one. The long one is an IPv6 address, which module 9 explains. The short one is the one this gate is about.
