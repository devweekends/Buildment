AS64500 and AS64501 are joined by a direct cable. AS64501 also has a cable to AS64502, where a server lives. Yet traffic from AS64500 to that server never crosses the direct cable. It takes another road, through a fourth network. Nothing is broken. The reason is in who pays whom, and in the rules each network writes for itself.

## A customer pays its provider

<MermaidDiagram title="A provider and its customers" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["Provider AS64503"] -->|"carries traffic to and from<br>the rest of the internet"| C1["Customer AS64500"]
  P --> C2["Customer AS64510<br>a university"]
  C1 -.->|"pays"| P
  C2 -.->|"pays"| P
`} legend="Made-up networks. Each customer pays its provider, and in return the provider carries the customer's traffic to the rest of the internet and back." />

The most common link is a sale. A <GlossaryTerm term="customer" definition="A network that pays another one for reach." /> pays its <GlossaryTerm term="provider" definition="The network that is paid to carry a customer's traffic." />, and the provider carries its traffic to and from the rest of the internet [S145]. Carrying traffic between other networks like this is called transit.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where this model comes from">

The customer, provider and peer model in this chapter comes from a paper by Lixin Gao and Jennifer Rexford, published in December 2001. They describe the money (a customer pays its provider) and the routing rules that follow from it, and they write that the rules match conventional practice of internet providers [S145]. This chapter uses that paper for the rules, and RFC 9234 for the names of the roles [S92].

Your home network is a customer of your provider, and so is a company with one link to the internet. Your provider, in turn, may be the customer of a bigger network. So the links form layers: small networks at the bottom pay the networks above them. A network can also be a customer of two providers at once, which is the multihomed case from 10.2 that needs its own AS number [S68].

RFC 9234, from May 2022, gives these roles official names that two BGP routers can tell each other: Provider, Customer, Peer, Route Server and Route Server Client [S92]. A route server is a helper at an exchange point, which 10.5 shows.

</GoDeeper>

## Peers swap their customers' traffic free

<MermaidDiagram title="Two providers peer" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["AS64501"] <-->|"peer link: no money"| B["AS64504"]
  A --> CA["AS64501's customers"]
  B --> CB["AS64504's customers"]
`} legend="Two made-up networks of similar size connect directly. Each one carries traffic between its own customers and the other's customers, and neither pays." />

Two networks of similar size often connect directly as <GlossaryTerm term="peers" definition="Two networks that swap traffic between their own customers, free of charge." />. Each carries traffic between its own customers and the other's customers, free of charge [S145]. Both save money: that traffic no longer goes through a provider they would have to pay.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: peers, and the label tier 1">

A peer link only covers the two networks and their customers. It is not a door to everything else each network knows. The next beat shows that rule.

You will read about "tier 1" networks: very large networks that reach the whole internet only through peer links, and pay nobody. William Norton calls it "an overloaded and misused term", and "Tier 1 Club" is community slang [S108]. No authority defines the tiers, and public data cannot prove that a given network is one. So this course uses "tier 1" only as an informal label and never names a network as tier 1.

"Peer" in BGP itself just means a neighbour you have a session with [S83]. This chapter means the business link, and says "neighbour" for the other meaning.

</GoDeeper>

## Routes from providers and peers go only down

<MermaidDiagram title="Which routes a network passes on" chart={`flowchart TD
  N["AS64501"] -->|"own routes and customer routes:<br>to everyone"| U["its provider and its peers"]
  N -->|"every route it knows"| D["its customers"]
  U -.->|"routes learned here go<br>only to customers"| N
`} legend="A made-up network and its neighbours. Its own blocks and its customers' blocks go to every neighbour. Routes it learned from a provider or a peer go down to its customers only." />

A network sends its own blocks and its customers' blocks to everyone. But routes it learned from a provider or a peer, it passes only to its customers [S145, S92]. Why? Passing them to a peer would mean carrying the peer's traffic to a provider: work nobody pays for.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the export rules, written out">

The rules fit in a table [S145, S92]:

| Learned from | Passed to a customer | Passed to a peer | Passed to a provider |
|---|---|---|---|
| Its own blocks | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| A customer | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| A peer | Yes | No | No |
| A provider | Yes | No | No |

Read the bottom two rows. A network never gives a peer or a provider a way to reach its other peers and providers. If it did, it would carry traffic between two networks that do not pay it, over links it pays for or that are meant only for customers.

RFC 9234 adds a mark called OTC to routes, so that a route which has gone down to a customer or sideways to a peer is not sent up or sideways again [S92]. When a network breaks these rules by mistake, the route goes further than intended. That is a route leak, and 10.9 shows a real one.

</GoDeeper>

## The direct cable carries no third network

<MermaidDiagram title="Map 1: why traffic goes the long way" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["AS64502<br>the server lives here"] -->|"provider of"| B["AS64501"]
  S -->|"peer of"| T["AS64503"]
  T -->|"provider of"| A["AS64500<br>sends the traffic"]
  A ---|"direct cable: peer link"| B
`} legend="Made-up networks. AS64500 has a direct peer cable to AS64501, and AS64501 is a customer of AS64502. But the route that AS64500 receives comes through its own provider, AS64503." />

AS64501 never tells its peer AS64500 about its provider's server, so the direct cable is not a way there. AS64500 learns the server's block from its own provider, AS64503, and sends the traffic that way. The cable is fine. It carries only traffic between the two peers' own customers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the same map from AS64501's side">

Why should AS64501 not carry the traffic? Follow the money. AS64501 pays AS64502, its provider. If it let AS64500 reach AS64502 through it, AS64501 would pay its provider to carry traffic for a peer that pays it nothing [S145].

There is a second reason a direct cable can be skipped, even for traffic it could carry. A network may prefer another route on purpose, for example one through a paying customer. The next two beats show how a network writes that choice down.

If you ever see a trace that seems to go the long way, as in 4.1, these two reasons are the first to think of. The path is a result of business links and of rules each network writes for itself, not only of the cables.

</GoDeeper>

## Your own value per neighbour decides first

<MermaidDiagram title="Local preference: a network's own setting" chart={`flowchart LR
  subgraph N["Inside AS64500"]
    LP["local preference per neighbour<br>customer AS64510: 120<br>peer AS64501: 100<br>provider AS64503: 100"]
  end
  LP -->|"higher wins, checked before<br>AS path length"| C["the chosen route"]
`} legend="A made-up network gives each neighbour a value of its own choice. This is not an AS number. The route with the higher value wins before path length is compared. The values never leave the network." />

Each network sets its own value per neighbour, called <GlossaryTerm term="local preference" definition="A network's own value for the routes from one neighbour. Higher wins." />. It is not an AS number. Higher wins, and it counts before AS path length [S83, S145]. The value stays inside the network. A network never prefers a route through a peer or a provider over one through a customer [S145].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: policy first, then the shortest path">

RFC 4271 compares routes to the same block in an order. First comes a degree of preference that the network sets by its own policy, most often local preference. Only when that ties does the shortest AS path win, and after that come more tie-breakers [S83]. So "the shortest path wins" alone is wrong: policy comes first.

Local preference is local in a strict sense. It is shared between the routers of one network, and it is never sent to another network [S83]. In 10.7 you will see it in the lab as `BGP.local_pref: 100`, the usual starting value. r1 did not receive that 100. It gave it to the route itself.

The rule from the paper is about customers. A network should never prefer a route through a peer or a provider over a route through a customer [S145]. That makes sense with the money: traffic to a customer earns money, and traffic to a provider costs money. The paper does not require a network to prefer a peer over a provider. That choice is each network's own, so the picture gives both the same number.

In 10.6 you write this choice as code: `bestRoute` takes a `policy` object that maps each neighbour to its local preference. In 10.8 you set it in the lab with one line.

</GoDeeper>

## A longer customer path beats a short one

<MermaidDiagram title="Map 2: AS64500 chooses between two ways" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["AS64500"] -->|"via peer AS64501<br>AS path: 64501 64509<br>local preference 100"| D["block of AS64509"]
  A -->|"via customer AS64510<br>AS path: 64510 64511 64509<br>local preference 120"| D
`} legend="Made-up networks. AS64500 knows two ways to the same block. The way through its customer has a longer AS path but a higher local preference." />

AS64500 knows two ways to the block of AS64509. Through its peer, the path has two numbers. Through its customer, it has three. Local preference counts first: 120 beats 100, so the customer path wins, and the shorter path never gets compared [S83, S145]. That is the second reason traffic goes the long way.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what changes the choice">

Change one thing at a time and the choice follows the order of the rules.

- If both neighbours had local preference 100, the tie would go to the shorter AS path: the two-number path through the peer.
- If the peer's route covered a longer, more specific block, for example a `/25` inside the customer's `/24`, the router would forward by longest prefix match first, as in 4.3. BGP picks the best route for each block, and the packet then uses the most specific block that holds its address [S83, S30].
- If the customer withdrew its route, the peer's route would be the only one left, and it would win.

The third case is a backup: the route that loses on preference still waits in the table and takes over when the winner disappears. In 10.8 you build exactly that in the lab, and watch traffic move when you cut a link.

</GoDeeper>
