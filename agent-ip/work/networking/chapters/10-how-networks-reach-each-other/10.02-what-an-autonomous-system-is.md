In 4.5 you met three made-up networks, A, B and C, that announced blocks to each other. Real networks have numbers, not letters. In 10.1 you looked up the number of your own provider's network. What does that number stand for, and who gave it out?

## One network, one routing policy

<MermaidDiagram title="An autonomous system from the outside" chart={`flowchart LR

    B1["block 198.51.100.0/24"]
    B2["block 203.0.113.0/25"]
    R["its routers follow<br>one routing policy"]
  end
  AS -->|"tells its neighbours:<br>I can reach these blocks"| N["Neighbour networks"]
`} legend="A made-up network with two blocks. From the outside, the whole group of blocks and routers acts as one thing: one number, one set of rules for routing." />

An <GlossaryTerm term="autonomous system" definition="A connected group of address blocks run by one or more operators, with one clear routing policy." /> is a connected group of address blocks with one clearly defined routing policy [S68]. From outside, it looks like one network with one number, its AS number. Your provider is one. So is a university or a cloud company.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two definitions, and who needs a number">

RFC 1930, from March 1996, gives the definition used in this course: "a connected group of one or more IP prefixes run by one or more network operators" with a single and clearly defined routing policy [S68]. A prefix is a block of addresses, written with a `/n`, as in module 2.

RFC 4271, the BGP standard from January 2006, words it from the inside. An AS is a set of routers under one technical administration. Inside, they use one routing protocol and the same measures of cost. Towards other networks, they use a protocol between networks, which is BGP [S83]. The two definitions agree on what matters here: one network, one set of rules, seen from outside as one thing.

Not every organisation has a number. RFC 1930 says a site with one provider and one block does not need one: its block sits inside the provider's AS. A site with two or more providers, called multihomed, does need its own [S68]. So most homes, most small offices and most learners are inside their provider's AS and have no AS number of their own. That is why the lookup in 10.1 named your provider and not you.

</GoDeeper>

## Numbers grew from 16 to 32 bits

<MermaidDiagram title="Two sizes of AS number" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["16-bit numbers<br>0 to 65,535"] -->|"RFC 6793, December 2012"| B["32-bit numbers<br>0 to 4,294,967,295"]
`} legend="The first BGP standard had room for 16 bits. A later standard widened the field to 32 bits, the same size as an IPv4 address." />

An AS number started as a 16-bit number, so the biggest was 65,535. Like IPv4 addresses in 2.1, that was not enough. In December 2012 RFC 6793 widened it to 32 bits, up to 4,294,967,295 [S84]. Small numbers still work, so your provider may have either size.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how old and new routers talk">

The base BGP specification kept the AS number in a field of two bytes, so 16 bits [S84]. Module 2 showed that 16 bits give 2 to the power 16 = 65,536 values, from 0 to 65,535. RFC 6793 widens the pool to four bytes, 0 to 4,294,967,295: the same 32-bit size as an IPv4 address in 2.2 [S84].

RFC 6793 also plans for old routers that know only 16-bit numbers. When a new router talks to an old one and must show a 32-bit number that does not fit, it writes a special stand-in number, 23456, called AS_TRANS [S84]. So if you ever see 23456 in a path, it is not a real network. It means "a 32-bit number was here".

You do not have to do anything about the two sizes when you look a number up.

</GoDeeper>

## Some numbers never appear on the internet

<MermaidDiagram title="Special ranges of AS numbers" chart={`flowchart TD
  D1["64496 to 64511<br>documentation"] --> P1["64512 to 65534<br>private"]
  P1 --> R1["65535<br>reserved"]
  R1 --> D2["65536 to 65551<br>documentation"]
  D2 --> P2["4,200,000,000 to 4,294,967,294<br>private"]
  P2 --> R2["4,294,967,295<br>reserved"]
`} legend="From small to big. Two ranges are kept for examples, two are private, and a few single numbers are reserved. Every other number is given to real networks by the registries." />

Like addresses in module 2, AS numbers have special ranges. Two are kept for examples, so a book never names a real network. Two are private: a network may use them inside, but they must never reach the public internet. The lab uses the example range 64496 to 64511 [S86, S85].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: private, documentation and reserved">

RFC 6996, from July 2013, sets aside two private ranges: 64512 to 65534, and 4,200,000,000 to 4,294,967,294. Private numbers are not unique: many networks can use the same ones. So they must be removed from the AS path before a route goes to the global internet [S85]. They work like `192.168.0.0/16` for addresses: fine inside, never outside.

RFC 5398, from December 2008, keeps two ranges of 16 numbers each for documentation: 64496 to 64511 and 65536 to 65551 [S86]. They are the AS-number twins of the address ranges `192.0.2.0/24`, `198.51.100.0/24` and `203.0.113.0/24` from 2.4. This course uses only these for its made-up networks.

The IANA registry lists both kinds, and a few reserved single numbers as well: 0, 65535 and 4,294,967,295 [S87]. The trap is to think that everything from 64496 up is private. It is not. 64496 to 64511 is documentation, the private range starts at 64512, and 65536 to 65551 is documentation again.

</GoDeeper>

## IANA, then five registries, then networks

<MermaidDiagram title="Who hands out AS numbers" chart={`flowchart TD
  I["IANA"] --> A1["AFRINIC"]
  I --> A2["APNIC"]
  I --> A3["ARIN"]
  I --> A4["LACNIC"]
  I --> A5["RIPE NCC"]
  A5 --> N["A network in its region<br>for example AS3333"]
`} legend="IANA gives blocks of AS numbers to the five regional registries. Each registry assigns numbers to networks in its part of the world. Only one registry's branch is drawn to the bottom." />

IANA gives blocks of AS numbers to five regional registries, and each registry assigns numbers to networks in its region [S87, S88]. They are the same registries whose address pools you met in 2.1. AS3333 is a real example: RIPE NCC's own network, from a block that RIPE NCC hands out [S94].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the five registries and one real number">

The five regional internet registries are AFRINIC, APNIC, ARIN, LACNIC and RIPE NCC. The NRO, set up in 2003 to coordinate them, lists the region of each [S88]. The IANA registry of AS numbers, last updated on 2026-06-01 at the time of writing, shows which block went to which registry. It keeps 16-bit and 32-bit numbers in two separate lists [S87].

AS3333 is a safe number to practise with. It is held by RIPE NCC itself, and it sits in the block 3154 to 3353, which the IANA list marks "assigned by RIPE NCC" [S94]. You looked it up in 10.1: it announces `193.0.0.0/21`.

The same chain works for addresses, as module 2 showed: IANA gives big blocks to the registries, and the registries hand them to providers. A network gets its number from the registry of its region, which assigns numbers to operators [S87].

</GoDeeper>

## About 80,000 numbers in one table

<MermaidDiagram title="AS numbers in one BGP table view, 27 October 2026" chart={`flowchart TD
  T["80,111 AS numbers seen"] --> O["67,416 only at the end of paths"]
  T --> M["11,974 at the end of some paths<br>and in the middle of others"]
  T --> X["721 only in the middle of paths"]
`} legend="One public view of the BGP table, counted on 27 October 2026. A BGP table is the list of routes one router has learned from its neighbours. An AS path lists the networks a route crossed. The last number is the network that announced the block." />

One public view of the BGP table held 80,111 different AS numbers on 27 October 2026. Of those, 67,416 appeared only at the end of AS paths: they announced their own blocks, and no route in that view passed through them [S144]. The last number of a path is the network that announced the block. Chapter 10.3 shows how a path grows.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the counts say, and what they do not">

The counts come from a public report that reads one BGP table. This copy was updated on 27 October 2026 at 14:00 [S144]. It splits the AS numbers by where they appear in the AS paths of that table:

| Where in the paths | AS numbers |
|---|---|
| Only at the end | 67,416 |
| At the end of some, in the middle of others | 11,974 |
| Only in the middle | 721 |
| All | 80,111 |

The first row adds up with the others: 67,416 + 11,974 + 721 = 80,111. Read the first row with care. It does not say that 67,416 networks carry nobody's traffic. It says only that, in this one view, no path showed them in the middle. Another view of the table, from another place, can see other paths. Still, the picture is clear: most networks are at the edge, and a small number pass routes on for others.

So "the internet is about 80,000 networks" is a fair rough number for October 2026. Your trace in 4.1 crossed only a handful of them.

</GoDeeper>

## Ten words for the whole module

<MermaidDiagram title="The module's words on one announcement" chart={`flowchart LR
  O["AS64498 owns a block<br>prefix 203.0.113.0/24"] -->|"announce, AS path 64498"| P["AS64497<br>its BGP neighbour"]
  P -->|"AS path 64497 64498"| C["AS64496"]
  O -.->|"withdraw: I lost it"| P
`} legend="One made-up announcement from AS64498. It carries a block and an AS path, it travels between BGP neighbours, and it can be taken back with a withdrawal." />

Keep this table open for the rest of the module. Every word is on the picture or comes back in a later chapter: transit in 10.4, a leak and a hijack in 10.9. In 10.4, "peer" also gets a second, business meaning.

| Word | One plain sentence |
|---|---|
| Autonomous system (AS) | A network with one routing policy, run by one or more operators |
| AS number | The number that names an AS, such as 64496 |
| Prefix, block | A range of addresses, written with a `/n`, such as `203.0.113.0/24` |
| Announce | Tell your neighbours: I can reach this block, send its traffic to me |
| Withdraw | Tell your neighbours: I can no longer reach this block, forget my route |
| AS path | The list of networks a route crossed, the newest first and the origin last |
| BGP neighbour | A network that you talk BGP with directly. BGP's own word for it is peer |
| Transit | Carrying traffic between two other networks, usually for a customer who pays |
| Leak | A route passed on further than it was meant to go |
| Hijack | A network announces a block that is not its own, and traffic for it goes to the wrong place |

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two words that mean two things">

"Peer" has two uses, and this module keeps them apart. In BGP itself, a peer is any router you have a session with: your BGP neighbour [S83]. In 10.4, "peer" also names a kind of business link between two networks that swap their customers' traffic free of charge, as opposed to a customer who pays a provider [S145]. When this module means the first, it says "neighbour".

"Leak" and "hijack" are both wrong routes, but in different ways. In a hijack the wrong network is the origin, the last number of the path. In a leak the origin can be right, and the route has gone to places it should not reach [S91]. Chapter 10.9 shows one real case of each.

</GoDeeper>
