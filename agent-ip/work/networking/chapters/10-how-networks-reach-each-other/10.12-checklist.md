This is the gate for module 10. In 4.1 you ran a trace and could only say "private, public or unknown" for each hop. Now you can say much more. Your evidence is a map of your own path, network by network: which networks your packet crossed, where it crossed from one to the next, and how those networks know the way. You have met every piece in the last eleven chapters, so nothing here is new. The gate asks you to put the pieces together for one real path: yours.

A mentor reads your answers within three days. You do not have to wait. You can carry on to module 11 as soon as you submit, and if the mentor has a question, it reaches you while you work. You answer it by editing and sending your answer again. Your tests from 10.6 and your challenge 7 results are part of the evidence too, so check that they pass and are committed before you submit. Your answers save as you type, so you can stop after a power cut and come back.

You may answer in writing, as a voice note, or with a labelled diagram. A photo of a drawing on paper is fine. Add a link if you record or draw. What matters is that each claim can be checked, not how it is delivered.

## What your map must show

Start from your trace in 4.1, or your notes from 10.1. For each public hop, write its address, the AS number and the name the lookup gave you. Mark the hops where the AS number changes: that is where your packet crossed from one network into the next, give or take one hop. Remember the care note from 10.1. A hop's address can come from the neighbour network that numbered the cable, or from an exchange point's own block, so a lookup is a good guess about the router, not proof. Say so in your map where you are not sure, with a question mark.

Then pick two of the networks on your path and look both up on PeeringDB, as in 10.5. Do they list a port at the same exchange point? If yes, name it and its city. If no, say so: that is a fine answer, and it tells you they probably meet somewhere else, or through a third network. You cannot see from a trace alone whether two networks are customer and provider or peers. You can only say which is more likely and why.

Last, link the map to BGP. Your provider's router knew where to send your packet because the network at the far end announced its block, and every network on the way passed the announcement on and added its number to the AS path. In two or three sentences, say how the route to the website's block reached your provider.

## If you have no usable trace

On a phone, on a mobile hotspot or behind carrier-grade NAT, your trace may show only stars or only private hops. That is normal and not a mistake. Use the sample trace in `challenges/challenge-7-bgp/README.md` in your repository, and do the same work on it. Use the real address `193.0.0.1` from 10.1 for at least one lookup of your own: it is in `193.0.0.0/21`, announced by AS3333, RIPE NCC's network, and PeeringDB lists AS3333 at AMS-IX and NL-ix. Say in your answer which trace you used.

## A worked example, for a made-up path

Here is the shape of a good answer for a path that is not yours. It uses documentation numbers, so it shows the shape without giving away your answer.

A weak answer says: "My trace went through a few networks and then reached the website." It is not wrong, but nobody can check it, and it says nothing about how the networks knew the way.

A strong answer says: "Hops 1 and 2 are private, my home router and the provider's router. Hops 3 to 5 look up as AS64500, my provider. Hop 6 looks up as AS64501 and hop 7 as AS64502, the website's network, so my packet crossed two network borders, near hop 6 and near hop 7. Hop 6 could also be a cable that AS64500 numbered, so I mark it with a question mark. On PeeringDB, AS64500 and AS64502 both list the same exchange point, so they may peer there, but my trace went through AS64501, so on this path they did not. The route reached my provider because AS64502 announced its block to AS64501, which added its number and passed it to AS64500."

The strong answer names each network with a number that can be checked, marks the one hop it is unsure of and says why, uses PeeringDB to make a claim and then tests it against the trace, and ends with the announcement that built the route. Your answer has the same moves, for your own path.
