This is the gate for module 7. Your evidence is a runbook for your family's Wi-Fi. One day someone at home will ask you why the Wi-Fi is down. A runbook is what you would do, written down so that someone else could do it too. It is the method of this module, written for another person: check one layer at a time, from the bottom up, and let the first failing check name the problem. You have met every piece in the last seven chapters, so nothing here is new. The gate asks you to put the pieces together, in your own words and for your own devices. Your answer saves as you type, so you can stop after a power cut and come back.

A mentor reads your runbook within three days. You do not have to wait. You can carry on to module 8 as soon as you submit, and if the mentor has a question, it reaches you while you work. You answer it by editing and sending your runbook again. Your challenge 5 results are part of the evidence as well, so check that they are committed before you submit.

## What your runbook must cover

Your runbook has six layers, in this order: the link, the address, the gateway, the route out, the name and the web request. These are the six checks of lab 7.5. For each layer, write four things. The first is one command, for the operating system you really use. The second is what a good output looks like. The third is what a bad output looks like. The fourth is what the bad output means: which part is broken and who can fix it. If a layer has no command, it is only a wish. If it has no bad output, nobody can tell when to stop.

Take care with three layers, because they are easy to blur. The gateway check uses a ping to the router only. It does not use a ping to the internet. The route check has two parts, the route your computer knows and a ping to a public address, and a silent ping does not name a layer by itself. The name check must separate a time-out from a refusal, because a time-out is silence and a refusal is an answer. Say these three things in your own sentences.

Use one real number from your own network. At least one command must be run on your real home network, and you must label it "On your computer" in the runbook. The commands in the lab run in the lab, and the lab is not your home. Run the address and the DNS checks on your own computer, and write down what they print. Keep the numbers private: your mentor sees them, and nobody else does.

## A worked example, for a different home

Here is a filled runbook for a fictional home: the network `192.168.1.0/24`, the router `192.168.1.1`, and a stand-in public address `203.0.113.45`. On a real network use a real public address, because the example ranges are not reachable on the internet [S10]. The commands for macOS have not yet been checked on a Mac for this course. If a Mac screen looks different, trust your own screen.

| Layer | Windows | Linux | Good | Bad, and what it means |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Link | The Wi-Fi status in Settings | `ip link` | The status says connected, or the line shows `state UP` | Not connected or not UP: the link is broken, so fix the Wi-Fi first |
| 2. Address | `ipconfig /all` | `ip addr` | An IPv4 address such as `192.168.1.23` | No address, or a `169.254.x.x` address on Windows and macOS [S12]: the lease failed, so look at the DHCP server |
| 3. Gateway | `ping 192.168.1.1` | `ping -c2 -W1 192.168.1.1` | Every ping gets an answer | No answer at all: wrong gateway address or router off |
| 4. Route out | `tracert -d 203.0.113.45` | `ip route get 203.0.113.45`, then `ping -c2 -W1 203.0.113.45` | A route through the gateway, and answers | Network is unreachable, or Destination Net Unreachable: no route out. Silence: read on |
| 5. Name | `nslookup shop.example.com` | `dig +short shop.example.com` | An address comes back | A time-out: the DNS server is silent. A refusal: it answered, and will not look the name up |
| 6. Web | `curl.exe -sS -m 5 http://shop.example.com/` | `curl -sS -m 5 http://shop.example.com/` | The page | A login page: a captive portal. A refusal at once: the server. A time-out: silence on the way |

Read the table as a decision list, not as six separate checks. The first row that has a bad output is the one to fix. Everything below it passed.

Now compare a weak runbook with a strong one. A weak runbook says: "Check the router. Restart it. Call the provider." It is not wrong, but nobody can repeat it, and nobody can check it. It has no command and no expected output. A strong runbook says: "Run ping on the router address. If every ping gets an answer, the link, the address and the gateway are fine, so go to the route. If there is no answer at all, the address of the gateway may be wrong or the router may be off, so look at the router before you look at anything else." This one names a command, a good output, a bad output and a meaning. Yours must do the same for all six layers.

## If your result was different

Three results change the runbook a little. None is a mistake, and none needs a new idea.

If you use a phone only, you cannot type these commands. Write the runbook for a laptop in your family, or use the Wi-Fi details screen of your phone for the link, the address, the router and the DNS server. Then describe what you would check on a laptop. A labelled diagram or a voice note is accepted, and so is a link to one.

If your home is behind a provider that gives you a shared address, the address that websites see for your home may start with `100`, as in 2.4. That does not change the runbook.

If a VPN or a captive portal is in the way, say so at the layer where you noticed it. A VPN can change the route and the name that you see. A portal shows itself at the last layer.
