On 4 October 2021 Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp could not be found by anyone on the internet. What went missing?

## Facebook could not be found

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m7-scene-facebook-missing" />

On 4 October 2021 a maintenance command cut the links between Facebook's data centres. Its DNS servers could not reach them, so they withdrew their route announcements. The servers still ran, but no route led to them. So nobody could look up the names [S48].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what happened, step by step">

The company's own account gives the order of events [S48]. A maintenance command was meant to assess the capacity of the backbone, the network between the data centres. A bug in the audit tool let the command run. It took down all the backbone connections. Without them, the DNS servers could not reach the data centres, so they withdrew their route announcements.

A route announcement is a message in which a network tells its neighbours which addresses it can reach. When the announcements stopped, the rest of the internet no longer had a route to the DNS servers. They were running, and nobody could find them. The engineers also needed to be on site, because the same failure had cut their remote access. The account gives no total duration, so this chapter gives none.

Chapter 4.5 shows the idea of route announcements. This course does not teach how they work in detail. They are here for one lesson only: DNS depends on routing.

## A cached answer has an expiry date

<MermaidDiagram title="A copy lives for its TTL" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Authoritative server"] -->|"answer 203.0.113.45, TTL 3600"| R["Resolver"]
  R --> C["Copy kept for 3600 s"]
  C -->|"copy still fresh"| L["Laptop gets the copy"]
  C -->|"time is up"| Q["Resolver asks the server again"]
`} legend="The server sends the answer with a TTL. The resolver keeps a copy until the TTL runs out, and answers from the copy. After that it asks again." />

A <GlossaryTerm term="cache" definition="A store of copies, kept to answer faster." /> keeps copies of answers. Each answer comes with a <GlossaryTerm term="DNS TTL" definition="The seconds a copy of a DNS answer may be kept." />. The resolver answers from its copy until the TTL runs out, and then it asks again. A TTL of zero means: do not keep a copy [S24].

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: TTL in numbers">

The TTL is a number of seconds [S24]. These are the sizes this chapter uses.

| TTL in seconds | In words |
|---|---|
| 0 | Do not cache |
| 60 | 1 minute |
| 300 | 5 minutes |
| 3600 | 1 hour |
| 86400 | 1 day |

The server picks the TTL, and the resolver keeps the copy for that long. A short TTL makes changes spread fast, but it makes resolvers ask the server more often. A long TTL saves questions, but it keeps an old answer longer. The owner of a name chooses between the two.

A copy has its own clock. The TTL belongs to the copy: it counts from the moment the resolver made the copy. If the owner later sets a shorter TTL, copies that exist already do not become shorter. They keep the time they were given. The next two beats show why this matters.

</GoDeeper>

## One resolver keeps the old answer

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m7-ttl-one-resolver" />

The record changed at 10:00, but this resolver copied the old answer at 09:58. Its copy lives one hour, so laptops behind it get the old address until 10:58. The change worked. A cache holds the old answer. Drag the two times and watch the wait grow and shrink.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the change did work">

<Callout type="tip" title="The change did work">
When people say "the DNS change did not work", often it did. The server already gives the new address. A cache somewhere still holds the old answer, and it will until the copy's TTL runs out.
</Callout>

How can you tell? Ask the server that owns the name, and then ask a resolver. If the server gives the new address and the resolver gives the old one, a cache is holding the old answer. In the lab, `dig @192.168.1.1 web.test` asks that one server and nobody else. Chapter 7.6 builds it.

The slider has two inputs because two moments matter. The first is when the record changes. The second is when the resolver made its copy. The old address is served only if the copy was made before the change and has not run out yet. Try three cases. If the copy was made after the change, it holds the new address from the start. If the copy ran out before the change, the next question gets the new address. Only a copy made before the change and still alive keeps the old address, and the longest wait is the whole TTL, one hour here.

</GoDeeper>

## Short and long TTLs spread differently

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m7-ttl-three-resolvers" />

Three resolvers copied the old answer, with TTLs of 60 seconds, 1 hour and 1 day. After the change at 10:00 they switch to the new address at very different times. A longer TTL keeps the old address longer. Lowering a TTL helps only if you do it at least one old TTL before the change.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to plan a change">

A record has a TTL of 3600 seconds. At 10:00 the owner lowers it to 60. At 10:05 the owner changes the address. A resolver that copied the old record at 09:55 holds a copy with the old TTL of 3600. That copy lives until 10:55. The new TTL of 60 only reaches resolvers that ask again after 10:00.

So the careful order is this. Lower the TTL first. Wait at least one old TTL, here an hour, so that every old copy has run out and the new, shorter copies are in place. Then change the address. After the change has spread, raise the TTL again if you want fewer questions.

The picture shows the other side of the trade-off. A resolver with a TTL of 60 seconds switches almost at once. One with a TTL of one day can keep the old address for nearly a day. The slider has no resolver that ignores TTLs, and no negative caching. A real network has more cases, and the rule in this chapter is the one the standard gives: a copy is kept for its TTL at most [S24].

</GoDeeper>

## A name needs a route to its servers

<MermaidDiagram title="Names depend on routes" chart={`flowchart LR
  L["Laptop"] -->|"1. asks"| R["Resolver"]
  R -->|"2. needs a route to"| RT["Route to the DNS servers"]
  RT -->|"3. leads to"| S["Authoritative servers"]
  RT -.->|"announcements withdrawn: the route is gone"| X["Names cannot be looked up"]
`} legend="The servers can be healthy and still unreachable. If the announcements that tell the world how to reach them disappear, no resolver can ask them." />

A name is only as reachable as the route to the servers that answer for it. In 2021 the DNS servers were running, but their route announcements were gone, so the world could not find them [S48]. Names need addresses, and addresses need routes (4.5).

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a second outage, with an empty record">

A second outage shows a different failure. From late on 19 October 2025 until 20 October, parts of Amazon Web Services in one region (us-east-1) failed. A latent race condition in the DNS management of its DynamoDB service left an incorrect, empty DNS record for `dynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com` [S49]. The database itself was running. An empty record means: the name exists and has no addresses. Programs that asked for the name got nothing back and could not connect. Other services that depended on it failed in turn: the account lists EC2 launches and load balancer health checks [S49].

Compare the two outages. In 2021 the DNS servers were healthy but unreachable, because the routes to them were gone. In 2025 the DNS servers were reachable but gave an empty answer. Both ended with the same result for the user: a name that could not be turned into an address, so the service could not be found. Chapter 7.5 gives a method for finding which layer is broken.

This chapter gives no minute-level times for either outage.

</GoDeeper>

## DNS TTL counts seconds; IP TTL counts hops

<MermaidDiagram title="Two numbers called TTL" chart={`flowchart TD
  subgraph D["DNS TTL: seconds of cache life"]
    D1["Answer arrives with TTL 3600"] --> D2["Copy kept for up to 3600 s"] --> D3["Copy dropped, ask again"]
  end
  subgraph I["IP TTL: router hops"]
    I1["Linux sender starts at 64"] --> I2["Router 1 makes it 63"] --> I3["Router 2 makes it 62"]
  end
`} legend="The same three letters mean two different things. The DNS TTL counts down seconds in a cache. The IP TTL from 4.4 counts routers crossed." />

Two different numbers are called TTL. The DNS TTL counts seconds of cache life. The IP TTL from 4.4 counts router hops: a Linux sender starts at 64, and each router takes one off [S28] [S30]. A packet with ttl 62 crossed two routers. A DNS answer with TTL 300 may be kept for five minutes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: never mix the two">

| | DNS TTL | IP TTL |
|---|---|---|
| Lives in | A DNS answer | The header of every packet |
| Counts | Seconds | Routers crossed |
| Starts at | The number the server chose | 64 on a Linux sender [S28] |
| Goes down | With time, in the cache | By one at each router [S30] |
| At zero | The copy is dropped; ask again | The router drops the packet |

You met the second number in 4.4. A capture on the server that shows `ttl 62` means the packet crossed two routers, because 64 minus 62 is 2. The first number you met in this chapter. They share a name and a short form, and nothing else.

A wrong reading is easy to make: "the TTL is 62, so the packet lives 62 more seconds". It does not. The packet lives until it has crossed 62 more routers.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

[S24] RFC 1034: the TTL is a number of seconds that limits caching, and zero means do not cache. [S28] Linux kernel documentation: `ip_default_ttl` is 64. [S30] RFC 1812: a router must decrement the TTL and drop the packet at zero. [S48] Facebook's own account of the outage of 4 October 2021: a maintenance command took down the backbone connections, and its DNS servers withdrew their route announcements. [S49] AWS's summary of the DynamoDB disruption of 19 and 20 October 2025: an empty DNS record for the regional endpoint. The slider and table numbers are arithmetic.

</GoDeeper>
