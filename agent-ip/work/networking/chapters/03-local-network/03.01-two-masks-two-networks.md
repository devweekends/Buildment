## One laptop, two networks, two masks

<MermaidDiagram title="One laptop changes networks" chart={`flowchart TD
 L["One laptop"] --> C["Campus: 10.20.143.7 | 255.255.240.0"]
 L --> H["Home: 192.168.1.23 | 255.255.255.0"]`} legend="The address and mask change together when the laptop joins a different network." />

The campus address comes with mask `255.255.240.0`; the home address comes with `255.255.255.0`. A netmask marks the network part of an address, which helps the laptop decide whether to send directly. The same laptop can use either mask because these settings describe its connection, rather than its identity. [S7]

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: one laptop, two networks, two masks">

The numbers in this picture are a fictional teaching example. Record both numbers as a pair. An address on its own does not tell you where its network ends. The mask supplies that missing information, as the next chapter shows.

A prefix length writes the same boundary as a slash followed by a count. The count is the number of 1 bits in the mask. The home mask has 24 of those bits, so it is written /24. The campus mask has 20, so it is /20. You met prefix lengths in module 2. Here you are finding the value that your own connection uses.

The mask is not a second device address. Do not paste it into the address field of your notes. Keep separate labels for address and mask. You do not need to change either setting for this chapter. [S7]

## Three written forms show the same boundary

<MermaidDiagram title="Reading the mask field" chart={`flowchart TD
 W["Windows: dotted mask"] --> D["255.255.255.0"]
 L["Linux: slash prefix"] --> P["/24"]
 M["Hex mask notation"] --> X["0xffffff00"]
 X --> B["ff . ff . ff . 00"]
 B --> D
 D --> P
 F["f0 = 240; ff = 255; 00 = 0"]`} legend="A notation guide, not a screenshot or a claimed recording from three operating systems." />

Find the mask beside your current IPv4 address and copy it into your notes. Linux shows a slash prefix; Windows shows a dotted mask, while a hex mask can be decoded in pairs. Read `ff` as 255, `f0` as 240, and `00` as 0. Thus `0xffffff00` means `255.255.255.0`, and `0xfffff000` means `255.255.240.0`.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three written forms show the same boundary">

Choose the command for your own computer. These are commands to run, not recorded terminal output.

Windows:

```text
ipconfig
```

Linux:

```text
ip addr
```

macOS:

```text
ifconfig
```

Look for the connection you are using. Copy the IPv4 address and its mask or slash prefix. Keep them in your own notes; you do not need to share them. If you cannot use a computer now, record the two fictional pairs from the first picture and return to your own settings later.

For hex, remove the initial 0x and split the remaining eight digits into four pairs. Each pair stands for one byte. In f0, the four high bits are on and the four low bits are off: 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 = 240. In ff all eight are on, giving 255. This makes fffff000 a /20 and ffffff00 a /24.

The course's numbers checker converts both hex masks correctly. We have not yet checked how a Mac prints its mask, so the picture is a notation guide, not a Mac screenshot. Use the values your computer actually shows.

Source: [S7] RFC 950, the address mask.
