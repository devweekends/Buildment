A school has been given one block of addresses, `10.30.0.0/22`, and it asks you to plan the network. The block has 1,024 addresses, from `10.30.0.0` to `10.30.3.255`. Nothing else in the school uses these addresses yet, so you are free to choose. But every choice you make now is hard to change later. A network that is renumbered in the middle of a school year means visiting every device. So the plan must be right the first time, and it must still work next year.

This lab uses everything from the module. You will use the masks and boundaries from 3.2 and 3.5, the reasons to split from 3.4, and the overlap check from 3.6. The network lab where you drag blocks into the `/22` is not built yet. This chapter is the same lab as a paper plan, and it is graded by three questions on the plan that every learner shares. An interactive version comes later.

## The school and its rules

The school has five groups of devices. Guest Wi-Fi has 250 devices, mostly phones. Staff has 90 devices. There are three computer labs, each with 40 devices. The printers number 10. These are the numbers you plan for. Every device needs its own address, and the groups must be separate networks, for the reasons you saw in 3.4.

| Group | Devices today |
|---|---|
| Guest Wi-Fi | 250 |
| Staff | 90 |
| Computer lab (three of them) | 40 each |
| Printers | 10 |

Four rules decide whether a plan is good. The lab checks them all, and so should you. First, every group must fit in its block, counting only usable addresses, as you did in 3.5. Second, every block must hold at least 20% more than its need. This is the room to grow. Third, every block must start on its own boundary, so its start has all host bits zero. Fourth, no two blocks may share an address, and every block must lie inside `10.30.0.0/22`.

## Why a margin, and why a number

The words "room to grow" are not a plan. A plan needs a number, so that two people reading it agree. This lab uses 20%. It is a choice for this school, not a law. A school that adds a few devices every year may choose more, and a fixed installation such as a printer room may choose less. What matters is that the margin is written down, so that someone can check it and change it.

Look at what the margin does to a size. Without it, 250 guest devices fit in a `/24`, which has 254 usable addresses. With a 20% margin the guests become 300, and a `/24` is too small. The next size up is a `/23`. A margin can push a group into a bigger block. That is the point: it is cheaper to find out now, on paper.

## A method you can repeat

The picture shows the order of work. It is a method, not the answer. Every step uses an idea you already know.

Here is a worked example for a different case, so you can see each step. A clinic has the block `10.50.0.0/23`, which is 512 addresses. It has 110 guest devices, 45 staff devices and 20 instruments. The margin is 20%, so the needs become 132, 54 and 24. A `/25` has only 126 usable addresses, so 132 needs a `/24`. For 54, a `/27` has 30 usable and is too small, but a `/26` has 62. For 24, a `/28` has 14 and is too small, but a `/27` has 30.

Now sort and place. The `/24` goes first, at `10.50.0.0`, and takes `10.50.0.0` to `10.50.0.255`. The next free address is `10.50.1.0`, which is a multiple of 64, so the `/26` starts there and takes `10.50.1.0` to `10.50.1.63`. The next free address is `10.50.1.64`, which is a multiple of 32, so the `/27` starts there and takes `10.50.1.64` to `10.50.1.95`. Check: the blocks use 256 + 64 + 32 = 352 addresses of 512, nothing overlaps, and every start is a multiple of its size. The free space is `10.50.1.96` to `10.50.1.255`, which is 160 addresses in one piece.

<Callout type="info" title="Usable is not total">
Every ordinary block loses two addresses, the first and the last. A `/26` has 64 addresses and holds 62 devices. Compare your margin with the usable number, never with the total.
</Callout>

If you ran the 3.8 `splitBlock` on the clinic's three needs with the margin already added, 132, 54 and 24, it would return the same three blocks. The function does the sort, size, align and place steps. The margin and the checks are still yours.

## Write your plan

Now do the same for the school. Open a text file, or use the learning log below, and write a small table. It has one row per network, with the prefix, the first address and the last address. Use the needs from the table above, and add the 20% margin before you choose any prefix. Work through the groups from the biggest to the smallest.

Do not use a calculator. Use the steps from 3.5: find the smallest block, place the biggest first, and move to the next multiple of the block size. When your table is ready, check it against the four rules. Take each rule in turn and test every row, not just the ones that look doubtful. Most mistakes sit in the row you felt sure about.

Three mistakes are common in this lab. The first is to add the margin to the total and not to each group, which hides the group that tips into a bigger block. The second is to place the printers first because they are small. A small block placed first takes the start of the space and forces the big blocks to jump to their next boundary. The third is to read a `/23` as one third number. It covers two, so the block after it starts two numbers further on.

After the table, write three short sentences for each size. The first sentence says the need and the margin. The second says the prefix and how many usable addresses it has. The third says what a smaller block would have missed, or what the larger block would have wasted. You have four sizes: the guest network, the staff network, a computer lab and the printers. The three labs share one size, so they share one explanation. The sentences are not decoration. In 3.12 you will use them to defend your plan, and a reason you cannot write in three sentences is a reason you do not understand yet.

## Three graded questions on the fixed plan

Everyone who follows the rules above arrives at the same four sizes, because each size is the smallest block that holds the need plus 20%. That is why the questions below can be graded, even though you wrote your own plan. If your table disagrees with an answer, find the row where it differs and the rule it breaks.

The first question is about sizes. It uses the biggest group, where the margin changes the answer.

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "The guest network has 250 devices today and the school wants 20% room to grow. Which block is the smallest that fits?"
options:
  - "A /22, with 1,022 usable"
  - "A /24, with 254 usable"
  - "A /23, with 510 usable"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "That fits, but it is the whole block the school was given. It is more than three times the 300 the guests will need, and nothing else would have a network."
  - "That fits 250 today, but 250 plus 20% is 300. A /24 has only 254 usable addresses, so there is no room to grow."
  - "Right. 250 plus 20% is 300. A /23 has 512 addresses and 510 usable, which holds 300. A /24 is too small."
```

The second question is about boundaries. Suppose the guest network is placed first, as `10.30.0.0/23`. A `/23` covers two third numbers, as you saw in 3.2.

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "The guest network is 10.30.0.0/23. The staff network is a /25 and goes next, with no gap. Where does it start?"
options:
  - "10.30.2.0"
  - "10.30.1.128"
  - "10.30.0.251"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. 10.30.0.0/23 runs from 10.30.0.0 to 10.30.1.255. The next free address is 10.30.2.0, and it is a valid start for a /25 because the last number is 0."
  - "That address is a valid start for a /25, but it is still inside the guest block. A /23 reaches to 10.30.1.255, not only to 10.30.1.0."
  - "That counts 250 devices from the start. A block is taken whole. The guest block reaches 10.30.1.255, however many guests there are."
```

The third question is about overlaps. Suppose the staff network is `10.30.2.0/25`, which covers `10.30.2.0` to `10.30.2.127`.

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "The staff network is 10.30.2.0/25. Someone proposes the printers at 10.30.2.64/28. What does the overlap check say?"
options:
  - "No overlap, because the starts differ"
  - "Overlap, because the /28 is inside the /25"
  - "A boundary error, because .64 is off a /28 start"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "Different starts do not mean separate blocks. A block that starts later can still lie inside a bigger one, as with Docker and the office in 3.6."
  - "Right. The /28 covers 10.30.2.64 to 10.30.2.79, and that is inside 10.30.2.0 to 10.30.2.127. Nested blocks overlap."
  - "The start is fine. 64 is 4 times 16, so it is a valid /28 start. The problem is that the block sits inside the staff network."
```

## Check your total

A last check uses only arithmetic, so it does not give the plan away. Add up the sizes of your blocks. A correct plan uses 848 addresses of the 1,024 and leaves 176 free. The free addresses should form one piece at the end of the `/22`, not many small gaps. If your total is more, one block is too big for its need. If your total is less, a block is too small to hold its margin.

Keep your table and your sentences. Your plan is the evidence for the gate in 3.12, where a fourth lab arrives and you have to show where it goes.
