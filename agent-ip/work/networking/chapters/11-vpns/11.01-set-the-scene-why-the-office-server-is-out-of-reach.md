## The internet has no route to 10.20.0.5

<MermaidDiagram title="A private address in another building" chart={`flowchart LR

The internet carries only public addresses, so no router has a row for `10.20.0.0/24`. The internet router, `198.51.100.1`, says so and drops your ping. The office router would not help either: module 6 showed that a router lets nothing in without a NAT row or a forwarding rule. This is the recorded ping from the lab you build in 11.4:

```
PING 10.20.0.5 (10.20.0.5) 56(84) bytes of data.
From 198.51.100.1 icmp_seq=1 Destination Net Unreachable

--- 10.20.0.5 ping statistics ---
1 packets transmitted, 0 received, +1 errors, 100% packet loss, time 0ms
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two walls, not one">

Two separate things stop you, and module 6 showed both.

The first wall is routing. `10.20.0.5` is in `10.0.0.0/8`, one of the three private blocks [S8]. Private addresses are not globally reachable, so the public internet does not carry them [S9]. Thousands of offices may use `10.20.0.5` at the same time. A router on the internet could not know which one you mean, so it has no row at all. In the lab, the internet router says `Destination Net Unreachable`. A real router may also drop the packet in silence.

The second wall is NAT. Suppose you typed the office's public address, `203.0.113.2`, instead. Your packet would arrive at the office router. But the router only passes a packet inside when a device inside sent first and made a row, or when someone wrote a forwarding rule [S17, S18]. Nobody in the office has talked to your laptop, so there is no row.

A VPN gets through both walls with one trick. It wraps your private packet inside a normal packet between two public addresses, which the internet can carry. The office opens one door for that outer packet: a forwarding rule for one UDP port. Chapter 11.2 shows the wrapping, and 11.4 builds the door.

</GoDeeper>

## Two networks, one range: the VPN breaks

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m3-scene-late-overlap" />

You met this in 3.6. Home and office both use `192.168.1.0/24`, and each works alone. Join them with a VPN, and the laptop cannot tell which `192.168.1.5` you mean. In the usual set-up, it picks its own network: the route for its own Wi-Fi. So before you build a tunnel, look at the ranges on your own computer.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: list the adapters on your own computer">

Do this **on your own computer**, not in the lab. Each network adapter has its own address and its own range.

- On Windows, open a terminal and run `ipconfig`.
- On Linux, run `ip addr`.
- On macOS, run `ifconfig` in Terminal. `ip addr` works too if you installed it.

You will see your Wi-Fi or cable adapter, with an address from your home router. You may see more. WSL on Windows sits behind its own NAT by default: the WSL machine has its own address, and Windows is its gateway [S35]. Docker creates networks from a list of default pools that starts at `172.17.0.0/16` and covers `172.17` to `172.31` and `192.168.0.0/16` [S46]. A company VPN app, if you have one, adds an adapter of its own. Write down each adapter's address and prefix, for example `192.168.1.23/24`.

Now compare the ranges, as in 3.6. Two ranges overlap when they share at least one address. If your home Wi-Fi is `192.168.1.0/24` and your office network is also `192.168.1.0/24`, a VPN between them cannot work cleanly. In the usual set-up, the laptop treats `192.168.1.5` as a neighbour on its own Wi-Fi, so the packet never enters the tunnel. The exact result depends on the routes the VPN adds, which is one more reason to avoid the overlap. The fix is a plan, not a trick: one side must move to another range.

The lab in this module avoids the trap on purpose. The office is `10.20.0.0/24`, the café is `192.168.50.0/24`, and the tunnel itself is `10.99.0.0/24`. The three ranges share no address.

Your adapters and their addresses are personal. Keep them in your own notes and do not post them.

</GoDeeper>
