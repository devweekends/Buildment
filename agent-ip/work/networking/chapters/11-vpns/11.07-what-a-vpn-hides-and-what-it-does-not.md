A VPN app promises that you are "anonymous" and "safe". In 11.6 you ran a VPN yourself and captured it from three places. So you do not need to trust the promise. You can answer from your own files: who could still see what you did?

## The café sees one address, sizes and times

<MermaidDiagram title="What the café's network sees in a full tunnel" chart={`flowchart LR
  L["Your laptop"] -->|"UDP 51820, locked"| C["Café network and its provider"]
  C -->|"sees: 203.0.113.2, port 51820,<br>sizes, times"| I["Internet"]
  C -.->|"cannot see: the website,<br>the page, the inner addresses"| X["Hidden"]
`} legend="Left to right: in a full tunnel, everything leaves the laptop as locked UDP. The café and its provider see the outer packets. What is inside stays hidden from them." />

In your full-tunnel capture, the café's outside plug carried only UDP between `198.51.100.2` and `203.0.113.2`. The café knows you talk to one address, how much, and when. Once your name questions also go through the tunnel, as after Gap 4, it does not know which website you visited, or what was on the page. This is the part a VPN really hides: your traffic, from the network you sit on.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the outside view still leaks">

The EFF, a digital rights group, describes what a VPN does in these terms. It sends your traffic through an encrypted tunnel to the VPN server, which hides it from your provider and from the local network, and it makes your location look like the server's [S123]. Your capture showed the first part.

But "hidden" is not "invisible". The outer packets still carry three kinds of facts. The first is the address and port of the other end: anyone on the café network can see that you use `203.0.113.2` on UDP 51820, and can guess it is a WireGuard tunnel. The second is the size of each packet, and the third is the timing. In 11.5 three pings showed as six packets of `length 128`, one pair per second. That shape is visible even though the content is locked.

So a VPN changes what the café can learn from "everything you do" to "that you use a VPN, how much and when". For a person on a shared café Wi-Fi, that is a real gain. It is not the same as nobody knowing anything.

</GoDeeper>

## The far end sees everything you send

<MermaidDiagram title="Where the locked packets are opened" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["Café: sees locked UDP"] --> V["VPN box or VPN company:<br>unlocks every packet"]
  V -->|"sees: every inner packet,<br>every website, every name"| W["The internet"]
`} legend="Left to right: the packets stay locked only as far as the other end of the tunnel. There they are unlocked, and whoever runs that end can read all of them." />

The tunnel ends somewhere, and there the packets are unlocked. In 11.5, the VPN box's `wg0` showed your ping in plain text. In 11.6, the office's name server saw every name you looked up. Whoever runs the far end sees what your café or provider saw before. With a VPN app, that is the company you pay.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: you move your trust, you do not remove it">

The EFF says this plainly: whoever runs the VPN service sees your traffic [S123]. A VPN does not remove the watcher. It replaces the café and your provider with the VPN company. Whether that is better depends on whom you trust more.

The EFF adds three more cautions [S123]. A VPN company can be asked for records by law enforcement. A company's claim that it keeps no logs is not a guarantee, because you cannot check it. And its advice is short: do not use a VPN that you do not trust.

In challenge 8 the far end is your own office, which is the normal case for a company VPN. You trust your employer to run the office network anyway, so the tunnel adds no new watcher. With a VPN app for personal use, the far end is a company you may know nothing about.

</GoDeeper>

## The website sees the exit address

<MermaidDiagram title="Which address the website logs" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["No tunnel: 198.51.100.2, the café"] --> B["Full tunnel: 203.0.113.2, the office"]
  B --> C["Split tunnel: 198.51.100.2, the café again"]
`} legend="Top to bottom, the address the lab website printed in your three runs of 11.6. The website sees whichever network the visit finally leaves from." />

The website sees the address where your traffic leaves the tunnel. In your runs, the café first, the office in the full tunnel, and the café again in the split tunnel. In the full tunnel, the café's address, where you really sit, is hidden from the website. But the site still sees everything else you give it: your login, your cookies, and what you click.

```
the server sees you as 198.51.100.2:34276
the server sees you as 203.0.113.2:50228
the server sees you as 198.51.100.2:60596
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a VPN is not anonymity">

"A VPN is not a tool for anonymity", says the EFF [S123]. The reason is in this beat. A VPN changes one fact the website learns about you, your address. It changes nothing the website learns in other ways. If you log in to an account, the account says who you are, tunnel or not [S123]. A website that tracks you with its own cookies tracks you just as well through a VPN, because the cookies travel inside the tunnel with everything else.

The exit address tells a story of its own. In your full tunnel the website saw `203.0.113.2`, the office. A website that sees a company's address learns which company you came from.

None of this means a VPN is useless. It protects your traffic on a network you do not trust, such as a café's Wi-Fi, and it lets you reach private systems. Those are real jobs. Being anonymous is not one of them.

</GoDeeper>

Step through the four set-ups from your `results.md` in 11.6. Before you open each step, say what the café sees, using your own table. Each moves what one network can see to another network. No set-up makes everything invisible to everyone. A full tunnel with the office's name server hides the most from the café, and gives the most to the office.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the name questions and the split tunnel">

Two of the set-ups deserve a second look. The first is the full tunnel with the café's name server. The web traffic was hidden, but `dns-cafe.txt` showed `A? web.test.` crossing the café's network in plain text. The name server was on the laptop's own café cable, so its route was more specific than the default route into `wg0`. A name says a lot about you: a list of the names you looked up is close to a list of the sites you visited. A VPN app can set its own name server when it connects, as `wg-quick` does from its `DNS=` line [S118]. When it does not, you get what you captured.

The second is the split tunnel. It is a choice, not a fault. The company decides that only its own networks go through the tunnel, and the rest of your traffic goes out the normal way. One VPN service describes the same two modes: by default all client traffic goes through the tunnel, and with split tunnel only the networks that the server sends to the client do [S125]. A split tunnel is lighter for the office, but it means the café sees your other traffic as if there were no VPN at all.

</GoDeeper>

## A leak: the tunnel covers only IPv4

<MermaidDiagram title="Two roads out of one laptop" chart={`flowchart LR
  L["Laptop with IPv4 and IPv6"] -->|"IPv4: into wg0"| T["Tunnel to the VPN server"]
  L -->|"IPv6: no tunnel"| C["Café network, in the clear"]
  T --> W["Website"]
  C --> W
`} legend="Left to right: a VPN that handles only IPv4 sends IPv4 into the tunnel. IPv6 has no tunnel, so it takes the café's network straight to the website." />

A tunnel covers only the addresses it is told about. `0.0.0.0/0` is every IPv4 address, and `::/0` would be every IPv6 address. A VPN that handles only IPv4 leaves IPv6 traffic on the local network, in the clear. An RFC from 2014, one of the internet's public documents, describes exactly this leak. The lab has no IPv6, so here you only read about it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: RFC 7359, and how an attacker can force it">

RFC 7359, from August 2014, is about this problem in networks that have both IPv4 and IPv6 [S122]. A VPN that only handles IPv4 can leave the IPv6 traffic going out in the clear on the local network, outside the tunnel.

It gets worse. The RFC also describes an attacker on the local network who forces the IPv6 path on purpose, with forged router advertisements [S122]. Those are the messages from module 9 that tell a device "here is an IPv6 network, and here is your router". The attacker can also advertise a name server of its own [S122]. Now the laptop's IPv6 traffic, and maybe its name questions, go to the attacker, while the VPN icon still says "connected".

In WireGuard terms, AllowedIPs must cover IPv6 as well, with `::/0` for every IPv6 address [S117], and the routes must send IPv6 into `wg0` too. You can check your own VPN app: with the VPN on, look at your adapters as in 11.1, and see whether an IPv6 address remains outside the tunnel.

</GoDeeper>

## A company VPN is another product

<MermaidDiagram title="Same word, two products" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Company VPN"] --> A1["Job: reach private systems<br>such as 10.20.0.5"]
  A --> A2["Both ends run by the company"]
  B["VPN app you pay for"] --> B1["Job: hide your traffic<br>from the local network"]
  B --> B2["Far end run by the VPN company"]
`} legend="Two products called VPN. Top, a company VPN joins you to private systems, and the company runs both ends. Bottom, a VPN app hides your traffic from the network you sit on, and a company you pay sees it instead." />

Challenge 8 built a company VPN: its job is to reach a private server, and your own office runs the far end. A VPN app you pay for has another job: to hide your traffic from the café, while a company you pay sees it instead. Same technique, different promise. Judge each one by its job.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: site to site, remote access, and other protocols">

Your challenge joined one device, the laptop, to a network. That is called remote access. A VPN can also join two whole networks, so every device in one office reaches every device in another without running anything itself. That is site to site. Amazon's cloud documents both kinds as separate services: a Site-to-Site VPN joins your network to a cloud network over IPsec, with two tunnels for each connection [S124], and a Client VPN joins single devices [S125].

WireGuard is not the only way to build a tunnel. IPsec, described in RFC 4301 from December 2005, protects traffic at the IP layer; its tunnel mode wraps a whole IP packet, as WireGuard does [S121]. Other protocols exist too. The idea underneath is always the one from 11.2: a packet inside a packet, locked, with the far end deciding what happens next.

One more detail from the same documentation is worth knowing: the Site-to-Site service does not support Path MTU Discovery [S124]. That is the size feedback from 11.2. On such a tunnel, keeping the inner packets small enough matters even more.

</GoDeeper>
