Your router's status page says its outside address is `100.72.14.9`, but websites see `203.0.113.45`. Who is the second router? A packet can be rewritten twice, and this chapter shows how, and how to look for it on your own connection.

## The router's outside address is private

<MermaidDiagram title="Two numbers that disagree" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Router status page: outside address 100.72.14.9"] --- Q["Something between them rewrites the source"]
  Q --- W["A website says it sees 203.0.113.45"]
`} legend="Left: the number on your router's own page. Right: the number a website sees. The two differ, so a device between them must rewrite the source address again." />

The router's own page says its outside address is `100.72.14.9`. A website says it sees `203.0.113.45`. The two numbers differ, so something between them rewrites the source again. And `100.72.14.9` is not a public address. It is in `100.64.0.0/10`, a block of shared addresses that providers use [S11]. That is the clue this chapter follows.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the shared block is">

In 2.4 you met four kinds of address: public, private, shared and special. The block `100.64.0.0/10` is the shared kind. The registry marks it as forwardable, but not globally reachable [S9]. RFC 6598 sets it aside for the links between a provider and its customers, and it says shared space has limits that private space does not [S11].

So the number on your router's page is a clue. A home router gets its outside address from the provider's network. If that address is in the shared block, the provider is not giving your router a public address. It is giving it a shared one, and a provider router further along holds the public address. Routers name this address differently on their pages: look for words such as "WAN", "internet" or "outside". Your router may call it something else.

</GoDeeper>

## A second router rewrites it again

<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m6-cgnat-layers" />

The laptop's source is `192.168.1.23`. After the home router it is `100.72.14.9`. After the provider's router it is `203.0.113.45`. Each router that does NAT rewrites the source, so it happens twice. A provider's router that does this for many customers is called <GlossaryTerm term="carrier-grade NAT" definition="NAT run by a provider for many customers, on the link between the provider and their homes." />.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what a provider's NAT must do">

A provider's NAT serves many homes, so its rules are stricter than a home router's. RFC 6888 says it must limit the ports each customer can use, so that one customer cannot use them all [S19]. When it cannot create a new mapping, it must drop that packet. It must not delete existing mappings to make room. A port that is freed should wait 120 seconds before it is used again [S19].

For a customer, this means a busy provider NAT can refuse new conversations while old ones keep working. The standard gives no idle timeout figure for TCP, so we give none [S19]. For you, the main point is simpler. In the picture, the second router has its own table. Packets that arrive from outside meet that table first, and it has no row for them. You will see what that does to port forwarding in 6.9.

</GoDeeper>

## A shared address is a sign, not proof

<MermaidDiagram title="Collect signs, then decide" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Sign: router outside address is in 100.64.0.0/10"] --> D["A provider NAT is likely"]
  B["Sign: a private 10.x hop in your trace"] --> E["A second NAT router is possible, yours or the provider's"]
  D --> F["Use more than one sign before you decide"]
  E --> F
`} legend="Two signs lead to a likely or possible second NAT. Neither is proof on its own, so use more than one." />

An outside address in `100.64.0.0/10` is one sign of provider NAT. It is not proof. A private `10.x` hop in a trace is another sign, but it may be a second router inside your own home. Two signs that agree are stronger than one. Collect signs. Do not decide from only one.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why one sign is not enough">

The shared block is meant for provider links [S11], but the number on your router's page only describes the link between your router and the provider. It does not tell you what else sits between you and the website. A private hop in a trace can come from a second home router, for example one that sits behind another in the same house.

The course's own test host showed this. A Windows trace on that host listed every hop, and it contained two private home routers and then a private `10.x` address inside the provider [D5.5]. The first two private hops were both in the home. Only the third belonged to the provider. A reader who counted all three as "the provider's NAT" would be wrong.

</GoDeeper>

## Look for private hops in your own trace

<MermaidDiagram title="A sample trace, drawn as hops" chart={`flowchart LR
  H1["Hop 1: private address, your router"] --> H2["Hop 2: private address, a second home router"]
  H2 --> H3["Hop 3: a private 10.x address inside the provider"]
  H3 --> H4["Later hops: public addresses"]
`} legend="The shape of a trace recorded on the course's test host: two private home routers, then a private address inside the provider, then public addresses. This is a sample. Your hops and addresses will differ." />

Run a trace on your own computer, not in the lab. Look at the first few hops. Private addresses start with `10.`, or are `172.16` to `172.31`, or start with `192.168.`. Two private hops before a public one can mean two NAT routers. If you cannot open your router's page, or you use a phone as a hotspot, the trace is all you need.

```
# Windows, in PowerShell, on your own computer
tracert -d example.com

# Linux, in a terminal, on your own computer
traceroute -n example.com

# macOS, in Terminal (not yet checked on a Mac for this course)
traceroute -n example.com
```

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a trace, and why not in the lab">

Each line of a trace is one hop, a router that your packet crossed. On Linux, `traceroute` sends probes with a TTL that starts at 1 and rises by one each time, up to 30 hops by default [S31]. Each router that lowers the TTL to zero throws the packet away and sends back a message, and that is how it shows itself [S30]. A line of `* * *` means no answer came. Some routers do not answer, and that alone proves nothing. The options `-d` on Windows and `-n` on Linux keep the numbers and skip the name lookups.

The lab container hides the real path. In the course's test, a trace from inside the lab showed the Docker network as hop 1 and then nothing useful [D5.5]. The reason is that Docker Desktop's own networking sits between the lab and your home. The same test from the Windows host showed every hop. So the commands about your real internet path run on your computer, never in the lab. Write what you find in your learning log. Your trace is personal, so do not post it.

</GoDeeper>

## Providers ran out of addresses too

<MermaidDiagram title="Why a provider shares addresses" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["The free pool of public addresses is empty (2.1)"] --> B["A provider cannot give every customer a public address"]
  B --> C["Many customers share one public address through the provider's NAT"]
`} legend="Left to right: the shortage from 2.1 means a provider has fewer public addresses than customers. So it shares each public address between many customers, using NAT." />

In 2.1 you saw that the free pool of addresses ran out. A provider needs addresses for its customers too, so it shares one public address between many of them. A study with data from 2014 to 2016 found carrier-grade NAT in over 92 percent of cellular networks [S34]. We have no sourced figure for today.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the study measured, and what it did not">

The study counted networks, not people, and its data is from 2014 to 2016. It found that in cellular networks the use of carrier-grade NAT was over 92 percent in all cases. For networks that were not cellular, it found 13.3 percent of the networks it checked [S34]. The networks it counts are autonomous systems, an autonomous system being a connected group of address blocks under one or more operators with a single, clearly defined routing policy [S68].

Do not copy these numbers as today's. A current measurement was not found, so any claim about the share in 2026 is unverified. The lesson that lasts is the reason: a provider with fewer public addresses than customers has to share. That is why a phone on mobile data can have an address that starts with `100`, as you saw in 2.4.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

[S9] IANA special-purpose registry: `100.64.0.0/10` is forwardable and not globally reachable. [S11] RFC 6598: `100.64.0.0/10` for provider-side shared address space. [S19] RFC 6888: a provider NAT must limit ports per subscriber, must drop a new flow rather than delete old mappings, and should wait 120 seconds before reusing a freed port; it gives no TCP idle timeout figure. [S30] RFC 1812: a router that lowers the TTL to zero discards the packet and sends a Time Exceeded message. [S31] The Linux traceroute manual: probes start at TTL 1, the default maximum is 30 hops. [S34] A study with data from 2014 to 2016, published in 2016: carrier-grade NAT in over 92 percent of cellular networks and 13.3 percent of the non-cellular networks it checked. [S68] RFC 1930: the definition of an autonomous system. [D5.5] The course's own test: a Windows trace on the host showed two private home routers and a private `10.x` address inside the provider, and a trace from inside the lab hid the real path.

</GoDeeper>
