# Chapter 2.2: An address is one 32-bit number

G3 sample chapter. Learn, 35 minutes, `format: beats` (agent-ip v0.5.3). Draft to `agent-ip/work/networking/chapters/02-ip-addresses/02.02-an-address-is-one-32-bit-number.md`.

## Outcome
Convert an IPv4 address between dotted form, binary and one number, by hand, and explain why no part goes above 255.

## Prose target
Beats: four beats, 300 to 700 visible words, at least 900 words with the `GoDeeper` folds. Each beat: a heading of eight words or fewer that states what the picture shows, the visual first, at most 70 visible words that say what the learner just saw. Depth (mechanism, numbers, near misses, sources) goes in folds.

## Hook (one or two sentences before the first beat)
In 2.1 the learner saw that IPv4 addresses ran out. Now: your laptop's address from 1.3 is something like `192.168.1.23`, and no phone, laptop or router will accept `192.168.1.256`. Why does each part stop at 255?

## Beats

1. **"One part is one byte"** (key beat, playable): `<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m2-byte-bits" />`. Right before it, a `predict`: "Each of eight lamps is worth 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2 or 1. Turn all eight on. What number do you get?" Options 255, 256, 128 (answerable by adding the numbers in the question; never ask before you teach). Text after: eight bits, place values, all on is 255, adding one needs a ninth bit that is not there, so 256 cannot be one part.
2. **"Four bytes make one address"** (playable): `<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m2-address-bits" />`. Text: the four parts are four bytes, 32 bits in all; flip the last bit and `192.168.1.23` becomes `192.168.1.22`; type your own address from 1.3.
3. **"Three ways to write one address"**: a `MermaidDiagram` (or a ```mermaid fence) with three boxes for `192.168.1.23`: dotted `192.168.1.23`, bits `11000000.10101000.00000001.00010111`, one number `3,232,235,799`, all pointing at "the same 32 bits". Text: dots are for people; computers store the number; code and databases often keep addresses as one number.
4. **"Now do it by hand"** (playable practice): `<InteractiveCanvas slug="net-m2-address-drill" />`. Text: the hand method, write 128 64 32 16 8 4 2 1, take the biggest that fits, subtract, repeat; five questions, no calculator.

## Worked example (in the beat 3 or 4 fold)
`192.168.1.23`: 192 = 128 + 64 = `11000000`; 168 = 128 + 32 + 8 = `10101000`; 1 = `00000001`; 23 = 16 + 4 + 2 + 1 = `00010111`. As one number: 192 x 16,777,216 + 168 x 65,536 + 1 x 256 + 23 = 3,232,235,799 (checked by script). `10.0.0.1` is 10 x 16,777,216 + 1 = 167,772,161.

## Folds (`GoDeeper`), at least these
- Why 32 bits: RFC 791 (1981) fixed addresses at four octets, 32 bits [S1], so there are 2^32 = 4,294,967,296 possible addresses. That is the 4.3 billion from 2.1.
- The hand method with a second example, and the reverse (number to dotted: divide by 16,777,216, then 65,536, then 256).
- Near misses: reading `192.168.1.23` as the decimal 192168123 (the dots are not digits); a part above 255; leading zeros: this course writes `1`, not `001` (do not claim what other tools do with leading zeros).
- Where the one-number form shows up: code exercise 2.5 asks you to write `ipToNumber` and `numberToIp`, and warns about a JavaScript trap with the top bit (details there, not here).
- Sources: [S1] RFC 791.
